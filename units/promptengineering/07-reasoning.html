Getting the model to reason

Asking a model to work through a problem before answering measurably improves hard tasks and measurably wastes money on easy ones. The techniques differ mainly in how many extra tokens they buy and what they buy them for.

chain of thought step-back self-consistency tree of thoughts

Why thinking out loud works

Two mechanisms, both consequences of chapter 1.

More computation per answer
The model does a fixed amount of work per token. A question answered in one token gets one token's worth of computation; the same question answered after 200 tokens of working gets 200 times more. Reasoning tokens are compute you are buying.
Intermediate results enter the context
Each token attends to everything before it. Writing "the sale window is 14 days" puts that fact in the context where the final answer can attend to it — rather than requiring it to be recomputed implicitly inside a single forward pass.

Both explain the observed pattern: reasoning helps most on problems with multiple dependent steps, and helps least on lookup, classification and formatting, where there is nothing to decompose.

Reasoning models have not made this obsolete

Models trained to reason before answering do this internally, and for those you should generally stop adding chain-of-thought instructions — you get worse results and pay twice. But the technique still matters for three reasons: most production traffic still runs on standard models because they are cheaper and faster; you often want the reasoning visible for auditability; and the decision of when reasoning is worth its cost is the same decision either way. Chapter 14 covers adapting prompts for reasoning models specifically.

Chain of thought

three strengths of the same idea
# 1. Zero-shot CoT — the classic one-liner. Cheap, and often enough.
Think step by step before answering.

# 2. Structured — separates working from answer, so you can strip one.
Work through this in  tags, then give the final answer in  tags.
Nothing outside  will be shown to the user.

# 3. Guided — name the steps. Strongest, because it specifies the decomposition
#    rather than hoping the model finds a good one.
Work through this in :
1. What did the customer buy, and when?
2. Was it a sale item, and is it marked final sale?
3. Is it faulty, or a change of mind?
4. Which policy section applies, given 2 and 3?
5. What is the return window, and who pays postage?
Then give the answer in , quoting the window and postage rule exactly.

Guided decomposition is underrated. "Think step by step" leaves the model to invent the steps, and on a domain task it often invents the wrong ones. Naming the five questions that actually determine the answer is more reliable, more auditable, and makes the reasoning comparable across runs — which matters for evaluation.

extracting the answer, safely
import re

ANSWER = re.compile(r"(.*?)", re.S)

def extract(text: str) -> str:
    m = ANSWER.search(text)
    if not m:
        # No tags: the model ignored the format, or was truncated mid-scratch.
        # Do NOT fall back to returning the whole string — that shows the user
        # the working, which often contains discarded wrong turns.
        raise MalformedOutput("no  block")
    return m.group(1).strip()

# Log the scratch content. Never render it. It routinely contains sentences like
# "Actually that's wrong, the window is 14 not 30" — useful for debugging,
# damaging for user trust.

Step-back prompting

Before answering the specific question, ask the model to state the general principle that governs it. Cheap — one extra paragraph — and disproportionately effective on questions where the specifics invite pattern-matching to the wrong rule.

step-back, in one prompt
First, state the general policy that governs this situation, in one sentence,
without reference to the specific case. Then apply it to the case below.

Case: A customer bought a discounted pan on 20 February. The handle snapped on
1 March. They want a refund and are asking who pays return postage.

# The step-back forces "faults are governed by the faulty-items rule regardless
# of whether the item was discounted" to be stated BEFORE the specifics are
# processed. Without it, "discounted" and "20 February" pull toward the sale
# window, and the model reasons its way into the wrong section of the policy.

Self-consistency

Sample the same prompt several times at non-zero temperature, then take the majority answer. It works because errors from sampling tend to be scattered while correct answers agree — so a vote filters noise that a single sample cannot.

self_consistency.py — vote, and use the margin
from collections import Counter

async def self_consistent(prompt: str, n: int = 5, temp: float = 0.7):
    outs = await asyncio.gather(*[call(prompt, temperature=temp) for _ in range(n)])
    answers = [normalise(extract(o)) for o in outs]     # normalise before counting

    counts = Counter(answers)
    winner, votes = counts.most_common(1)[0]

    # The margin is a genuine confidence signal, and it is free.
    return {
        "answer": winner,
        "agreement": votes / n,
        "dissent": [a for a in answers if a != winner],
        "escalate": votes / n < 0.6,        # 3-2 is not a decision
    }

# Requirements, all of them:
#   - the answer must be comparable. Normalise, or vote on an extracted field
#     rather than on free prose, where every sample differs trivially.
#   - temperature must be > 0. At 0 you get five identical samples and pay 5x
#     for no information. This is a real and common misconfiguration.
#   - use the margin. A 5-0 result and a 3-2 result are different situations;
#     treating them the same throws away the most useful thing the method gives you.
Self-consistency multiplies cost linearly and helps sub-linearly

Five samples cost five times one. The accuracy gain is real on multi-step reasoning but flattens quickly — most of the benefit is in the first three samples, and going from five to nine rarely justifies itself. It also cannot help where the model is consistently wrong: if it misreads the policy the same way every time, five votes for the wrong answer look exactly like confidence. Reserve it for high-value decisions where you will use the agreement margin, not as a general accuracy setting.

Tree of thoughts

Generate several candidate approaches, evaluate them, expand the promising ones, backtrack from dead ends. Powerful for genuine search problems, and heavy — expect 5–10× the cost.

RequirementWhy it is non-negotiable
A real evaluatorWithout something objective to score branches — tests passing, a constraint satisfied, a numeric objective — "evaluate the branches" is another model call with no ground truth, and the search wanders expensively
A large branching factor that mattersIf there are two plausible approaches, just try one. Search pays when there are many and most are wrong
Steps you can abandonExploration only makes sense if a branch costs nothing to discard
Budget for the multiplierDecide whether the problem is worth 5–10× before building it, not after

In practice, most application teams do not need tree search. If you find yourself reaching for it, check first whether the problem is really "the model needs to explore" or actually "the model needs a tool" — the latter is far more common and far cheaper (chapter 7 of the AI Agents unit).

Choosing

Is the task a lookup, classification or reformat?Yes → answer directly. Reasoning is cost with no return
↓

↓

↓

↓
Many candidates and a real verifier?Yes → tree search, budgeting 5–10×
↓

Where reasoning misleads

FailureWhat is happening
Plausible working, wrong answerThe reasoning was generated to be coherent, not to be correct. A confident chain of thought is not evidence
Post-hoc rationalisationThe model settled on an answer early and the "working" justifies it. Guided decomposition helps because it fixes the order of questions
Reasoning leaks into outputUsers see "wait, that's wrong" mid-answer. Use tags and strip them
Truncation mid-scratchToken limit hit before the answer block. Always check finish_reason (chapter 2)
Latency doubles unnoticedReasoning tokens are generated at normal speed. 300 extra tokens is several seconds

Do not evaluate reasoning by reading it

A chain of thought that reads well is persuasive and proves nothing — models produce fluent justifications for wrong answers as readily as for right ones. Evaluate the answers against ground truth (chapter 12), and use the reasoning only for diagnosis once you already know a case failed. Teams that grade the working rather than the outcome consistently overestimate how well their reasoning prompt is doing.

The short version

  1. Reasoning buys more computation per answer and puts intermediate results in the context. Both explain where it helps.
  2. It helps on multi-step dependent problems and wastes money on lookup, classification and formatting.
  3. Guided decomposition beats "think step by step" — name the steps rather than hoping the model finds good ones.
  4. Separate working from answer with tags, log the working and never render it.
  5. Step-back is one extra paragraph and disproportionately effective when specifics invite the wrong rule.
  6. Self-consistency needs temperature above 0 and comparable answers, and its real product is the agreement margin.
  7. Tree search needs a real evaluator and reversible steps, costs 5–10×, and is usually a tool problem in disguise.
  8. Never grade the reasoning. Grade the answers; read the reasoning only to diagnose.
