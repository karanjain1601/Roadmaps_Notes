Reliability tactics

A prompt at 88% is often the best a prompt can do. The tactics here spend extra calls to buy some of the remaining twelve points — and the useful thing most of them produce is not a better answer but a signal about which answers to distrust.

ensembling self-evaluation calibration debiasing

The move that actually matters

Before any of the techniques below: the most valuable thing you can add to an unreliable prompt is a route for the uncertain cases. A system that answers 85% of inputs well and escalates the other 15% is more useful than one that answers 92% and cannot tell you which. The tactics in this chapter are mostly ways of producing that division.

AnswerConfident cases, handled automatically
›
FlagUncertain cases, marked for review
›
EscalateCases the system declines to handle
Accuracy without a confidence signal is half a system

Two systems at 88%: one returns an answer every time, the other returns an answer plus "I am unsure about this one" on 14% of inputs and is right on 97% of the rest. The second is dramatically more useful, because the failures are now findable. Every tactic below should be judged on whether it improves the answer or improves your ability to spot a bad one — both are wins, and the second is often cheaper.

Self-consistency, revisited

Chapter 7 covered the mechanism. The point worth restating here: its output is not really the majority answer, it is the agreement margin.

using the margin rather than discarding it
result = self_consistent(prompt, n=5, temperature=0.7)

if result.agreement >= 0.8:          # 4-1 or 5-0
    auto_apply(result.answer)
elif result.agreement >= 0.6:        # 3-2
    apply_with_flag(result.answer, note=f"model split: {result.dissent}")
else:
    escalate(reason="no majority", candidates=result.counts)

# Most implementations return only .answer and throw the margin away, which is
# paying 5x for one number when you could have had two. The distribution of
# agreement across your traffic is also a free quality metric: if 40% of inputs
# produce a 3-2 split, the prompt is ambiguous, not the inputs.

Ensembling across prompts

Self-consistency varies the sampling. Ensembling varies the prompt — different phrasings, orderings, or framings of the same task — and takes the consensus. It catches a different class of error: cases where the model is consistently wrong under one framing but not another.

ensemble.py — vary the framing, not the temperature
VARIANTS = [
    # 1. direct
    "Classify this ticket into exactly one category.\n\n{ticket}",
    # 2. evidence-first
    "Quote the phrase that determines the category, then give the category.\n\n{ticket}",
    # 3. elimination
    "For each category, say in three words why it does or does not fit, "
    "then choose.\n\n{ticket}",
]

async def ensemble(ticket: str) -> Verdict:
    outs = await asyncio.gather(*[call(v.format(ticket=ticket), temperature=0)
                                  for v in VARIANTS])
    answers = [extract_category(o) for o in outs]
    counts = Counter(answers)
    top, votes = counts.most_common(1)[0]
    return Verdict(answer=top, agreement=votes / len(VARIANTS),
                   by_variant=dict(zip(range(len(VARIANTS)), answers)))

# Which variant disagrees is diagnostic. If variant 1 is the odd one out on 30%
# of your traffic, the direct framing is under-specified — fix it and drop the
# ensemble. Ensembling is often a temporary measure that tells you what to fix.
What each tactic buys Illustrative accuracy, real cost arithmetic. The last column is the one that usually decides it.

Self-evaluation

Ask the model to check its own answer. It works, but only under conditions that are easy to get wrong.

Does not work

"Review your answer and improve it if needed."

Produces a longer, more hedged answer with the same errors. Models are poor at spotting their own factual mistakes and good at adding qualifications.

Works

"For each criterion below, decide pass or fail and quote the text that fails. If all pass, reply PASS."

Named criteria, an independent decision each, quoted evidence, and a permitted clean pass.

check.py — a verifier, not a vibe
class Check(BaseModel):
    grounded:   bool = Field(description="Every figure and name appears in the source")
    complete:   bool = Field(description="Every part of the question is addressed")
    in_format:  bool = Field(description="Matches the required output shape exactly")
    consistent: bool = Field(description="No internal contradictions")
    failures:   list[str] = Field(description="Specific quoted problems, empty if all pass")

CHECK = """Assess the ANSWER against each criterion independently.
Quote the exact text that fails. Judge only what is present — do not speculate,
and do not reward length or confidence.

{q}
{src}
{a}"""

verdict = structured_call(Check, CHECK.format(...), temperature=0)
if verdict.failures:
    answer = revise(answer, verdict.failures)     # one round only

# Two properties make this a verifier rather than self-congratulation:
#   - the criteria are named, so there is something to check against
#   - failures must be quoted, so the check cannot be vague approval
# A second round of critique adds almost nothing over the first. Do not loop.
A model checking its own work shares its blind spots

If the model misread the policy, it will misread it again during the check and confirm its own answer. Self-evaluation catches format, completeness, internal contradiction and ungrounded claims reliably, because those are checkable against text present in the context. It does not catch reasoning errors, and it does not catch a misunderstanding of the task. For those you need a different framing (the ensemble above), a different model, or a human.

Calibration

Asking a model for a confidence score produces a number. Whether that number means anything is an empirical question, and the answer is usually "not by default" — models cluster their self-reported confidence high and narrow.

calibrate.py — check before you trust the number
# 1. Get a confidence signal. Verbal buckets calibrate better than percentages,
#    which cluster at 90/95/99 regardless of the actual difficulty.
class Answer(BaseModel):
    answer: str
    confidence: Literal["certain", "probable", "unsure", "guessing"]
    what_would_settle_it: str | None = Field(
        description="If not certain, what information would resolve it.")

# 2. Measure whether the buckets separate. This is the whole exercise.
def reliability(labelled: list[tuple[Answer, bool]]) -> dict:
    out = {}
    for bucket in ["certain", "probable", "unsure", "guessing"]:
        rows = [ok for a, ok in labelled if a.confidence == bucket]
        out[bucket] = {"n": len(rows),
                       "accuracy": sum(rows) / len(rows) if rows else None}
    return out

# A useful result looks like:
#   certain   n=142  accuracy=0.97
#   probable  n=88   accuracy=0.84
#   unsure    n=41   accuracy=0.55
#   guessing  n=9    accuracy=0.22
# Monotonic and well separated: you can set an automation threshold at "probable".
#
# A useless result looks like:
#   certain   n=268  accuracy=0.87
#   probable  n=11   accuracy=0.82
#   unsure    n=1    accuracy=1.00
# Everything is "certain". The signal carries no information and the model is
# overconfident. Do not build a threshold on this — fix the prompt first.

Make "unsure" cheap to say

Models are trained toward helpfulness, which makes declining feel like failing. Counteract it explicitly: give an unsure value in the schema, demonstrate it in an example (chapter 5), and say in the prompt that flagging uncertainty is the correct behaviour rather than a fallback — "an escalated case is a good outcome; a confident wrong answer is not". Without that, the confidence field will read "certain" on everything and your threshold will have nothing to work with.

Debiasing

Several systematic biases affect prompted outputs regardless of task, and each has a countermeasure that costs almost nothing.

BiasShows up asCountermeasure
Position biasIn a list of options, first and last are over-chosenRandomise order; for pairwise comparison run both directions and keep only consistent verdicts
Majority-label biasFew-shot class distribution leaks into predictionsBalance the examples, or over-represent rare classes deliberately (chapter 5)
Recency biasThe last example or document dominatesPut the most representative case last on purpose; rotate in evaluation
Verbosity biasLonger answers are rated better by judgesRubric criteria rather than holistic scores; an explicit instruction not to reward length
SycophancyAgrees with a user who pushes back on a correct answerA rule that evidence outranks assertion, and a re-check rather than capitulation
AnchoringA number in the prompt pulls the estimate toward itAsk for the estimate before showing any reference figure
Demographic effectsTone or judgement shifts with names, dialect or locationCounterfactual testing — vary only the attribute and compare
two countermeasures worth having by default
# Position bias in any A/B comparison, including LLM judges.
def compare(a: str, b: str) -> str | None:
    first  = judge(option_1=a, option_2=b)
    second = judge(option_1=b, option_2=a)          # same pair, reversed
    if first == "option_1" and second == "option_2":
        return "a"
    if first == "option_2" and second == "option_1":
        return "b"
    return None            # inconsistent under reversal: genuinely a tie

# Counterfactual bias test, run in CI alongside your evals.
NAMES = {"a": ["James","Emily"], "b": ["Aisha","Kwame"], "c": ["Wei","Priya"]}

def bias_check(template: str, metric) -> dict:
    scores = {g: mean(metric(call(template.format(name=n))) for n in names)
              for g, names in NAMES.items()}
    spread = max(scores.values()) - min(scores.values())
    return {"by_group": scores, "spread": spread, "flag": spread > TOLERANCE}

Choosing among these

Is there a confidence route at all?No → build that first. It is worth more than any accuracy tactic
↓
Are the failures format, completeness or grounding?Yes → self-evaluation with named criteria. Cheap and effective
↓
Are they scattered reasoning slips?Yes → self-consistency at 3, and act on the margin
↓
Is it consistently wrong the same way?Yes → ensembling across framings, then fix the framing that loses
↓
Is it a systematic bias?Yes → the specific countermeasure. Randomise, balance, reverse, counterfactual-test
↓
Still short?You are at the prompt's ceiling. Retrieval, tools, decomposition or a better model
These stack multiplicatively in cost and sub-linearly in benefit

Self-consistency at 5, plus an ensemble of 3, plus self-evaluation is 20+ calls for one answer. Teams arrive there incrementally, each addition defensible, and end up with a system that costs 20× and nobody can say which part earns its place. Add one tactic, measure, keep or remove — and remember that a cheaper alternative to all of them is often a better prompt, a schema, or retrieval.

The short version

  1. The highest-value addition is a route for uncertain cases, not another point of accuracy.
  2. Self-consistency's real product is the agreement margin. Most implementations throw it away.
  3. Ensembling varies the framing and catches consistent errors that resampling cannot — and tells you which framing to fix.
  4. Self-evaluation works with named criteria and quoted failures; "review and improve" produces longer answers, not better ones.
  5. A model checking its own work shares its blind spots. It catches format and grounding, not reasoning errors.
  6. Verbal confidence buckets beat percentages — and measure whether they separate before trusting them.
  7. Make "unsure" cheap to say, in the schema, in an example, and in the prompt.
  8. Add one tactic at a time. Cost stacks multiplicatively; benefit does not.
