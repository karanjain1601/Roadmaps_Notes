Sampling and output control

Chapter 1 established that a prompt shapes a distribution. Sampling parameters decide how you draw from it. They are the most fiddled-with and least understood settings in the stack, and for most production work the right answer is to change almost none of them.

temperature top-p top-k penalties stop

The pipeline

Every parameter acts at a specific point between the model's raw scores and the token that comes out. Knowing the order explains why they interact the way they do.

1 · LogitsRaw scores over the whole vocabulary, from the forward pass
↓
2 · Penalties appliedFrequency and presence penalties subtract from tokens already used
↓
3 · TemperatureDivide the logits. Below 1 sharpens, above 1 flattens
↓
4 · Softmax → probabilitiesNow a proper distribution summing to one
↓
5 · Truncation: top-k, then top-pDiscard the tail, renormalise what remains
↓
6 · Sample one tokenThen append it and repeat from step 1

Temperature

Divides every logit before the softmax. Below 1, differences between logits are magnified and the distribution sharpens. Above 1, they are compressed and it flattens. At exactly 0 (as most APIs implement it) it becomes greedy selection of the single highest-scoring token.

ValueEffectUse for
0Always the top tokenClassification, extraction, structured output, evaluation, anything you want reproducible

> 1.2Unlikely tokens gain real massDeliberate divergence — idea generation, creative constraint-breaking. Expect incoherence
Temperature 0 is not deterministic

It is the most common misconception in this chapter. Greedy decoding removes sampling randomness, but you can still get different outputs from identical inputs: floating-point non-associativity means results depend on how requests were batched on the server, mixture-of-experts routing can vary, and hardware or kernel changes shift ties. Providers usually describe temperature 0 as "nearly deterministic" for exactly this reason. Design for it — your tests should tolerate variation, and your evals should run each case more than once (chapter 12).

Top-p and top-k

Both truncate the tail before sampling; they differ in how they decide where the tail begins.

Top-p (nucleus)

Sort by probability, keep adding tokens until the cumulative mass reaches p, discard the rest.

Adaptive. Where the model is confident, the nucleus is one or two tokens. Where it is uncertain, it widens. This is why it is the better default.

Top-k

Keep the k highest-probability tokens regardless of their mass.

Fixed. Cuts too aggressively when the model is genuinely uncertain, and leaves nonsense in the pool when it is confident. Mostly superseded, but present in local-inference stacks.

Change one, not both

Temperature and top-p both control variety, and adjusting them together makes the effect impossible to reason about. Pick one as your lever — temperature is the more intuitive — and leave the other at its default. Setting temperature to 0 while also setting top-p to 0.5 is a common configuration that means almost nothing: the truncation is irrelevant once the distribution has already collapsed.

Penalties

ParameterMechanismEffect
Frequency penaltySubtracts an amount proportional to how many times the token has already appearedSuppresses repetition progressively. Good against loops
Presence penaltySubtracts a flat amount from any token that has appeared at allPushes toward new vocabulary and new topics
Repetition penaltyA multiplicative variant common in local inferenceSame intent, different arithmetic. Values near 1.1 are typical
Penalties are blunt and they damage structured output

They apply to every token equally, including ones that should repeat: JSON braces, quotes, field names, code keywords, a person's name in a summary about them. A frequency penalty of 0.5 on a task that emits JSON will eventually produce malformed JSON, and it will look like a model problem. Keep penalties at 0 for anything structured. If prose is repetitive, fix the prompt first — repetition is usually a symptom of an under-specified task, not a decoding problem.

Stop sequences and max tokens

the two controls people forget to set
resp = client.chat.completions.create(
    model=MODEL,
    messages=messages,
    temperature=0,
    max_tokens=300,                 # a hard ceiling — protects against runaway cost
    stop=["\n\nUser:", "```", "###"],   # stop as soon as this appears
)

# Two things to know about stop sequences:
#   1. The stop string itself is NOT included in the output. If you stop on "```"
#      you get the code without the closing fence, which breaks naive parsers.
#   2. Stopping is not truncation. finish_reason tells you which happened:
#         "stop"   -> it finished, or hit a stop sequence
#         "length" -> it was cut off mid-thought. Your output is incomplete.

if resp.choices[0].finish_reason == "length":
    # Do not parse this. Do not show it. Either retry with a higher limit or
    # continue from where it stopped.
    handle_truncation(resp)
Always check finish_reason

An answer cut off at the token limit is a truncated string that often still parses as prose and reads as complete until the last sentence. Code that ignores finish_reason will happily store a summary missing its conclusion, or JSON missing its closing brace. It is one if statement and it catches a category of bug that is otherwise very hard to notice.

Set max_tokens to a real ceiling rather than the model's maximum — it is your protection against a runaway generation, and it makes cost predictable. But set it high enough that legitimate answers are not clipped, and treat a rising rate of finish_reason == "length" as a signal that the task or the limit is wrong.

Seeds and reproducibility

best-effort reproducibility, and how to verify it
resp = client.chat.completions.create(
    model="pinned-model-version",   # a floating alias defeats the whole exercise
    messages=messages,
    temperature=0,
    seed=42,                        # where supported: best-effort, not guaranteed
)

# Some providers return a fingerprint identifying the backend configuration.
# If it changes between two calls, identical output was never on the table.
print(resp.system_fingerprint)

# For anything that must be genuinely reproducible — an audit trail, a published
# result, a regression baseline — do not rely on the seed. Store the output.

Notice how much of that column is zero. Most production prompting runs at temperature 0 with everything else default. The variety you want almost always belongs in the prompt, not the sampler.

Variety belongs in the prompt

A frequent instinct: output is repetitive, so raise the temperature. That does produce different text, but it produces it by making every token choice less careful — including the ones about facts, format and instruction adherence. You get variety and a higher error rate together.

two ways to get five different subject lines
# Variety from the sampler: five calls, temperature 1.1.
# Different each time, and each one independently more likely to ignore the
# length limit, drop the product name, or invent a feature.
lines = [call(PROMPT, temperature=1.1) for _ in range(5)]

# Variety from the prompt: one call, temperature 0.
# Reliable, cheaper, and the differences are the ones you asked for.
PROMPT = """Write five subject lines for this email, each under 45 characters.

Make them genuinely different in approach, not rewordings:
1. Lead with the concrete benefit
2. Ask a question the reader wants answered
3. State a specific number or result
4. Create mild urgency without hype
5. Be plain and factual

Email: {body}"""

# The second version is one call instead of five, deterministic, and the
# variation is structured rather than random. It also makes the output
# reviewable: you can tell whether line 3 actually led with a number.

The short version

  1. Parameters act in a fixed order: penalties, temperature, softmax, truncation, sample. That order explains their interactions.
  2. Temperature 0 is not deterministic — batching, routing and hardware still cause variation. Design and test for it.
  3. Top-p adapts to the model's confidence; top-k does not. Prefer top-p, and change only one variety control.
  4. Penalties break structured output. Keep them at 0 for anything with a schema, JSON or code.
  5. Repetition in prose is usually an under-specified prompt, not a decoding problem.
  6. Always check finish_reason. A length-truncated answer often reads as complete.
  7. Seeds are best-effort. For anything that must be reproducible, store the output.
  8. Most production prompting is temperature 0 and everything else default. Put the variety in the prompt.
