Roles, system prompts and context

"Act as an expert" is the most-repeated prompting advice and one of the least examined. It does something real — just not what most people think, and not enough to justify how much prompt real estate it usually occupies.

roles system prompt context persona

What a role actually changes

Conditioning on "you are a senior tax accountant" shifts the distribution toward text that co-occurs with that phrase in the training data. That means vocabulary, register, hedging behaviour, what gets treated as obvious, what caveats appear, and how the answer is structured — all moving together, because they co-occur in real accountancy writing.

What it does not do is add knowledge or capability. The model does not know more tax law because you called it an accountant. It writes more like one.

A role reliably changes

  • Vocabulary and terminology density
  • Register and formality
  • How much is assumed versus explained
  • Which caveats and disclaimers appear
  • Structure — a lawyer enumerates, a teacher builds up
  • Default length

A role does not change

  • What the model knows
  • Whether it can do the reasoning
  • Factual accuracy — often the reverse, since confident register masks error
  • Its willingness to say "I don't know" (usually reduces it)
  • Adherence to your format rules

The audience is the stronger lever

"Explain this to a small-business owner with no finance background" does more useful work than "you are an expert financial analyst", and it does it in the direction you usually want. A role conditions on how an expert writes, which tends toward density and jargon. An audience conditions on how something is explained to that person, which is almost always what you actually asked for. If you only have room for one, take the audience.

When a role earns its place

Use a role whenSkip it when
Register is the point — you want it to sound like a particular professionalThe output is structured data. A JSON emitter has no persona
Domain vocabulary matters and you cannot enumerate the termsYou have already specified audience, format and task
You want a specific critical stance — "review this as a sceptical auditor"You are hoping it will make the model more accurate. It will not
The product has a consistent voiceThe role is longer than the task description
You need one perspective among several (chapter 9's ensembling)You cannot say what would change if you deleted it
The confidence side-effect

Expert personas make models sound more certain, including when they are wrong. Hedges disappear, "I'm not sure" becomes rare, and plausible-but-incorrect claims arrive in the register of authority. If you use an expert role for anything factual, counteract it explicitly: "State your confidence. If a figure is not in the source, say so rather than estimating." Otherwise you have made errors harder to spot, which is the opposite of an improvement.

The system prompt

Chapter 1 established that the system role is a convention rather than a privilege level — the model was trained to weight it heavily, and it mostly does. That gives it two real properties worth designing around.

Assume your system prompt is public

Extraction attempts succeed often enough that you should treat the contents as published. No API keys, no internal URLs, no unreleased product names, no commercially sensitive logic, and nothing that would embarrass you in a screenshot. If your system prompt is a competitive moat, the moat is one persuasive message deep. Put the genuinely confidential logic in code, where the model never sees it.

Supplying context

Reference material is the third leg. Most factual failures are not prompting failures — they are the model having been asked something it was never given the information to answer.

how to present reference material


Items purchased during a sale event may be returned within 14 days rather than 30.
This does not apply to items marked final sale, which cannot be returned unless faulty.


If an item is faulty or arrives damaged we cover return postage in every case,
including sale and final sale items.



Answer using only the sources above. Cite each claim as [S1]. Where sources
conflict, prefer the later date and say that you are doing so. If they do not
answer the question, say: "I couldn't find this in the documentation."
ElementWhy it is there
An id per sourceMakes citation possible, and makes citation checkable in code
A titleGives the model the section context, and gives the user something to click
A dateThe only way it can prefer current material over superseded material
Tags around the blockMarks where instructions end and content begins
The instruction after the sourcesLast position, strongest influence — and it survives long source blocks
An explicit refusal stringWithout it, the model answers from pre-training when the sources fall short, and that answer looks identical to a grounded one
State the completeness assumption

"They are the complete set of material available to you" is a small phrase with a large effect. Without it, the model may reason that other documents probably exist and hedge accordingly, or fill gaps from general knowledge on the assumption you would want it to. Saying the set is complete makes "not in here" mean "not true", which is the behaviour you want — and it makes the refusal path coherent rather than apologetic.

Persona for a product voice

Different problem from a role. Here you are not trying to change capability or register for one task — you are trying to make every output sound like the same product.

a voice specification, written to be checkable
Voice:
- Direct. Lead with the answer, then the reason. Never open with restating the question.
- Plain words over formal ones: "use" not "utilise", "help" not "facilitate".
- Contractions are fine. Exclamation marks are not.
- One idea per sentence. Average under 20 words.
- No hedging stacks: choose "this will" or "this may", never "this may potentially".
- Apologise once, briefly, and only when we got something wrong. Then move to the fix.
- No emoji. No "Great question!". No "I hope this helps!".

If you cannot help, say so in the first sentence and say what can be done instead.

Every line there is a behaviour you could write a test for, which is the difference between a voice specification and a mood board. "Be friendly but professional" is neither implementable nor checkable; "contractions are fine, exclamation marks are not" is both.

reinforcing a rule without touching the prefix
# WRONG — invalidates the cache for the rest of the conversation.
messages[0]["content"] = SYSTEM + "\n\nREMEMBER: cite every claim."

# RIGHT — append at the end, where nothing was cacheable anyway, and where the
# model attends most strongly.
if turn > 6 and turn % 4 == 0:
    messages.append({"role": "user",
                     "content": "(Reminder: cite every factual claim as [S1], "
                                "and use only the sources provided.)"})

# confusing, and they are an implementation detail of the prompt, not content.

The short version

  1. A role shifts register, vocabulary and structure — all together, because they co-occur in the training text. It adds no knowledge.
  2. Audience beats role. "Explain to someone with no finance background" is more useful than "you are an expert".
  3. Expert personas increase apparent confidence, including when wrong. Counteract it explicitly for factual work.
  4. The system prompt is weighted, stable and invisible — so: rules and examples go there, varying content does not.
  5. Assume it is public. No secrets, no keys, no confidential logic.
  6. Reference material needs ids, titles, dates, delimiters, and the instruction placed after it.
  7. Say the sources are complete, or the model hedges and fills gaps from pre-training.
  8. Write a voice as checkable behaviours, and reinforce rules in late turns rather than by editing the prefix.
