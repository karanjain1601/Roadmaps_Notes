Integration and team boundaries

Eight providers and twelve consumers need 96 integration test suites or 20 contract suites. That is not a tooling preference — integration testing is multiplicative and contract testing is additive, and the gap widens with every service you add.

At 8 × 12 that is 1,976 hours a year saved — 1.2 full-time engineers. They cross at three providers and three consumers, so below that integration testing is genuinely fine and contract testing is overhead. The argument only starts at four.

And the second benefit is the one people actually feel

An integration suite tells you that something broke. A contract test tells you who broke it, because it runs on the provider's side against the consumer's recorded expectations.

That converts a cross-team debugging session into a failed build on one team's pipeline — which is the same conversion chapter 11 kept finding: turn a coordination problem into a local one.

Nine of eleven are breaking, and only four of those are obvious.

The five that catch people — ordering, latency, validation, bug fixes, new enum values — change no schema at all. Every one changes behaviour someone depends on, which is why a schema diff is not a compatibility check.

The worst one has no good version

Fixing a bug in a computed value. It is unambiguously correct, it is breaking, and there is no way to ship it that is not — consumers have built around the wrong number and some of them are now compensating for it.

The only honest handling is to treat the fix as a breaking change with a version and a migration, which feels absurd and is cheaper than the alternative.

Stronger than I expected

I assumed there was a range where informal coordination wins — a few consumers, everyone in one room, versioning is ceremony. There is essentially none. No versioning is only cheapest at one consumer. The second consumer already justifies a strategy.

And additive-only wins almost everywhere, at 4.0× cheaper than no versioning at eight consumers. It is also the least fashionable option, because it means never removing anything — you accumulate cruft, and the cruft is cheaper than the coordination it avoids.

Versioned endpoints look reasonable and cost more than either. The expense is not the second version; it is the migration tail.

The curve is logarithmic in consumer count, which is the good news: 200 consumers takes 15 months to fall below one, and 20 consumers takes 9. Ten times the consumers costs six extra months, not ten times as long.

The bad news is who the last few are

The curve says 2.8 teams remain at a year. Those 2.8 teams are the ones with no active maintainer, and they will still be there at month 36 — the exponential describes teams that are choosing when to migrate, and the tail consists of the ones who are not choosing at all.

A hard deadline is the only thing that finishes it. And what a deadline actually is: a decision to break the stragglers on a known date rather than at a random one. That is chapter 6's "set a decision date", applied to other people's backlogs.

Which is why redesigning the interface does not work

It will grow back into the same shape, because chapter 11's ceiling has not moved. The interface is a photograph of an argument, and retaking the photograph does not settle the argument.

What works: give the interface one owner with the authority to say no. That is an organisational change costing days, against an interface redesign costing months and reverting.

Check yourself

Why does contract testing beat integration testing?

Growth order. Integration testing needs one suite per provider-consumer pair — N × M — while contract testing needs one per participant, N + M. At 8 providers and 12 consumers that is 96 against 20; at 20 × 40 it is 800 against 60. Worth 1,976 hours a year at the middle size, or 1.2 engineers.

Is it always worth it?

No — they cross at three providers and three consumers. Below that, integration testing is genuinely fine and contract testing is overhead. The argument only starts at four of each. There is a second benefit that does not depend on size, though: an integration suite tells you that something broke, a contract test tells you who.

How many common API changes are breaking?

Nine of eleven — and only four of those are obvious. The five that catch people change no schema at all: list ordering, latency, tighter validation, bug fixes in computed values, and new enum values. Which means a schema diff is not a compatibility check, and every automated gate can pass on a change that breaks everyone.

How do you ship a bug fix in a computed value?

As a breaking change, with a version and a migration. It is unambiguously correct, it is breaking, and there is no version of it that is not — consumers have built around the wrong number and some are compensating for it. Treating the fix as breaking feels absurd and is cheaper than the alternative.

When is informal coordination enough?

At one consumer. This surprised me — I assumed there was a range where everyone being in one room made versioning ceremony, and there essentially is not. The second consumer already justifies a strategy, and additive-only wins almost everywhere: 162 hours over three years at eight consumers against 648 for no versioning, a 4.0-fold difference.

What does versioned endpoints actually cost?

Not the second version — the migration tail. At 30% a month with 200 consumers: 23.5 teams still on v1 at six months, 2.8 at twelve, and it takes 15 months to fall below one. About the versioning costs

Four strategies, each with its own cost function and all four visible in the harness. The rank order is what matters: additive-only wins at every consumer count above one, and the specific hours depend on a breaking-change rate you can set from your own history.

The recurring conversion

Contract tests, one writer per table, one interface owner. All three are the same move — turning something that needs coordination between teams into something one team can decide alone.
