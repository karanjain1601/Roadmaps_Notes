What an architect actually decides

Twenty-three decisions a year, out of four hundred and forty-two thousand. On every aggregate measure that is a rounding error — so if the role has leverage, it is not in the decisions the architect personally makes.

Twenty-three of 442,023 — 0.005%. Five orders of magnitude rarer than a line-level decision and three orders of magnitude more expensive to undo.

And in aggregate they are not even the biggest risk

Multiply count by undo cost: line-level decisions carry 40,000 hours of total exposure and platform decisions carry 9,000. Small decisions carry 4.4× the aggregate risk.

What makes an architectural decision special is therefore not the total. It is that the risk is concentrated — it sits in one choice, made once, and cannot be spread across sixty people the way four hundred thousand small ones are.

The role's product is the distribution, not the decisions

This is why the roadmap for the job is mostly communication, documentation, standards and coaching rather than design. Those are not soft-skill garnish on a technical role — they are the mechanism by which 0.38% gets exceeded.

At a 1% shift the crossover is 23 engineers. Below that, an architect's influence does not beat their direct work — and the honest conclusion is that in a small team the architect should be building.

Where this goes wrong in practice

The job is one title spanning a 30-fold range of leverage ratios. A ten-person startup and a three-hundred-person organisation both advertise for a software architect, and the right behaviour in one is the wrong behaviour in the other.

An architect who stops writing code at 10 engineers has removed their most productive contribution. One who is still hand-designing modules at 300 has become a bottleneck — which chapter 8 of the Code Review unit measured as a queue, and the shape is the same.

The test

Two questions, and both must be yes

Would two competent teams, given the same requirements, reasonably land in different places? If no, it is not a decision — it is a fact someone needs to look up.

Is it hard to change later? If no, it is a decision but not yours — let whoever is closest make it and correct it if they are wrong.

The two-question form is worth more than a list of topics because it survives contact with situations the list does not cover. It also disqualifies most of what architects are asked to weigh in on.

Check yourself

How many decisions does an architect actually own?

Around 23 a year out of 442,023 — twelve service boundaries, eight data model choices, three platform or vendor decisions. That is 0.005% of the total: five orders of magnitude rarer than a line-level decision and three orders of magnitude more expensive to undo.

Do architectural decisions carry the most risk?

Not in aggregate. Multiplying count by undo cost, line-level decisions carry 40,000 hours of exposure and platform decisions carry 9,000 — small decisions carry 4.4× the total. What makes an architectural decision special is that the risk is concentrated: one choice, made once, that cannot be spread across sixty people the way four hundred thousand small ones are.

If the decisions are a rounding error, where is the leverage?

In shifting everyone else's. An architect personally makes about 1,600 decisions a year; influencing just 0.38% of sixty engineers' decisions equals all of it, and a 5% shift is 13× it. The role's product is the distribution of other people's decisions, which is why the job description is mostly communication, documentation, standards and coaching.

Should an architect write code?

It depends on team size, and the crossover is sharp. At a 1% shift, influence beats direct work only above 23 engineers — at 10 engineers a 1% shift moves 700 decisions against 1,600 made directly, a ratio of 0.4×. So in a small team the architect should be building, and stopping is removing their most productive contribution.

Why is "software architect" a confusing title?

Because it spans a 30-fold range of leverage ratios. A ten-person startup and a three-hundred-person organisation both advertise for one, and the correct behaviour in each is the wrong behaviour in the other: build at 10, distribute at 300. Someone still hand-designing modules at 300 engineers has become a bottleneck — the same queue shape that governs review latency.

What do the twenty-three decisions have in common?

Every one is about a boundary — between services, between teams, between owned and bought, between consistent and eventual. An architect draws lines. And none of them is about which algorithm, which library version or how a function is structured; those are the other four hundred thousand and they belong to whoever is writing them.

What is the test for whether a decision is yours?

Two questions, both must be yes. Would two competent teams reasonably land in different places? If no, it is a fact to look up, not a decision. Is it hard to change later? If no, let whoever is closest make it and correct it if they are wrong. One quadrant of four qualifies — and the main use of the test is declining the other three, which is most of what lands in the inbox.

About the numbers

The decision counts are a model of a sixty-engineer system, with every parameter visible in the demos. The conclusions that matter — that the architect's own decisions are a small fraction, and that the crossover with team size exists — hold across the whole plausible range rather than at the specific figures.
