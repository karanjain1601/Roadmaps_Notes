Data: the hardest thing to change

The same ALTER TABLE takes two seconds at launch and nine days on a payments ledger. Code changes cost the same regardless of how much data sits behind them. Data changes do not, and that one asymmetry drives everything in this chapter.

Which makes chapter 5's ratio a moving target

A schema decision's reversibility is not fixed — it grows with the table. At 1.5 million rows a month, a rewrite costs 6 minutes at one year and 30 minutes at five: sixty times the month-one cost, with nothing about the decision having changed.

The window closes at the rate the table fills, which is the most predictable closing rate in either unit and the one least often planned around.

Which migrations are one-way

Ten migrations, split evenly
MigrationWhy

Rename a columnReversibleIf nothing cached the old name

Narrow a typeOne-wayThe truncated data is gone
Drop a columnOne-wayThe data is gone
Merge two tablesOne-wayThe split point is not recoverable
Change a primary keyOne-wayEvery foreign key and external reference breaks
Delete rowsOne-wayGone

The dividing line is exact: every irreversible migration destroys information. After this change, can the previous state be reconstructed from what remains? That is the whole test, and adding is always reversible while removing never is.

Why expand-and-contract works

It splits one irreversible change into an add (reversible), a period of dual writing (reversible), and a drop done later. The drop is still one-way — you have simply moved it to after the evidence arrives.

And it gets relatively cheaper as the table grows: 3.0× a direct migration at 50 million rows, 2.4× at two billion, because the fixed human cost amortises over a rewrite that dominates either way. The reluctance to use it is strongest exactly where it costs least.

And compensators can fail too

Counting that, a five-step saga has 30 states to reason about against a transaction's 2. That is what leaving a single database costs in correctness surface, and it is paid in bugs rather than hours.

Put the consistency boundary where a transaction can still cover it.

If two things must be atomic, they belong in one database — and that constraint outranks the service boundary, not the other way round. Chapter 12 found deploy independence was the one thing worth splitting for; this is the thing that overrules it.

A schema outlives the service that created it by 3.2×, and the data lasts 5.5× as long as a service. You will rewrite the service around the schema, not the schema around the service.

Which makes the schema the more architectural decision

And it gets a fraction of the attention that the service topology does. It also explains a common failure precisely: teams split their services and keep the shared database. That is backwards — they have taken the reversible decision and left the irreversible one untouched.

Check yourself

Why is data harder to change than code?

Because the cost scales with volume and code's does not. At 50,000 rows a second, the same rewrite is 2 seconds on 100,000 rows and 9.3 days on 40 billion — a 400,000-fold range for the identical schema change. And it moves: at 1.5 million rows a month a rewrite costs 6 minutes at one year and 30 minutes at five, sixty times the month-one cost.

Which migrations are one-way?

Exactly the ones that destroy information: narrowing a type, dropping a column, merging tables, changing a primary key, deleting rows. Adding is always reversible; removing never is. The test is whether the previous state can be reconstructed from what remains — five of ten common migrations fail it.

When is expand-and-contract worth the ceremony?

More often than instinct suggests, and it gets relatively cheaper as tables grow — 3.0× a direct migration at 50 million rows and 2.4× at two billion, because the fixed human cost amortises over a rewrite that dominates either way. On a two-billion-row table that 2.4× buys away an eleven-hour outage. The reluctance is strongest exactly where the cost is lowest.

What does replacing a transaction with a saga cost?

Correctness surface, growing as steps(steps+1)/2. A five-step saga has 15 recovery paths against a transaction's 2, and 30 states once compensator failure is counted. That is the same n² term as chapter 2's consensus and chapter 11's coordination — it appears wherever pairs of things must agree.

So where should the consistency boundary go?

Where a transaction can still cover it. If two things must be atomic they belong in one database — and that constraint outranks the service boundary. Chapter 12 found deploy independence was the one benefit worth splitting for; atomicity is the thing that overrules it.

How long does a schema last?

13 years against a service's 4 — a 3.2× difference — and the data itself lasts 22, or 5.5× a service. You rewrite the service around the schema, not the other way round, which makes the schema the more architectural decision and it gets a fraction of the attention. It also names a common failure: splitting services while keeping the shared database takes the reversible decision and leaves the irreversible one alone.

How many services should write to a table?

One. Coordination on schema changes goes 0 → 1 → 6 → 28 pairs as writers go 1 → 2 → 4 → 8. Readers can be many, because a reader can be given a view (coupling 6), an API (4), their own copy (3) or an event stream (2) instead of direct table access (9). That 4.5-fold spread is chapter 11's table-to-event figure, because it is the same phenomenon.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. The same migration is 2 seconds or 9.3 days. Volume decides.
  2. A schema's reversibility ratio grows as the table fills — 60× in five years.
  3. Five of ten common migrations destroy information and are one-way.
  4. Adding is reversible; removing is not. That is the whole test.
  5. Expand-and-contract gets cheaper, relatively, as tables grow.
  6. A five-step saga has 15 recovery paths; 30 with compensator failure.
  7. Atomicity outranks the service boundary.
  8. A schema outlives its service 3.2×; the data 5.5×.
  9. One writer per table. Eight writers is 28 coordinating pairs.
  10. Readers get an event stream, not a table. Coupling 2 against 9.

About the rewrite rate

50,000 rows a second is an optimistic figure for a full table rewrite and is adjustable in the harness. The conclusions are ratios rather than absolutes: the 400,000-fold range and the 60× growth hold at any rate, because the rate cancels.

The recurring term

n(n−1)/2 appears here twice — saga recovery paths and writer coordination — having already set consensus cost in chapter 2, coordination in chapter 11 and the authorisation surface in the Code Review unit. It is the most portable piece of arithmetic in either unit.
