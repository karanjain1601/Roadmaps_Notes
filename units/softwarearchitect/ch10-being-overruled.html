The three free levers are the three most effective.

Evidence outperforms refusing to sign off by 2.1× — and the reason is mechanical rather than political. An escalation asks someone to overrule a peer. Evidence asks them to look at a fact. The second is cheaper for everyone, including the person you disagree with.

The first row is the one architects under-use

Writing the decision record is free, always available, and it is the lever that reliably moves things — not because it changes this decision, but because it makes the reasoning inspectable by whoever revisits it. Chapter 9 measured what happens when nobody does.

Which means architects should escalate more than they do

Credibility breaks even at a 57% win rate. The chance of actually winning an escalation is around 45%.

So escalation is credibility-negative on average

Every escalation costs you a little, even when the arithmetic says to do it. Over a career that works out at roughly 29 before you are down to half credibility — which is not a per-year allowance, it is most of a working life.

That resolves the tension with the previous section. The arithmetic says escalate above 78 hours; the budget says you get about thirty. So the operation is not a threshold, it is a ranking: sort the decisions you disagree with by downside and spend on the top few percent.

And note which row of the table is healthy. Four escalations with two won leaves you at 96% — better than not escalating at all, once the wins are counted. The failure mode is not escalating; it is escalating on things you cannot win.

A 3.9-fold difference at two years. Your prediction decays at the same rate as anything else — chapter 9's 22-month half-life applies to what you said as much as to what was decided.

So write the dissent into the record

Dated, one paragraph, without rancour: "I argued for X because Y. We chose Z. If Y turns out to matter, we will see it as W."

That is not politics. It is the only mechanism by which the organisation can learn anything from having overruled you — and the last clause is what makes it checkable rather than a grievance.

The organisational cost of skipping it is larger than the personal one. Chapter 2 measured twelve feedback cycles in an enterprise architecture career; unrecorded dissent throws away the cycles you did get, which is the most expensive habit in either of these two units.

Only two of the six are about persuasion, which is where most of the effort goes. Check whether you are wrong first — it is the cheapest hypothesis to test, chapter 7 gives you the method, and it is the one nobody tests.

And one is not fixable by trying harder

"You are not in the room" is structural. No amount of better argument reaches a decision made without you, and the fix is a calendar change rather than a rhetorical one — which is worth diagnosing early, because the rhetorical version of the fix can absorb years.

Check yourself

What can an architect actually do about a decision they disagree with?

Six levers, and the three that cost nothing are the three most effective: writing the decision record (100%), supplying evidence (85%), naming the reversibility ratio (70%). Escalating is 55%, refusing to sign off 40%, and a veto 10% — which most architects do not hold. Evidence beats refusing to sign off by 2.1×, because an escalation asks someone to overrule a peer while evidence asks them to look at a fact.

When is escalating worth it?

Above a 78-hour downside, at a 40% chance they are wrong and a 45% chance you win. Chapter 1's cheapest architectural decision has a 400-hour undo cost, so essentially everything an architect owns clears the bar — the arithmetic says escalate considerably more than most architects do.

So why does nobody escalate that often?

The credibility budget. Each escalation spends about 8% and each win returns 14%, so credibility breaks even at a 57% win rate against an actual 45% — escalation is credibility-negative on average. That gives roughly 29 before you are at half credibility, over a career rather than a year. The operation is therefore a ranking, not a threshold: sort by downside and spend on the top few percent.

Is never escalating the safe option?

No — four escalations with two won leaves you at 96% credibility, better than the arithmetic of not escalating suggests, because the wins count. The failure mode is not escalating; it is escalating on things you cannot win: ten with none won leaves 20%.

You were overruled and turned out right. Does anyone notice?

At two years, 23% of the time without a record and 90% with a dated one — a 3.9-fold difference. Your prediction decays at chapter 9's 22-month half-life just like anything else, so being right is only observable if someone wrote down what you predicted before the outcome arrived.

How should a dissent be written?

Dated, one paragraph, without rancour, and with a test: "I argued for X because Y. We chose Z. If Y turns out to matter, we will see it as W." The last clause is what makes it checkable rather than a grievance — and it means you also find out when you were the one who was wrong. A dissent recorded as "I warned them" supplies no test and is unreadable later.

You keep getting overruled. What do you check first?

Whether you are wrong — using chapter 7's reference class. It is the cheapest hypothesis to test and the one nobody tests. Only two of six causes are about persuasion, which is where most of the effort goes. And one cause is not fixable by trying harder at all: "you are not in the room" is structural, and the fix is a calendar change rather than a rhetorical one.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. The three free levers are the three most effective.
  2. Evidence beats refusing to sign off by 2.1×.
  3. The escalation threshold is 78 hours of downside — almost everything clears it.
  4. Credibility breaks even at a 57% win rate; you win 45%.
  5. Escalation is credibility-negative on average.
  6. About 29 escalations in a career. Rank, do not threshold.
  7. Four with two won leaves you at 96%. Not escalating is not safer.
  8. 23% credit at two years without a record; 90% with one.
  9. Write the dissent with a test in it, not a grievance.
  10. Check whether you are wrong first. Cheapest hypothesis, least tested.

About the credibility model

The 8% cost, 14% gain and 45% win rate are assumptions, with all three exposed in the demos. The structural conclusion — that escalation is net negative when the win rate sits below cost over gain, and that the constraint is therefore a ranking rather than a threshold — holds wherever those two numbers cross.

The shape of Part 2
