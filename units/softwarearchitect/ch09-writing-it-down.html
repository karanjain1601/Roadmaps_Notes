How long "why" survives

Two mechanisms erode it. People leave — 15% a year — and the ones who stay forget the reasoning at about 5.5% a month. Neither alone is fatal.

"Nobody knows why" is recoverable — you re-decide. The risk is a plausible reconstruction that is wrong, offered by someone who was in the room and does not realise they are guessing.

Nobody questions it, because the source is impeccable. That is how a constraint that no longer exists survives for a decade as folklore.

Putting more people in the room is a weak lever: doubling from three to six extends the half-life by nine months, and costs the n² alignment from chapter 2. Writing it down costs four hours and does not decay at all.

What re-litigating costs

An undocumented decision gets re-argued — modelled at 0.8 times a year, 26 hours each time in meetings, threads and someone redoing the analysis.

Options rejected plus why rejected is 2.5 of the 5 hours — and "why rejected" is the most-used paid field at 90%, and the one most often left out.

"We considered X and Y" without saying why not invites the exact conversation the record exists to prevent. A record that is mostly about what you did is the wrong shape.

And two fields cost nothing at all: the date and who decided. The date alone tells a reader how much of the surrounding context has since changed, which is often all they needed.

The counterintuitive part

When the system moves off the choice, the record becomes more valuable, not less. It is now the only surviving account of why the thing you are replacing exists — which is exactly what you need in order to replace it safely.

Where the standard format falls short

Six gaps in a conventional decision record
GapConsequence
No field for how you would know you were wrongNothing triggers a review
Status is binary — accepted or supersededNo "accepted, and we are watching X"
No record of what it cost to decideThe next estimate has no reference class
Numbered sequentially, not by subjectFinding the relevant one means reading all of them
No link to the code it governsThe code changes and the record does not notice
Written once, at the moment of least knowledgeThe day you knew least about the outcome

Add a dated postscript when the outcome is known.

Two lines eighteen months later are worth more than the original four hours, because they are the only place the outcome is recorded. Everything else in the record is a prediction.

And it feeds chapter 7 for free

A decision record with an outcome postscript is a reference-class entry. Twelve of them is exactly the sample size chapter 7's forecasting method needed — and nobody has to run a study to get it.

That is the strongest argument for the postscript habit: it converts the documentation you were reluctantly writing into the estimation data you were going to lack.

Check yourself

How long does the reasoning behind a decision survive?

A 22-month half-life, from two mechanisms: people leave at 15% a year and the ones who stay forget the reasoning at about 5.5% a month. With three people who knew, an accurate answer is available 81.6% of the time at one year, 46% at two, and 22.2% at three.

What is the actual risk — that nobody remembers?

No, that is recoverable: you re-decide. The risk is a confident wrong answer. By month 36 a plausible reconstruction is more likely than an accurate memory — 39.2% against 22.2% — and nobody questions it because the source was in the room. That is how a constraint which no longer exists survives for a decade as folklore.

Would involving more people help?

Barely. Doubling from three people to six extends the half-life by nine months and costs the n² alignment burden from chapter 2 — twenty-eight relationships instead of three. Writing it down takes four hours and does not decay at all, which is a much better trade for the same goal.

Does documentation actually pay for itself?

In about seven months, on re-litigation alone. Modelled at 0.8 re-arguments a year costing 26 hours each: three years undocumented is 38.6 hours against 8.8 documented — a 4.4-fold saving. And that excludes onboarding and knowledge transfer, which are the arguments usually made and the weaker ones.

Which field matters most?

Why rejected — used later 90% of the time, costs one hour, and is the field most often left out. Together with "options rejected" it is 2.5 of the 5 hours, so half the writing effort belongs to the alternatives. "We considered X and Y" without saying why not invites the exact conversation the record exists to prevent.

Which fields are free?

The date and who decided — no writing time at all, and used later 35% and 25% of the time respectively. The date is the more valuable of the two, because it tells a reader how much of the surrounding context has since changed, which is often the only thing they needed to know.

What is missing from a conventional decision record?

It is written once, at the moment of least knowledge — the day you knew least about the outcome. The fix is a dated postscript when the outcome is known: two lines eighteen months later are worth more than the original four hours, because everything else in the record is a prediction. And a record with an outcome postscript is a reference-class entry, so twelve of them supplies exactly the estimation data chapter 7 needed.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. A reason has a 22-month half-life. People leave and people forget.
  2. By month 36 a confident wrong answer beats an accurate one.
  3. More people in the room buys nine months and costs n² alignment.
  4. Re-litigation alone pays for the record in seven months.
  5. 4.4-fold saving by year three, before onboarding value.
  6. "Why rejected" is used 90% of the time and skipped most often.
  7. Half the effort belongs to the alternatives, not the choice.
  8. The date and who decided are free. Always write them.
  9. Current-state docs are 11% accurate at three years; decisions stay 100%.
  10. Add a dated postscript. It is the only record of the outcome.

About the model

Retention, forgetting rate, re-litigation frequency and the per-field usage rates are all assumptions, and three of them are sliders. The structural conclusions — that two decay mechanisms compound, and that confabulation overtakes accuracy — hold across the plausible range.

Where it connects

Chapter 3 measured the eleven-month half-life of a current-state document. Chapter 7 needed twelve reference-class data points. This chapter's postscript habit is the same artefact solving both.
