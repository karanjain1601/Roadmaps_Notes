Chapter 6 · Deciding

Options and deferring

Under passive waiting, no length of deferral has a positive net. Not three months, not twelve. "Decide at the last responsible moment" is good advice attached to an assumption almost nobody checks: that waiting teaches you something.

What an option is worth

Deferring buys one thing: the chance to choose better later. Its value is the reduction in how likely you are to be wrong, times what being wrong costs.

The assumption everything rests on

Deferring pays only if information arrives. "We'll decide later" with no mechanism for learning anything is not an option — it is a delay, and the third row prices it exactly.

And holding the option open costs

Not committing is not free. You build an abstraction you might not need, and you work around the undecided thing every week until it is decided.

The last responsible moment, computed

The phrase is popular and usually applied by instinct. It has an arithmetic form: defer while one more month of waiting teaches you more than it costs.

A 7.5-fold decline. Against 14 hours a month of drag, the last responsible moment lands at month 7 — and unlike the instinct version, it terminates.

What actually sets the window is the drag, not the learning

At 5 hours a month of drag the window extends to month 12. At 40 hours it collapses to month 3. Same learning rate, same option value, a fourfold difference in how long you can honestly wait.

So the lever for buying more time is not finding out faster — it is making it cheaper to work around the undecided thing. That is a design activity, and it is usually available.

The abstraction layer is the most seductive one

It feels like keeping your options open. Priced: it costs 2.5× the plain deferral up front, reduces the drag only partially, and is net negative even at twelve months — −72 hours.

And the abstraction itself is now a decision you did not want to make. You have replaced one open question with two.

The test is two questions. What will you know then that you do not know now? and what makes it arrive? A deferral that cannot answer both is a delay, and the fourth real option on that list — setting a date — works precisely because it answers the second one by force.

And the window is closing while you wait

Active learning is no longer enough

With the wrong-choice cost growing, the best deferral under a 20% learning rate collapses to zero months. You are learning at 20% a month while the penalty grows at 150% — you lose ground every month you wait.

It recovers at a 40% learning rate, which returns a ten-month window. Forty percent a month is not a reading list; it is a running prototype.

Defer decisions whose window is not closing. Make the ones whose window is.

That is the resolution between this chapter and the last. "Decide late" is excellent advice for decisions nothing is attaching to, and actively harmful for the ones accumulating dependants — which are, unhelpfully, the ones that feel most worth deferring.

Check yourself

What is a deferred decision actually worth?

The reduction in how likely you are to be wrong, times what being wrong costs. Against a 900-hour wrong-choice cost: six months of passive waiting is worth 83 hours, six months of active learning 232, and six months during which nothing arrives is worth exactly zero. Deferring pays only if information arrives.

Is deferring free otherwise?

No — you build an abstraction you might not need and work around the undecided thing every week. At 60 hours up front plus 14 a month, the best passive deferral is three months at a net of −57 hours: no length has a positive net at all. The same seven months is +91 with a learning mechanism and −63 without.

How do you find the last responsible moment?

Defer while one more month teaches you more than it costs. Under active learning the next month is worth +63 hours at month 0 and +8.5 at month 9 — a 7.5-fold decline — so against 14 hours a month of drag the moment lands at month 7. Unlike the instinct version it terminates, which is most of its value.

What sets the length of the window?

The drag, not the learning rate. At 5 hours a month the window extends to month 12; at 40 hours it collapses to month 3 — a fourfold difference at the same learning rate. So the lever for buying more time is making it cheaper to work around the undecided thing, which is a design activity and usually available.

Is writing an abstraction layer a way to keep your options open?

It is the most seductive fake option. Priced, it costs 2.5× the plain deferral up front, reduces drag only partially, and is net −72 hours even at twelve months. And the abstraction is itself a decision you did not want to make — you have replaced one open question with two.

What separates a real option from a delay?

Two questions: what will you know then that you do not know now, and what makes it arrive? A spike, a pilot, building the cheap version, and setting a decision date all answer both. Waiting for a vendor's roadmap, "until we have more data", and an abstraction layer answer neither. Setting a date is the cheapest real option, because it answers the second question by force.

Should you defer a decision that things are attaching to?

No — and this is where chapters 5 and 6 meet. With undo cost growing at 1.5 dependants a month, the best deferral under a 20% learning rate collapses to zero months: you learn at 20% while the penalty grows at 150%. It only recovers at a 40% learning rate, which means a running prototype rather than a reading list. Defer what nothing is attaching to; decide what is accruing dependants.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. An option is worth the error it removes, times the cost of the error.
  2. If nothing arrives, deferring is worth zero.
  3. Under passive waiting no deferral length has a positive net.
  4. Seven months: +91 with learning, −63 without.
  5. The last responsible moment has an arithmetic form and it terminates.
  6. Drag sets the window, not the learning rate — month 3 or month 12.
  7. Reduce the cost of not knowing before trying to learn faster.
  8. An abstraction layer is −72 hours and a second decision.
  9. Two questions: what will you learn, and what makes it arrive.
  10. Defer what nothing is attaching to. Decide the rest now.

About the learning rates

Five percent a month for passive waiting and twenty for an active mechanism are assumptions, exposed as a slider. The conclusion that survives any setting is the structure: the option value curve is concave and the carrying cost is linear, so a maximum exists and it is finite.

The pair with chapter 5

Chapter 5 measured that undo cost grows as dependants accumulate. This chapter shows what that does to deferral, and the two together give one rule rather than two competing slogans.
