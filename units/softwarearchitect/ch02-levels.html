A twentyfold difference in practice, over a whole career

An enterprise architect may make a dozen consequential calls in thirty years and see the outcome of each only after two and a half. There is no amount of diligence that turns twelve repetitions into two hundred and forty.

The model rewards repetition and nothing else, which is its honest limitation — and pointing at the limitation is where the useful advice lives. Adding 100 borrowed cycles takes the enterprise figure from 76% to essentially the ceiling.

What borrowing looks like

Post-mortems from other organisations. Migration write-ups. Conference talks about failures rather than successes. Reading your own estate's history — the decisions made before you arrived and what happened to them.

At application level, case studies supplement experience. At enterprise level they largely are the experience, because the alternative is twelve data points.

An 8.3-fold increase in headcount produces a 100-fold increase in alignment cost. At 25 stakeholders, full consensus is eleven weeks of meetings — which is why enterprise decisions are made by mandate, delegation or exhaustion rather than agreement.

The actual choice, and it is not good against fast

Hub-and-spoke costs 50 hours instead of 450 — linear rather than quadratic, a ninefold saving. It also produces a worse decision, because the stakeholders never hear each other's constraints and the architect becomes a lossy relay.

Pretending that is a choice between rigour and speed is how enterprise decisions get made badly and slowly. It is a choice between a decision that is worse and one that does not happen.

Planning against a context of which 97% will have changed.

That is the enterprise architect's structural problem, and the answer is not better forecasting — no amount of rigour recovers information that does not exist yet.

What falls out instead

The value of deferring a decision rises from 44% at a two-year horizon to 97% at twelve. The longer the horizon, the more the right move is to preserve options rather than to choose.

Which is chapter 6, and it is why enterprise architecture that looks like a twelve-year roadmap is usually a mistake dressed as diligence. The deliverable at that horizon is a set of constraints that keep options open, not a plan.

Moving up a level is not a bigger version of the same job. The unit of change, the audience, the artefact and the failure mode all change — and by the top, the constraint has stopped being technical altogether.

What does carry

Judgement about boundaries and reversibility. That is the entire transferable core, and it is why chapter 1's two-question test works unchanged at all three levels: would two competent parties reasonably differ, and is it hard to change later.

Everything else — the diagrams, the frameworks, the vocabulary — is level-specific and has to be relearned.

Check yourself

What actually distinguishes the three levels?

Scope is the stated difference and feedback is the real one. Application architects get 8 feedback cycles a year, solution architects 1.33, enterprise architects 0.4 — 240, 40 and 12 over a thirty-year career. That twentyfold gap in practice is what makes them three different jobs rather than three sizes of one.

Why is enterprise judgement hard to acquire?

Because you get twelve consequential decisions in a career and see the outcome of each only after two and a half years. Modelled competence after thirty years is 76% at enterprise level against 100% at application level — and no amount of diligence turns twelve repetitions into two hundred and forty.

What closes that gap?

Borrowing other people's cycles. Adding 100 borrowed cycles takes the enterprise figure from 76% to essentially the ceiling. In practice that means post-mortems from other organisations, migration write-ups, talks about failures rather than successes, and your own estate's history. At application level case studies supplement experience; at enterprise level they largely are the experience.

Why does consensus stop being possible as you move up?

It grows as n². Three stakeholders is 3 relationships and 4.5 hours; twenty-five is 300 relationships and 450 hours — an 8.3-fold increase in headcount producing a 100-fold increase in cost. At 25 people, full consensus is eleven weeks of meetings, which is why enterprise decisions are made by mandate, delegation or exhaustion.

Is hub-and-spoke the efficient alternative?

It is the linear one — 50 hours instead of 450, a ninefold saving — and it produces a genuinely worse decision, because stakeholders never hear each other's constraints and the architect becomes a lossy relay. Calling it "efficient" hides the trade. It is not a choice between rigour and speed; it is a choice between a decision that is worse and one that does not happen.

What is wrong with a twelve-year roadmap?

At 25% annual change, 3% of its assumptions still hold at the horizon — against 56% over two years. No amount of forecasting rigour recovers information that does not exist yet. What rises instead is the value of deferring: from 44% at a two-year horizon to 97% at twelve. The deliverable at that range is a set of constraints that keep options open, not a plan.

What transfers between the levels?

Judgement about boundaries and reversibility — which is why chapter 1's two-question test works unchanged at all three. Almost nothing else does: the unit of change, the audience, the artefact and the failure mode all differ, and by enterprise level the binding constraint is political and financial rather than technical. Promoting someone on application-level excellence gives them 240 cycles of the wrong kind.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Scope is the stated difference; feedback is the real one.
  2. 240, 40 and 12 cycles across a thirty-year career.
  3. The decisions that matter most get the least practice.
  4. Enterprise competence models at 76% on repetition alone.
  5. 100 borrowed cycles closes it. Case studies are the experience.
  6. Consensus grows as n²: 4.5 hours, 42, then 450.
  7. Hub-and-spoke is 9× cheaper and produces a worse decision. Say so.
  8. 3% of a twelve-year plan's assumptions survive.
  9. Deferring is worth 97% at that horizon. Constraints, not roadmaps.
  10. Only boundaries and reversibility transfer. The rest is relearned.

About the competence model

It rewards repetition and nothing else, which is deliberately its weakness — the useful conclusion comes from the limitation rather than the curve. The borrowed-cycles term is exposed as a slider so you can see how much of the gap it closes.
