The latency floor is physics

Light travels 299,792 km/s in vacuum. Single-mode fibre has a refractive index of about 1.4675, so a signal moves at 204,288 km/s — 68% of c. Cable routes are roughly 1.4× the great-circle distance.

A latency budget is a round-trip budget

At 100 ms you get 11 sequential round trips to Frankfurt and zero to Sydney. At 500 ms you get two to Sydney.

That turns a vague performance goal into an arithmetic constraint on the design. The only levers are the number of round trips and where the data is — "make it faster" is not on the menu, and "do not go there synchronously" is.

CAP, without the folklore

The version everyone quotes — "pick two of consistency, availability, partition tolerance" — is wrong in a way that matters. Partition tolerance is not a choice. Networks partition. The theorem says that when a partition happens, you must choose between consistency and availability.

Four states, and two of them are identical
NetworkSystemBehaviour
No partitionCPConsistent and available
No partitionAPConsistent and available
PartitionedCPRefuses writes on the minority side
PartitionedAPAccepts writes, diverges

With no partition, CP and AP behave identically. A four-nines link is down 52.6 minutes a year; a three-nines link, 525.6.

So the question is not "which two"

It is what should happen during those fifty-three minutes. For a payment ledger, refuse. For a shopping cart, accept and reconcile. That is the entire content of the theorem for a working architect, and stated that way it is answerable by a product owner rather than an engineer.

Quorum arithmetic

Strong consistency needs R + W > N. That is not a design philosophy, it is a pigeonhole argument — and everything else follows from it.

R + W > N buys consistency and costs failure tolerance.

At a fixed N you cannot have both. N=3 R=1 W=1 tolerates two failures and is eventual; N=3 R=2 W=2 is strong and tolerates one. The only escape is a bigger N — and N=5 R=3 W=3 gets the weakest N=3 configuration's fault tolerance with strong consistency, for 67% more storage and 67% more write cost.

That is what "not choosing" costs, priced. It is available, and it is not free.

The half of CAP nobody discusses

PACELC states it properly: if there is a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, choose Latency or Consistency.

And this is the half that actually matters

Partitions cost 53 minutes a year. The latency-consistency trade is paid on every single read, forever — and it gets a fraction of the discussion that CAP does, because CAP has a theorem attached and a memorable wrong summary.

The test

Could a sufficiently good engineer make it go away?

If yes, it is a preference and the argument is about cost. If no, the argument is over, and the only remaining question is what you build around it.

Both category errors are common and they fail differently. Treating a constraint as a preference produces "can we not just make the cross-region call faster" — a question with no answer, asked repeatedly. Treating a preference as a constraint is how a language choice becomes a two-year argument, because neither side can concede a point they have framed as physics.

The last row is worth separating out. "Eventual consistency is fine here" is neither — it is a claim about the domain, checkable by asking what a user sees when it is wrong. Chapter 13 is largely about how to check it.

Check yourself

What sets the floor on cross-region latency?

Distance divided by the speed of light in fibre. Light travels 299,792 km/s in vacuum and 204,288 km/s in single-mode fibre — 68% of c — and real cable routes run about 1.4× the great-circle distance. London to Sydney is 166 ms round trip in a straight line and 233 ms in practice. No engineering removes it.

So what is a latency budget, really?

A round-trip budget, with the exchange rate set by geography. At 100 ms you get 11 sequential round trips to Frankfurt and zero to Sydney; at 500 ms you get two to Sydney. That converts a vague performance goal into an arithmetic constraint, and leaves only two levers: the number of round trips, and where the data is.

What is wrong with "pick two of CAP"?

Partition tolerance is not a choice — networks partition. The theorem says that when a partition happens, you must choose between consistency and availability. With no partition, CP and AP systems behave identically. A four-nines link is down 52.6 minutes a year, so the real question is what should happen during those fifty-three minutes: refuse for a ledger, accept and reconcile for a cart.

What does R + W > N cost you?

Failure tolerance, at a fixed N. N=3 R=1 W=1 tolerates two failures and is eventual; N=3 R=2 W=2 is strong and tolerates one. You cannot have both without a bigger N — and N=5 R=3 W=3 gets two-failure tolerance with strong consistency, for 67% more storage and 67% more write cost. Not choosing is available and priced.

Is requiring all replicas to acknowledge a write safer?

It is stronger and considerably more fragile. N=3 W=3 drops write availability from 99.9702% to 97.0299% — nearly three percentage points — while tolerating zero node failures. Safety and availability point opposite ways in quorum arithmetic, and the intuitively cautious choice is the one that falls over first.

Which half of PACELC matters more?

The "else". Partitions cost 53 minutes a year; the latency-consistency trade is paid on every read, forever. Reading the same value in London with a New York leader: 1.0 ms locally and possibly stale, 39.2 ms at quorum, 77.3 ms from the leader — a 77-fold difference for the identical query. It gets a fraction of CAP's discussion because CAP has a theorem and a memorable wrong summary.

How do you tell a constraint from a preference?

Ask whether a sufficiently good engineer could make it go away. If yes it is a preference and the argument is about cost; if no the argument is over and the only question is what you build around it. Both category errors are common: treating a constraint as a preference produces "can we not just make it faster", and treating a preference as physics is how a language choice becomes a two-year argument.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Fibre carries signal at 204,288 km/s — 68% of c.
  2. London to Sydney is 233 ms round trip. Nothing changes that.
  3. A latency budget is a round-trip budget; geography sets the rate.
  4. Partition tolerance is not a choice. The trade bites during partitions only.
  5. CP and AP are identical when the network is healthy.
  6. Decide what happens in those 53 minutes a year.
  7. R + W > N buys consistency, costs fault tolerance.
  8. W = N drops write availability to 97.03% and tolerates nothing.
  9. The PACELC "else" is paid on every read.
  10. Could a good engineer make it go away? That is the whole test.
