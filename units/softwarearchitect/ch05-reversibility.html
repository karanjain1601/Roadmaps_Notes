Reversibility, and the cost of being wrong

Over-deliberating a JSON library wastes 63 hours. Under-deliberating a cloud vendor costs 1,691. A twenty-seven-fold asymmetry — which does not mean be careful about everything, because that turns out never to be free.

Reversibility is a ratio

"One-way door" and "two-way door" are useful names and not a measurement. The measurement is hours to undo divided by hours to do.

Seven decisions, priced
DecisionDoUndoRatio
Add a feature flag4 h1 h0.25
Pick a JSON library12 h6 h0.5
Choose a web framework200 h600 h3.0
Split out a service400 h1,400 h3.5
Pick a primary datastore300 h2,400 h8.0
Choose a cloud vendor600 h9,000 h15.0
Drop a column2 hnever∞

Below 1 the decision is cheaper to reverse than to make. Above 10 it is effectively permanent.

Why a ratio rather than an absolute number

A 9,000-hour undo is terrifying in a startup and routine at a bank. The ratio travels; the absolute figure does not — which matters because architects move between contexts and carry their instincts with them.

The crossover is an undo cost of 328 hours — at a 100-hour build, a reversibility ratio of 3.3. So: deliberate when undoing would cost more than about three times building. Below that, decide in an afternoon and be willing to be wrong.

Which policy, though?

Given the asymmetry, the obvious move is to classify every decision and apply the right rigour to each. That is right, and it is not always right, which was not what I expected.

Classifying beats both blanket policies while one-way doors are rare. Past about one in six, your own 15% misclassification rate costs more than simply being careful about everything.

Which reframes a familiar argument. At a startup, where almost every decision is genuinely reversible, classify and move fast. In a regulated estate where a fifth of decisions are one-way doors, blanket rigour is not bureaucracy — it is the cheaper policy, and the people defending it may be right for reasons neither side has articulated.

The one policy that is never best at any base rate is all quick above about 3%. It wins only where one-way doors are genuinely rare, and "genuinely rare" is an empirical claim about your estate rather than a temperament.

Making a one-way door two-way

The classification is not fixed. You can spend a small amount up front to buy back a large fraction of the undo cost — which is the third option the previous section did not have.

One pattern with several names

Expand-and-contract, a pilot, a feature flag, an adapter, an exit clause, insurance. All of them are the same trade: spend a small fixed amount to buy back a large fraction of the undo cost.

And the threshold matches section two's exactly. At a 50-hour undo, none of the six pay; at 200, two do. Below the deliberation threshold you should be moving fast and not buying insurance — the two decisions are the same decision.

Doors people misclassify

Seven, and four of them in the dangerous direction
DecisionFeelsActually
A database schemaTwo-wayOne-way once there is data in it
An event schemaTwo-wayOne-way once anyone consumes it
A vendor integrationTwo-wayOne-way by the time it is load-bearing
A public APITwo-wayOne-way from the first external caller
A URL structureOne-wayTwo-way, with redirects
A programming languageOne-wayTwo-way at a service boundary
A naming conventionOne-wayTwo-way — it is a formatter away
The four dangerous ones share a mechanism

Every one becomes irreversible when something else starts depending on it — data, consumers, callers. Reversibility is not a property of the decision. It is a property of the decision and its age.

Modelled at one and a half new dependants a month: a decision costing 100 hours to undo today costs 5,500 after three years. A fifty-five-fold increase, with nothing about the decision itself having changed.

The window in which a decision is two-way closes.

Which is why the useful question at the moment of deciding is not "is this reversible?" but "how long will it stay reversible, and what will have attached to it by then?" That question is free now and unanswerable later.

Check yourself

How do you measure reversibility?

Hours to undo divided by hours to do. Below 1 the decision is cheaper to reverse than to make; above 10 it is effectively permanent. A feature flag is 0.25, a web framework 3.0, a cloud vendor 15.0. The ratio rather than the absolute number, because a 9,000-hour undo is terrifying in a startup and routine at a bank — the ratio travels between contexts and the figure does not.

How different are the two ways of getting it wrong?

Twenty-seven-fold. Over-deliberating a JSON library wastes 63 hours; under-deliberating a cloud vendor costs 1,691. The crossover is an undo cost of 328 hours — at a 100-hour build, a reversibility ratio of 3.3. Deliberate when undoing would cost more than about three times building; below that, decide in an afternoon and be willing to be wrong.

Given that asymmetry, should you be careful about everything?

It depends on your base rate, and this surprised me. Classifying beats both blanket policies while one-way doors are rare — but above a 17.4% one-way base rate, your own 15% misclassification rate costs more than simply being careful about everything. At a startup, classify and move fast; in a regulated estate where a fifth of decisions are one-way, blanket rigour is the cheaper policy rather than bureaucracy.

Can you change a decision's classification?

Yes — spend a small fixed amount to buy back a large fraction of the undo cost. Against a 2,400-hour undo at a 30% chance of being wrong, putting it behind a flag costs 16 hours and leaves 15% of the exposure: an expected 124 hours against 720, an 83% reduction. Expand-and-contract, a pilot, an adapter, an exit clause and insurance are all the same trade under different names.

When is conversion not worth it?

Below the same threshold as deliberation. At a 50-hour undo, none of the six conversions pay; at 200, two do. That symmetry is the useful part: below the threshold you should be moving fast and not buying insurance, because they are the same decision. Adding a flag to a cheap reversible choice is cost with no purchase.

Which decisions get misclassified most often?

Four feel reversible and are not — a database schema, an event schema, a vendor integration, a public API. Three feel permanent and are not — a URL structure (redirects), a language (at a service boundary), a naming convention (a formatter away). The dangerous four share a mechanism: each becomes irreversible when something else starts depending on it.

Why does classifying once not work?

Because reversibility is a property of the decision and its age. At one and a half new dependants a month, a decision costing 100 hours to undo today costs 5,500 after three years — a fifty-five-fold increase with nothing about the decision itself changing. The window in which something is two-way closes, and the trigger is not a code change but the fifth team adopting it.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Reversibility = undo hours ÷ do hours. The ratio travels; the figure does not.
  2. Below 1, cheaper to reverse than to make. Above 10, permanent.
  3. The two mistakes differ 27-fold: 63 hours wasted against 1,691 lost.
  4. Deliberate above a ratio of about 3.3.
  5. Classifying wins only below a 17.4% one-way base rate.
  6. Blanket caution is the cheaper policy in a one-way-heavy estate.
  7. A flag buys back 83% of the exposure for 16 hours.
  8. Conversion and deliberation share a threshold. Below it, do neither.
  9. Four common decisions feel two-way and are not.
  10. Undo cost rises 55-fold over three years. The window closes.

About the policy comparison

The three-policy table depends on one number the chapter cannot supply: your own misclassification rate, set at 15% here. It is exposed nowhere in the demos because it is the assumption most worth arguing with — at 5% classifying wins everywhere, and at 30% blanket caution wins almost everywhere.
