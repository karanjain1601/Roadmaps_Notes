Monolith, services, and where it flips

I built this expecting a U-curve — some optimal number of services. There isn't one. On pure cost, every service you add costs more than the smaller codebase saves, and a twelve-service request path is less available than the monolith it replaced.

This is not the shape I expected

I wrote the model expecting a shallow dip — some service count where the smaller-codebase benefit beats the tax. There is no dip. Cost climbs at every step except one wrinkle at three services, which is 5 hours against a 3,902-hour baseline and is noise.

Change volume does move it eventually: at 1,200 changes a year the optimum is 5 services, and at 4,000 it is 9. But at 300 changes a year — a busy team — one deployable is cheapest, and it stays cheapest however large the codebase gets.

So splitting is not justified by cost. Ever, at normal volumes.

Which means the case has to rest on something cost does not capture. Two candidates get offered — availability and release cadence — and only one of them survives.

At 99.95% per service you can afford two hops before you are worse off than the monolith. A twelve-hop path has 6.0× the downtime.

And more nines per service does not fix it

To match a 99.9% monolith, a twelve-hop path needs each service at 99.99166% — ten times more reliable than the monolith was, just to break even.

The escape is not reliability engineering. It is fewer synchronous hops: caching, async, and graceful degradation take services off the critical path, which is the only lever that scales. Chapter 8's latency arithmetic pushes the same way.

So availability is not an argument for splitting. It is an argument against it, and the systems that split successfully spend real effort getting services off the request path afterwards.

This is the one benefit that survives. Everything else offered for splitting — independent scaling, language choice, team autonomy — is either secondary or achievable without separate deployables.

And it identifies the failure mode precisely

Services that must be released together give you the 182-hour tax, the availability compounding, and the release cadence of a monolith. That is the distributed monolith, and it is strictly worse than either endpoint — 2,184 hours of tax for twelve releases a year.

The test is one sentence: can you deploy one of them, on a Friday, without asking anyone? If not, the split has not bought the thing you split for.

So the question has a number in it

Is deploy independence worth 1,092 hours a year plus the availability cost?

If you release monthly and would like to release weekly, that is a clear yes — the 52× cadence dwarfs it. If you already release weekly and the bottleneck is review or QA, you are buying nothing, and chapter 8 of the Code Review unit will tell you where the queue actually is.

Which produces a boring recommendation with arithmetic behind it: modular monolith first, split the modules that need independent deploy, and stop.

Check yourself

What does a service cost before it does anything?

182 hours a year — dependency updates 38, on-call 30, integration tests 28, monitoring 26, CI 22, auth plumbing 18, deploy config 14, and a place in every diagram 6. Independent of what the service does: a 200-line service pays the same as a 20,000-line one. Twelve services is 2,184 hours, or 1.4 full-time engineers, and no feature ships because of it.

Is there an optimal number of services?

Not on cost. I wrote the model expecting a dip and there is none — cost climbs at every step bar one 5-hour wrinkle at three services, against a 3,902-hour baseline. At 300 changes a year, one deployable is cheapest and stays cheapest however large the codebase grows. Volume moves it eventually: 5 services at 1,200 changes a year, 9 at 4,000.

Does splitting improve availability?

It makes it worse. At 99.95% per service you can afford two synchronous hops before falling below a 99.9% monolith; a twelve-hop path is 99.4016% — 3,145 minutes of downtime a year against 526, a 6.0-fold increase. Availability is an argument against splitting, not for it.

Can you fix that with more reliable services?

Not realistically. To match a 99.9% monolith, a twelve-hop path needs each service at 99.99166% — ten times more reliable than the monolith was, just to break even. The escape is fewer synchronous hops: caching, async and graceful degradation take services off the critical path, which is the only lever that scales.

So what does splitting actually buy?

Deploy independence, and it is worth 52×: twelve independent services ship 624 times a year against 12 in lockstep. That is the one benefit that survives the arithmetic. Independent scaling, language choice and team autonomy are either secondary or achievable without separate deployables.

How do you know whether you got it?

One question: can you deploy one of them, on a Friday, without asking anyone? If not, you have a distributed monolith — the 182-hour tax per service, the availability compounding, and a monolith's release cadence. That is strictly worse than either endpoint: 2,184 hours of tax for twelve releases a year.

What is the recommendation, then?

Modular monolith first, split the modules that need independent deploy, and stop. A modular monolith gets the same six boundaries as a service-per-team split for zero extra tax and no availability compounding — so the question becomes whether deploy independence is worth 1,092 hours a year. Releasing monthly and wanting weekly: clear yes. Already weekly with review as the bottleneck: you are buying nothing.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. A service costs 182 hours a year to exist, whatever its size.
  2. Twelve services is 1.4 FTEs of pure tax.
  3. There is no cost optimum above one service at normal volumes.
  4. Volume moves it, not size: 5 services at 1,200 changes a year.
  5. Two synchronous hops and availability is already worse.
  6. Twelve hops needs 10× the monolith's reliability to break even.
  7. Reduce hops, not failure rates.
  8. Deploy independence is worth 52×. It is the only surviving benefit.
  9. Can you deploy one, on a Friday, alone? If not, you bought nothing.
  10. Modular monolith gets the same boundaries for 1,092 fewer hours.

About the tax figure

The 182 hours is eight line items, all visible in the harness and adjustable. The conclusion is not sensitive to the total: at any figure above about 60 hours, the cost curve has no interior minimum at 300 changes a year.

Where this contradicts common advice

Splitting is routinely justified on cost, scaling and resilience. Two of those three are measurably backwards here. The one real benefit — cadence — is also the one most often not achieved, which is what makes the distributed monolith so common.
