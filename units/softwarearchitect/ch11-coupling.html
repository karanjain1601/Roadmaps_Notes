Coupling and Conway's law

Below a 16% co-change rate, splitting two modules pays. Above it, a service boundary between things that change together costs more than the coupling did — and co-change is measurable from history without looking at a diagram.

Conway's law is a counting argument

An interface between two components requires the two owning teams to agree. The number of agreements a team can sustain is bounded — so the number of interfaces is bounded by the organisation, not by the design.

What Conway actually predicts

Not that the system mirrors the org chart. That the system cannot have more coordination surfaces than the organisation can sustain — a ceiling, which the design gets pushed under whether anyone decides to or not.

At 32 teams the gap is 6.2-fold: 496 possible channels against 80 sustainable ones. Something has to give, and it will be the architecture rather than the org chart.

Below about six teams the organisation is not the constraint at all, which is why Conway's law feels like folklore in a small company and like a physical law in a large one.

Coupling is measurable

Two modules that change together are coupled, regardless of what the diagram says. That is recoverable from commit history: of the commits touching A, what fraction also touch B — taking the weaker direction.

A 125-fold spread, and the four semantically coupled pairs are the top four measured — recovered without being told.

But not in the same order as their true rates

cart and orders are 75% coupled in the underlying model and measure 51.6%, while catalog and search are 55% coupled and measure 69.0%. The weaker direction is what counts: orders is touched by three coupled pairs, so only 52% of its commits touch cart even though 75% of cart's touch orders.

So co-change ranks by commit traffic, not by semantics. It is still the right measurement — but a hub module will look less coupled to each of its neighbours than it is.

The diagnostic value is that this disagrees with diagrams routinely. Two modules in different services with no declared dependency can co-change 60% of the time. The deployment boundary did not make them independent; it made their dependency invisible.

Superlinear, because the work scales with teams and the coordination scales with pairs of teams. That is chapter 2's n² term arriving in the delivery process.

Which reframes the goal

The objective is not fewer teams, or smaller backlog items. It is changes that span fewer teams — a statement about where the boundaries go, not about how work is chopped up.

When reducing coupling increases cost

Splitting genuinely helps the changes that don't span the boundary — smaller codebase, independent deploy, faster tests. It genuinely hurts the ones that do. So there is a crossover.

The threshold is a 15.8% co-change rate.

At 63% co-change, keeping them together saves 861 hours a year. And all four of the genuinely coupled pairs in the measured history sit above the threshold — so splitting any of them on the strength of a domain diagram would have been a loss.

So "reduce coupling" is not a general instruction

Measure co-change first, then put the boundary where the rate is low. Drawing boundaries from a domain model and hoping is how you get a distributed monolith — a system with all the coordination cost of separate services and none of the independence.

The diagnostic is one sentence: if a service boundary is crossed by most changes, it is not a boundary. It is a network call in the middle of a module.

Not all coupling is equal

Six kinds, priced out of nine
KindCostWhy
Shared database table9Any schema change is everyone's schema change
Shared deployment7Their bug blocks your release
Synchronous call6Their latency and their downtime are yours
Shared understanding4Invisible, and the hardest to remove
Shared library, versioned3You can lag; the lag is the cost
Asynchronous event2You are coupled to the event shape only
You cannot remove coupling — only change its kind

Moving from a shared table to an event takes the cost from 9 to 2, a 4.5-fold reduction, without making the two things independent. They still change together; they now change together at a much lower price.

That is usually the available move when the co-change rate is too high to split and too high to ignore.

The row nobody diagrams is shared understanding. Two teams that both believe an order has exactly one shipment are coupled by that belief, and no interface change removes it — which is why it sits mid-cost and is the hardest of the six to find.

The inverse manoeuvre

If Conway's law is a ceiling, you can move the ceiling: change the team structure to get the architecture you want. It works, and it is expensive in a way that is worth stating.

The cheaper version

Change ownership without changing reporting lines. Who is accountable for a component is a smaller lever than who sits with whom, it is reversible, and it moves the co-change pattern because it moves who is allowed to change what.

Check yourself

What does Conway's law actually predict?

A ceiling, not a mirror. An interface needs the two owning teams to agree, and the agreements a team can sustain are bounded — so the number of coordination surfaces is bounded by the organisation. At 32 teams there are 496 possible channels against about 80 sustainable ones, a 6.2-fold gap. Something gives, and it is the architecture rather than the org chart. Below six teams the organisation is not the constraint at all, which is why the law feels like folklore in small companies.

How do you measure coupling?

Co-change, from commit history: of the commits touching A, what fraction also touch B, taking the weaker direction. On 4,000 simulated commits the four genuinely coupled pairs were the top four measured — recovered without being told — with a 125-fold spread from strongest to weakest. It needs no reference to the architecture diagram, which is the point.

Does co-change rank pairs by how coupled they really are?

Not exactly. cart and orders are 75% coupled in the model and measure 51.6%, while catalog and search are 55% coupled and measure 69.0% — because the weaker direction counts and orders is touched by three coupled pairs. So a hub module looks less coupled to each neighbour than it is. Still the right measurement; worth knowing the bias.

What does a change spanning several teams cost?

Superlinearly more. At 8 hours per team plus 3 hours per pair of teams: a 3-team change is 33 hours rather than 24, and an 8-team change is 148 hours — 18.5× a one-team change and 2.31× the linear cost. Work scales with teams; coordination scales with pairs. So the goal is changes that span fewer teams, which is about boundary placement rather than backlog grooming.

Is reducing coupling always right?

No. Splitting helps changes that don't cross the boundary and hurts the ones that do, so there is a crossover at a 15.8% co-change rate. At 63% co-change, keeping two things together saves 861 hours a year — and all four genuinely coupled pairs in the measured history sit above the threshold, so splitting any of them from a domain diagram would have been a loss.

What is the one-sentence diagnostic for a bad boundary?

If a service boundary is crossed by most changes, it is not a boundary — it is a network call in the middle of a module. That is the distributed monolith: all the coordination cost of separate services and none of the independence. The fix upstream is to measure co-change before drawing the boundary rather than after.

If you cannot split, what can you do?

Change the kind of coupling. You cannot remove it, but moving from a shared database table to an asynchronous event takes the cost from 9 to 2 — a 4.5-fold reduction — without making the two things independent. They still change together, at a much lower price. And watch for shared understanding: two teams that both believe an order has one shipment are coupled by that belief, and no interface change removes it.

About the history

The 4,000-commit history is simulated with four hidden coupled pairs, so the measurement can be checked against a known truth. The specific rates move by a few points with the random sample — the weakest pair especially, since it rests on a handful of commits, which is why the 125-fold spread is the least trustworthy figure here. What holds across samples is that co-change recovers which pairs are coupled, and ranks them by commit traffic rather than semantics.
