The work that is not deciding

One architectural decision costs about seventy hours. One of them is choosing. The other sixty-nine are understanding, gathering, writing and persuading — which is why the job description looks nothing like the job title.

And the arithmetic does not close

Twenty-three decisions at seventy hours is 1,610 hours — 101% of a working year. The decisions from chapter 1 consume the entire year on their own, leaving nothing for the influence work that chapter 1 identified as the actual leverage.

Delegating 60% of the surrounding work — the gathering and the socialising, keeping the judgement — frees 804 hours. That is not a nicety. It is the only way the year adds up.

Availability is a queue

Chapter 1 said the leverage is in influence. Influence requires being asked. Being asked requires being available — and availability behaves like every other queue in these units.

The tension this creates

Influence requires low utilisation. Low utilisation looks like slack to anyone reading a calendar, and the natural response to visible slack is to fill it.

What is being protected is the ability to answer today. A question answered in an hour changes a decision; the same answer in three days arrives after the code is written, at which point it is a review comment rather than an input.

Understanding the problem

Twenty hours of the seventy go into understanding what is actually being asked for. It is the largest single phase, and it has by far the best return of anything in this chapter.

Why the bar is so much lower than design review's

The Code Review unit put design review's breakeven at 36.8%, because a design review costs a meaningful fraction of the work it protects. Elicitation costs 20 hours against 400 — so the bar is one in fourteen, which almost everything clears.

The practical rule: if there is any real chance you have the problem wrong, ask. That is a different and much cheaper question than whether you have the approach wrong.

An eleven-month half-life. Past about a year, a document nobody updates is worse than no document, because readers cannot tell which half is wrong and will act on the wrong half with confidence.

One kind of document does not decay

A description of current state — how the system works, what talks to what — decays at the rate above. A record of a decision and its reasons does not, because the decision was made at a moment and that remains permanently true.

"We chose Postgres in March 2023 because we needed transactions across these four tables and the team had operated it before" is as accurate in 2030 as the day it was written, even if the system has since moved off Postgres — at which point it is more valuable, not less.

So the four hours of writing in the seventy should go into the second kind. Chapter 9 is about what a decision record has to contain to survive; the point here is only that it is the one artefact whose value does not evaporate.

Check yourself

How much of an architectural decision is deciding?

1.4% — one hour of seventy. Twenty hours go into understanding the problem, fourteen into gathering constraints, fifteen into socialising the result, eight into revisiting it under challenge. Forty-two hours happen before the choice and twenty-seven after, either side of a moment that takes an hour. That is why the job description looks nothing like the job title.

Can one person do twenty-three decisions a year?

Not and do anything else. Twenty-three at seventy hours is 1,610 hours — 101% of a working year, leaving nothing for the influence work chapter 1 identified as the leverage. Delegating 60% of the surrounding work frees 804 hours. Delegation here is not politeness; it is the only way the arithmetic closes.

Which parts should be delegated?

The gathering and the socialising — 34 hours that any competent person can do. Not the choosing, which is the one hour drawing on the judgement the role exists for. Handing someone the decision while keeping the constraint-gathering is exactly inverted, and it feels like good delegation while being the opposite.

Why does a busy architect become unavailable so suddenly?

Because availability is a queue. At a capacity of twelve questions a day, six arriving gives a 1.33-hour wait and eleven and a half gives sixteen hours — a twelvefold increase in wait from a 1.9-fold increase in load. An architect who is busy is not slightly less available; they are on the vertical part of a hyperbola.

What does that imply about a calendar with gaps in it?

That the gaps are the product. Influence requires being asked, being asked requires being available, and availability requires low utilisation — which looks exactly like slack to anyone reading the calendar. What is being protected is the ability to answer today: an answer in an hour changes a decision, and the same answer in three days arrives after the code is written.

When is elicitation worth doing?

Almost always — the breakeven is a 7.1% chance of having the problem wrong. That is far below the Code Review unit's 36.8% breakeven for design review, and for a simple reason: elicitation costs 20 hours against a 400-hour build, where a design review costs a meaningful fraction of the work it protects. If there is any real chance you have the problem wrong, ask.

Which documents are worth writing?

The ones recording a decision and its reasons. A description of current state has an eleven-month half-life — 48% accurate at a year, 11% at three — and past about a year is worse than nothing, because readers cannot tell which half is wrong. A decision record does not decay: the decision was made at a moment and that stays true. When the system later moves off the choice, the record becomes more valuable, not less.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Choosing is 1.4% of a decision — one hour of seventy.
  2. 42 hours before the choice, 27 after.
  3. 23 decisions is 101% of the year. The arithmetic does not close.
  4. Delegate the gathering and socialising, never the judgement.
  5. Availability is a queue: 1.9× the load, 12× the wait.
  6. Low utilisation is the product, and it looks like slack.
  7. Protect the ability to answer today.
  8. Elicitation's breakeven is 7.1% — one in fourteen clears it.
  9. Current-state documents have an eleven-month half-life.
  10. Decision records do not decay. Write those.

About the phase breakdown

The seventy hours is a model with every phase exposed in the demo, so you can substitute your own. The conclusion that survives any plausible substitution is the ratio: choosing is a small single-digit percentage however you weight the rest.

The recurring shape

1/(μ − λ) again, this time governing an architect's availability rather than review latency. It is the same curve the Code Review unit hit four times, and the same conclusion: intuition tracks utilisation, outcomes track the reciprocal of what is left.
