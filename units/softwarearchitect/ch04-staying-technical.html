But you are judged on the fast-decaying half

Weight the six kinds by what decisions actually need and after two years you are at 81%. Weight them by what people notice — which framework, which bundler, which deployment tool — and you are at 58%.

A twenty-four point gap, opening in the direction that hurts. An architect can be making good calls while visibly not knowing what everyone is using this year.

Why staleness compounds

Chapter 1 put the leverage in influence and chapter 3 put influence behind availability. There is a third gate: people bring problems to someone they believe is current.

A 43% loss of influence with no change in judgement quality.

And it is self-reinforcing: stale → asked less → fewer problems seen → staler. The loop runs on perception, which is why arguing that your decisions are still sound does not interrupt it.

How much code to write

Two forces pull opposite ways. Writing no code lets currency decay and the credibility loop close. Writing a lot of code leaves no hours for the influence that is the leverage. So there is an interior maximum, and it is findable.

Both directions cost, and the peak is flat

Zero coding loses 12% against the peak; half your time coding loses 21%. The optimum sits at 19% — just under a day a week, or one real piece of work each quarter.

The peak being flat is itself useful: anything between about 10% and 30% is within four percent of optimal, so the precise number does not matter. Zero and half both do.

And the optimum moves sharply with team size: 80% at ten engineers, 19% at sixty, 15% at three hundred. Consistent with chapter 1 — below the 23-engineer crossover the architect should mostly be building, and above it code becomes a currency-maintenance activity rather than an output.

The underrated one is being on call

It is the only substitute that refreshes operational knowledge, which the decision weighting puts at 20% — four times the weight of framework specifics. It also supplies something no amount of reading does: the felt cost of decisions you made two years ago.

The fifth deserves care because the instinct behind it is right. An architect who wants to stay current reaches for real, meaningful work — and real, meaningful work is usually on the critical path, where being interrupted for the twenty-two hours of influence work makes them the thing everyone is waiting for. The version that works is real work that is genuinely not urgent.

Check yourself

Which technical knowledge decays fastest?

Framework specifics, with a 15-month half-life — 19% survives three years. Then tooling (23 months), operational practice (38), language semantics (86), protocols (173), and fundamentals which do not decay at all. So an architect three years out of the code has lost most of what they knew about frameworks and almost none of what they knew about distributed systems.

Does that matter much?

Less than it looks, for the decisions — and more than it should, for the perception. Weighted by what decisions need, two years away leaves you at 81%; weighted by what people notice, at 58%. A twenty-four point gap in the wrong direction: you are judged on the knowledge that decays fastest and matters least.

Why does staleness compound?

Because people bring problems to someone they believe is current. Stale → asked less → fewer problems seen → staler. Modelled, three years away costs 43% of influence with no change in judgement quality. The loop runs on perception, which is why arguing that your decisions are still sound does not interrupt it.

How much code should an architect write?

About 19% of the year at sixty engineers — just under a day a week, or one real piece of work per quarter. Zero sits 12% below the peak and half sits 21% below, so the curve has a genuine interior maximum. The peak is flat: anything from roughly 10% to 30% is within four percent of optimal.

Does that number depend on team size?

Sharply — 80% at ten engineers, 19% at sixty, 15% at three hundred. That is consistent with chapter 1's crossover: below about 23 engineers the architect should mostly be building, and above it code becomes a currency-maintenance activity rather than an output. One title, a 5.3-fold difference in the right answer.

What substitutes for writing code?

Only shipping a small real feature comes close, at 95%. Pairing is 70%, being on call 60%, close code review 45%, a side project 35% — and reading release notes is 15% against conference talks at 10%. A 6.3-fold spread between the best substitute and the most commonly used one, because the cheap options give you the names of things without the experience of fighting them.

What is the most underrated way to stay current?

Being on call. It is the only substitute that refreshes operational knowledge — weighted at 20% for decisions, four times framework specifics — and it supplies something no reading does: the felt cost of decisions you made two years ago. The thing that degrades first and matters most is knowing what it costs to change something, and every estimate you give depends on it.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Framework knowledge has a 15-month half-life; fundamentals have none.
  2. Two years away: 81% decision quality, 58% perceived currency.

  6. The optimum is 19% of the year coding — just under a day a week.

About the two weightings

The decision weighting and the credibility weighting are both assumptions, and the chapter's central claim is the gap between them rather than either set of numbers. Both are visible in the demo so you can substitute your own and see whether the gap survives.

The shape of Part 1

Four chapters, one argument: the leverage is in influence, influence needs capacity, capacity needs delegation, and influence needs credibility, which needs code. Each chapter's constraint is created by the previous one's conclusion.
