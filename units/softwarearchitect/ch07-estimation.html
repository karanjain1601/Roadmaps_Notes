Estimation and how wrong you are

A "plus or minus 20 percent" estimate contains the outcome 28.7% of the time. A range that means anything is roughly half to double — and it looks uselessly wide, which is why nobody gives one.

The error is not symmetric

A task cannot take negative time. The downside is bounded at zero and the upside is not, so every duration distribution is right-skewed and every symmetric range is wrong.

Which estimate are you quoting?

Quote the median and you are late half the time. Quote the mean and you are late 39% of the time and 17% over on average. Neither is wrong; they are different promises, and most estimates do not say which one they are.

What a range actually claims

A symmetric 90% range works out at 1/2.5× to 2.5×. The ranges people actually give hold less than a third of the time — so "three to four weeks" is a 30% claim dressed as a certainty.

State it as two numbers, not a band

"Usually three weeks, occasionally seven." A median and a p90 are honest, easy to say, and immediately understood — where "1/2.5× to 2.5×" is accurate and gets you removed from the meeting.

It also answers the question the asker actually has, which is almost never "what is the range" but "what do I plan around, and what would surprise you".

Where the buffer goes

The instinct is to pad each estimate. Simulated, that is the expensive option — because variances root-sum rather than add.

Padding every task costs 39% more for the same confidence.

Buffering each task pays for every task's worst case simultaneously, which never happens. One buffer at the end covers the same risk for considerably less — and the gap widens as the project grows.

The practical form

Estimate each task at its median, then add one project buffer sized to the whole. Per-task padding is the most common estimating habit in software and it is the one that costs.

When breaking it down does not help

The root-sum benefit above assumes the errors are independent. They usually are not.

A 30% worse tail from exactly the same tasks. And at full correlation, decomposition buys nothing — nine tasks have the same relative uncertainty as one.

Which is bad news specifically for architects

Errors correlate when they come from a shared assumption: the domain, the team's speed, the platform, the requirements. Those are exactly the things an architect is most likely to have wrong.

So architectural estimates are the least helped by decomposition. If all nine tasks are wrong because you misunderstood the domain, breaking the work down finer does not reduce the error — it gives you nine confidently wrong numbers instead of one.

What does work

Take the estimate the team produces, then multiply it by the median of what this team has historically done to estimates of this kind.

Why reference class beats every decomposition technique

It corrects for the correlated errors decomposition cannot see, because it measures the outcome rather than reasoning about the parts. You do not need to know why the last twelve projects ran 1.7× over to apply the correction.

And there is a piece of good news hidden in chapter 2. That chapter measured twelve feedback cycles in an enterprise architecture career — which sounds hopeless for building judgement. A reference class of twelve is exactly what this method needs, and it is enough.

Check yourself

Why is estimate error asymmetric?

A task cannot take negative time, so the downside is bounded at zero and the upside is not. Simulated: p10 is 0.49× the estimate and p90 is 2.03× — an upside of +1.03 against a downside of −0.51, a twofold asymmetry. That is why the mean (1.17×) sits above the median (1.00×) even when the median is exactly right.

What does "plus or minus 20 percent" actually claim?

That the outcome will land in a band which contains it 28.7% of the time. A symmetric 90% range is roughly 1/2.5× to 2.5×. So "three to four weeks" is a 30% claim presented as a near-certainty, and the honest alternative is two numbers — "usually three weeks, occasionally seven" — which is also what the asker actually wanted.

Median or mean — which should you quote?

Either, but say which. The median means you are late half the time; the mean (1.17× here) means late 39% of the time and 17% over on average. They are different promises, and most estimates do not state which one is being made — which is where the disagreement afterwards comes from.

Should you add a buffer to each task?

No — it costs 39% more for the same confidence at nine tasks, and 51% more at twenty-five. Buffering each task pays for every task's worst case simultaneously, which never happens; variances root-sum rather than add. Estimate each task at its median, then add one project buffer sized to the whole.

Does breaking work down reduce uncertainty?

Only if the errors are independent. Nine tasks with independent errors have a p90 of 1.47× the naive sum; at 70% correlation it is 1.91×, and at full correlation 2.03× — the same as one task, meaning decomposition bought nothing at all.

Why is that particularly bad for architects?

Because errors correlate when they share a cause — the domain, the team's speed, the platform, the requirements — and those are exactly what an architect is most likely to have wrong. Architectural estimates are therefore the least helped by decomposition: if all nine tasks are wrong because the domain was misunderstood, finer breakdown gives you nine confidently wrong numbers instead of one.

What actually improves an estimate?

Reference class forecasting: multiply the team's estimate by the median of what they have historically done to estimates of this kind. Twelve past projects here ran a median 1.70× over with only 1 of 12 under — applying that to a 90-hour estimate gives 153 hours. It works because it measures the outcome rather than reasoning about parts, so it corrects for the correlated errors decomposition cannot see. And twelve data points is enough, which is convenient given chapter 2's count.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Duration error is right-skewed. p10 is 0.49×, p90 is 2.03×.
  2. You can overrun twice as far as you can underrun.
  3. Plus or minus 20% holds 28.7% of the time.
  4. A 90% range is 1/2.5× to 2.5×. Give a median and a p90 instead.
  5. Say which estimate you are quoting — median or mean is a different promise.
  6. Padding every task costs 39% more for the same confidence.
  7. One project buffer, medians underneath.
  8. Decomposition only helps if errors are independent.
  9. At full correlation, nine tasks are one task.
  10. Reference class beats every technique. Twelve data points is enough.

About the simulation

Two hundred thousand samples from a lognormal with σ = 0.55, and forty thousand project simulations per configuration. The quantiles, the range coverages and the buffer comparison are all outputs of running it. σ is a slider in the first demo because it is the assumption worth arguing with.

The demos re-run the same simulation with a smaller sample so they stay responsive, so their figures sit about a percent from the ones quoted above — Monte Carlo noise, not a disagreement. If a number here mattered to a percent, none of these conclusions would be safe to state.

The connection to chapter 2

Twelve feedback cycles sounded like bad news for building judgement. For reference-class forecasting it is a sufficient sample — the one place where the enterprise architect's data poverty turns out not to bite.
