Choosing Redis

Redis is the default answer to so many questions that the interesting exercise is finding the ones where it is wrong. Plus the thing every other chapter has been quietly assuming: which fork you are running, and whether that matters.

Where it clearly wins

Obvious fits

  • Caching — the canonical use, and it is very good at it (chapter 7)
  • Session storage — small, hot, expiring, mostly reconstructible
  • Rate limiting — atomic counters with TTLs are exactly the primitive (chapter 10)
  • Leaderboards and rankings — sorted sets have no real equivalent elsewhere (chapter 5)
  • Real-time counters — INCR at a rate no relational database will match
  • Job queues — streams give acknowledgement and reclaim (chapter 6)
  • Ephemeral coordination — locks, semaphores, once-only flags (chapter 9)
  • Presence and live state — pub/sub fan-out (chapter 11)

Frequently a mistake

  • A primary datastore — no durability guarantee, no PITR, no schema (chapter 13)
  • Anything financial — see above, twice
  • Ad-hoc querying — you can only find what you can name (chapter 2)
  • Large cold data — paying RAM prices for disk-shaped data
  • A message bus across teams — Kafka or NATS, not this
  • Full-text search — without a module you will build a bad one
  • Analytics over history — no aggregation, no joins, no columnar storage
  • Multi-key transactions at scale (chapters 8 and 16)
The question that decides it

If this data vanished, what would happen? If the answer is "we rebuild it from the database and serve some slow requests meanwhile", Redis is an excellent choice and most of this unit's warnings are things to be aware of rather than problems to solve. If the answer is "we have lost something we cannot recover", you are relying on a guarantee Redis does not offer — and the fix is not tuning appendfsync, it is putting the durable record somewhere durable and letting Redis hold a derived copy. That one question resolves most arguments about whether Redis is a "real database". It is a very good tool for data whose loss is survivable, which is an enormous amount of data.

The licence, and the fork

what happened, and whether it affects you
2009-2024   Redis is BSD 3-clause. Genuinely permissive open source.

Mar 2024    Redis 7.4 moves to dual RSALv2 / SSPLv1. Neither is OSI-approved.
            The restriction targets offering Redis itself as a managed
            service. The change is NOT retroactive — 7.2.4 and everything
            before it stay BSD.

Apr 2024    The Linux Foundation forks 7.2.4 as VALKEY, BSD-licensed, backed
            by AWS, Google, Oracle and Ericsson. Much of the contributor base
            moves across. Fedora, Debian and Ubuntu switch their default
            packages, because they cannot ship source-available software in
            their main repositories — which mattered more than any commercial
            restriction did.

Nov 2024    Salvatore Sanfilippo, Redis's original author, rejoins.

May 2025    Redis 8 adds AGPLv3 as a third option. Redis is open source again
            by the OSI definition — under copyleft rather than the permissive
            terms it left. Redis 8 also folds the former Stack modules
            (search, JSON, time series, bloom) into the core distribution.

Mid-2026    Both projects are healthy. Redis is on its 8.x line; Valkey is on
            9.x. AWS ElastiCache and Google Memorystore default new instances
            to Valkey.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU — for most readers, nothing. Running Redis inside
your own application as a cache, queue or session store was permitted
throughout and remains so under all three current options.

IT MATTERS IF:
  * you offer Redis itself as a service to third parties
  * your policy forbids non-OSI licences, or forbids copyleft
  * you SHIP Redis inside a distributed product, where AGPL's network
    copyleft is a genuinely different proposition from BSD

ONE PRACTICAL MIGRATION NOTE: Valkey forked from 7.2.4, so file-level
migration works cleanly from Redis 7.2 and earlier. From 7.4 onwards the
RDB format has diverged and you need a logical or replication-based path.
Everything in this unit applies to both — the wire protocol, the commands,
the data structures and the operational behaviour are the same.
Everything in this unit applies to Valkey

The two projects have diverged in emphasis rather than in fundamentals. Valkey has pushed on performance — more aggressive multi-threading of I/O and memory-efficiency work — while Redis 8 brought the previously separate modules into the core distribution and posted substantial throughput gains of its own. For the material in these eighteen chapters they are interchangeable: the same commands, the same single-threaded execution model, the same replication semantics, the same eviction policies, the same cluster design. Choose on licence policy, on what your managed provider defaults to, and on whether you need a specific module — not on any expectation that one of them behaves differently in the ways this unit describes.

Signals you chose wrong

Worth checking against your own system. You are writing key-scanning code to find things you cannot address directly. Your Lua scripts are getting long, because business logic is migrating into the database to buy atomicity. You are afraid to restart it, which means it holds state nothing else does. You are reconstructing relationships across several keys in application code. Your `maxmemory` keeps going up and you cannot say what would let it come down. You have discussed making it durable more than once. Any one of these is survivable; three of them together means the data has outgrown the tool, and the honest move is to give it a real home and let Redis go back to being a cache in front of it.

Where the unit ends up

Almost everything in these eighteen chapters follows from the two facts in chapter 1.

It keeps everything in memory
Which is why it is fast, why maxmemory and eviction are the central operational concerns (chapter 12), why persistence is a bolt-on with a fork-shaped cost (chapter 13), why the working set fitting in RAM is a hard prerequisite, and why "large cold data" is the wrong shape for it.
It executes commands on one thread
Which is why every single command is atomic for free (chapters 3 and 8), why KEYS and a big HGETALL are dangerous rather than merely slow (chapter 14), why a Lua script blocks everyone (chapter 8), why a hot key cannot be sharded (chapter 16), and why running several instances is the honest way to use more cores.

The structures in chapters 3 to 6 are what make it a data-structure server rather than a key-value store, and the patterns in chapters 7 to 11 are almost all applications of one structure plus atomicity. The operational chapters are mostly consequences: asynchronous replication (chapter 15) is the price of not waiting, hash slots (chapter 16) are the price of distributing a keyspace whose commands assume co-location, and the security defaults (chapter 17) are the price of a tool designed for a trusted network in 2009.

What remains, when all of that is accounted for, is a genuinely excellent piece of software with an unusually clear set of things it will not do for you. It will not guarantee your write survived. It will not let you query what you did not plan to look up. It will not use your other fifteen cores. None of those are flaws to be worked around — they are the shape of the tool, and every serious Redis problem in this unit came from someone expecting otherwise.

The short version

  1. Ask what happens if the data vanishes. "We rebuild it" means Redis fits; "we lost it" means it does not.
  2. The working set must fit in RAM affordably, and you must be able to read it by a key you already know.
  3. Redis and Valkey are interchangeable for everything in this unit. Choose on licence policy and your provider's default.
  4. Redis 8 is AGPLv3, so it is open source again — under copyleft, not the BSD terms it left.
  5. Valkey forked from 7.2.4, so file-level migration works from 7.2 and earlier and not from 7.4 onwards.
  6. Memcached if you only need a cache; Kafka or NATS for messaging; Postgres if you need durability with speed.
  7. Long Lua scripts, key-scanning code and fear of restarting are the signs the data has outgrown the tool.
  8. Everything here follows from in-memory and single-threaded. The limits are the shape of the tool, not flaws in it.
