CHAPTER 07

Metadata, SEO and sharing

The third audience from Chapter 4: machines. Crawlers, preview bots, reader modes and increasingly AI agents all consume your page without rendering it for a human. This chapter is what they read, how they decide what your page is about, and the specific things that go wrong when the content only exists after JavaScript runs.

Part 2 Markup and meaning·Reading time 45 minutes·Demos preview simulator, JSON-LD builder·Prerequisites Chapters 4–6

1How a crawler sees your page

Four stages, one of them optional and slow. Knowing which is which explains most SEO advice.

Search engines run a pipeline that is worth knowing precisely, because front-end decisions land in different stages and have very different consequences depending on where.

#StageWhat happensYour leverage
1DiscoveryURLs are found from links, sitemaps and previous crawls, and queued.Internal linking, sitemap, no orphan pages
2CrawlThe raw HTML is fetched, subject to robots.txt and a crawl budget per site.Server speed, correct status codes, not blocking assets
3RenderThe page is queued for a headless browser that runs JavaScript. This queue can lag by days and does not always complete.Whether your content needs this stage at all
4IndexThe rendered content is parsed, canonicalised, de-duplicated and stored.Title, headings, structured data, canonical tags

Stage 3 is the one that matters to front-end architecture. Google does execute JavaScript, and has for years — but it does so in a second pass, on its own schedule, with a budget. Content that exists only after a client-side fetch may be indexed hours or weeks later than the HTML, or not at all if the render times out. Other crawlers are worse: many social preview bots, several search engines, and most link-unfurlers do not run JavaScript at all and see only your initial HTML response.

The practical rule

Anything that must be indexed or previewed should be present in the HTML that arrives from the server. That is not an argument against single-page apps — it is an argument for server rendering or pre-rendering the routes that need to be found, which Chapter 21 covers as a per-route decision rather than a project-wide one.

Bots you are actually serving

Search crawlers
Googlebot, Bingbot, and regional engines. Googlebot runs a recent Chromium; Bingbot renders too, though less reliably.
Social preview bots
Slack, Discord, WhatsApp, X, LinkedIn, Signal, iMessage. Almost none run JavaScript. Many have a short timeout and a small download limit, so a heavy page returns no preview at all.
Reader modes and clippers
Safari Reader, Firefox Reader View, Pocket, read-it-later apps. They apply heuristics over your semantic markup to decide what is article and what is chrome — another dividend of Chapter 4.
AI crawlers and agents
A growing category that reads your HTML directly. They benefit from the same things everything else does: clean semantics, real text, structured data. Whether to allow them is a policy decision expressed in robots.txt.

2The head tags that matter

Six that earn their place, and a long tail of ones that do not.

<title>
The single most important on-page signal, and the clickable headline in results. Aim for 50–60 characters; Google truncates by pixel width around 580–600px, not by character count. Front-load the distinctive part and keep the brand at the end. Unique on every page — duplicate titles across a site are a real ranking problem, not a cosmetic one.
<meta name="description">
Not a ranking factor, but it is often the snippet, and the snippet decides clicks. 140–160 characters. Search engines frequently rewrite it to match the query, which is fine — write it for the case where they do not. One per page, unique, written as a sentence rather than a keyword list.
<link rel="canonical">
Declares the preferred URL when the same content is reachable at several. Solves tracking parameters, session ids, filter and sort permutations, and http/https or www duplicates. Must be an absolute URL. A canonical is a strong hint, not an order — conflicting signals lose to actual redirects.
<meta name="robots">
Per-page indexing control: noindex (do not list this page), nofollow (do not follow its links), noarchive, max-snippet, max-image-preview:large. The X-Robots-Tag HTTP header does the same for non-HTML files such as PDFs.
lang and hreflang
lang on <html> for the page's language. For multilingual sites, a set of <link rel="alternate" hreflang="…"> tags mapping each language or region to its URL — including a self-reference and an x-default. They must be reciprocal on every page or they are ignored.
<meta name="viewport">
Covered in Chapter 4. Mobile-friendliness is a ranking factor and indexing is mobile-first, so the mobile rendering of your page is the indexed version.
Tags that do nothing

<meta name="keywords"> has been ignored by every major search engine for over fifteen years. <meta name="author">, revisit-after, distribution and rating have no effect on ranking. Any tool or template that generates them is padding your head. Delete them.

3URLs and structure

The part front-end developers usually inherit and can still improve.

PreferWhy
/guides/responsive-imagesReadable, memorable, describes the content, survives being pasted into a chat
Hyphens, not underscoresHyphens are treated as word separators; underscores historically were not
Lower case, consistentlyPaths are case-sensitive on most servers, so /Guides and /guides are two URLs and two index entries
One trailing-slash conventionPick with or without, redirect the other, apply it everywhere. Otherwise every page has a duplicate
Shallow pathsDepth is a weak signal, but deep paths usually indicate deep click distance, which is a real one
Stable URLsChanging them costs accumulated links and rankings. When you must, redirect with 301
Query parameters
Filters and sorts multiply into thousands of near-identical URLs and burn crawl budget. Canonical them to the unfiltered page, or block the parameter patterns in robots.txt, or use noindex on the combinations that have no independent value.
Pagination
rel="next" and rel="prev" are no longer used by Google. What works now: make every page linkable and self-canonical, ensure each has genuinely distinct content, and provide a route to deep pages that is not fifty clicks long.
Internal linking
The strongest structural lever you actually control. Link with descriptive text — "responsive images guide", never "click here" or "read more", which is bad for both crawlers and the screen-reader link list from Chapter 6. Every page should be reachable within about three clicks of the home page.
Breadcrumbs
Useful navigation and eligible for a rich result. Mark them up as a BreadcrumbList and put them in a <nav aria-label="Breadcrumb"> with an ordered list.
rel on outbound links
nofollow for links you do not vouch for, sponsored for paid, ugc for user-generated. And always rel="noopener" with target="_blank" — modern browsers imply it, but older ones let the opened page control yours via window.opener.

4Structured data

Telling machines what your content is, in a vocabulary they already have.

Semantic HTML says "this is a heading, this is a list". Structured data says "this is a recipe with a 25-minute cook time and a 4.6 rating from 812 people". It is what makes rich results — star ratings, FAQ accordions, event dates, product prices — possible in search listings.

Three syntaxes exist; use JSON-LD. It sits in a single script block, is decoupled from your markup so a design change cannot break it, and is what Google explicitly recommends. Microdata and RDFa interleave attributes with your HTML and are much harder to keep correct.

JSON-LD for an article HTML head or body
<script type="application/ld+json">
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Article",
  "headline": "How responsive images actually work",
  "datePublished": "2026-03-14T09:00:00Z",
  "dateModified": "2026-04-02T11:20:00Z",
  "author": { "@type": "Person", "name": "R. Okonkwo" },
  "image": ["https://example.com/img/hero-1200.jpg"],
  "publisher": {
    "@type": "Organization", "name": "Northwind"
  }
}
</script>

Three rules for structured data

1. It must describe content that is actually visible on the page. Marking up reviews that a user cannot see is a policy violation and gets rich results removed manually. 2. Rich results are earned, not guaranteed — valid markup makes you eligible, nothing more. 3. Validate with Google's Rich Results Test and the Schema.org validator before shipping; a single mistyped property name silently disables the whole block.

5Social sharing

Eight tags that decide whether a shared link looks like a product or a URL.

Open Graph started at Facebook and is now the de facto standard everywhere — Slack, Discord, LinkedIn, WhatsApp, iMessage and X all read it. X also reads its own twitter: tags and falls back to Open Graph when they are absent, which means the twitter:card type is usually the only extra tag worth writing.

The complete practical set HTML head
<meta property="og:type"        content="article">
<meta property="og:url"         content="https://example.com/guides/images">
<meta property="og:title"       content="How responsive images actually work">
<meta property="og:description" content="srcset, sizes and the maths behind them.">
<meta property="og:image"       content="https://example.com/og/images.png">
<meta property="og:image:alt"   content="Diagram of a srcset candidate list">
<meta property="og:site_name"   content="Northwind">
<meta name="twitter:card"     content="summary_large_image">
og:image is the whole thing
1200 × 630 pixels, under about 5 MB, PNG or JPEG. Must be an absolute URL — a relative path silently produces no image, which is the single most common mistake. Must not require authentication. Text on the image should be large, because it is often shown at 300px wide in a chat list.
Bots cache aggressively
Once a URL has been unfurled, the preview is cached for a long time. Fixing a tag does not fix the preview already in a Slack channel. Each platform has a debugger that forces a re-fetch — use it after changes.
They do not run JavaScript
Tags injected client-side by a helmet-style library will not be seen by most preview bots. This is the most concrete reason a marketing or content route needs server rendering.
Dynamic images
Generating an OG image per page from the title is now routine — an edge function renders HTML to a PNG on demand. Cache it hard; it is expensive and never changes for a given URL.

6Sitemaps, robots and redirects

Three files and one status code that between them control what gets crawled and what gets kept.

robots.txt
At the domain root. Controls crawling, not indexing — a blocked URL can still appear in results if other sites link to it, because the crawler never fetched the page to see your noindex. That is the classic mistake: to remove a page from the index you must let it be crawled and serve noindex. Never block your CSS or JavaScript; a crawler that cannot render your page assumes the worst about it.
sitemap.xml
A list of canonical URLs with optional lastmod. Matters most for large sites, new sites and pages that are poorly linked. It is a hint about discovery, not a promise of indexing. Keep it accurate — a sitemap full of redirects and 404s is a quality signal in the wrong direction. Reference it from robots.txt.
Redirects
301 and 308 are permanent and consolidate signals onto the new URL; browsers cache them aggressively, sometimes indefinitely, so issuing one by mistake is painful to undo. 302 and 307 are temporary. Avoid chains — every hop is a round trip for users and a dilution for crawlers.
Status codes for missing content
A missing page must return 404 or 410. A "not found" message returned with 200 is a soft 404: the crawler indexes an error page as real content. Client-rendered apps produce these constantly, because the server happily returns 200 for every route and only the JavaScript knows the item does not exist.
A minimal, correct robots.txt /robots.txt
# Everything crawlable by default is usually right.
User-agent: *
Disallow: /cart/
Disallow: /account/
Disallow: /*?sort=          # filter permutations, not real pages
Allow: /

Sitemap: https://example.com/sitemap.xml

7When the page is client-rendered

Everything above assumes the HTML contains the content. Here is what changes when it does not.

A pure single-page app serves the same near-empty HTML shell for every route and fills it in with JavaScript. That is fine for an authenticated dashboard nobody should index. It is a problem for anything public, in five specific ways.

ProblemWhat happensFix
Preview bots see nothingSlack, WhatsApp and LinkedIn read the shell. No title, no image, no description.Server-render or pre-render the head at minimum
Render queue delayContent indexed days after publication, or not at all if rendering times outServer-render the routes that must be found
Soft 404sEvery route returns 200, including ones for deleted itemsReturn real status codes from the server for known-missing resources
Unstable titlesdocument.title lags the route or never updatesSet it on every navigation — also an accessibility requirement, Chapter 6
Hash routes/#/products/12 — everything after # is not sent to the server and is not a distinct URL to a crawlerUse the History API and real paths
The route-level decision

You do not have to choose one rendering mode for a whole application. Marketing pages, blog posts, product and category pages: statically generated or server-rendered, because they need to be found and shared. The logged-in application behind them: client-rendered, with noindex, because nobody should index it and the flexibility is worth more. Chapter 21 works through the options and their costs.

What to do on every client-side navigation

  1. Update document.title.
  2. Update the canonical link and, if you server-render, the Open Graph tags.
  3. Move focus to the new view's heading, and announce the page name politely.
  4. Reset scroll to the top — unless the user is going back, in which case restore their previous position.
  5. Set noindex only where you mean it, and never leave a staging noindex on a production deploy. This is the single most expensive one-line mistake in web publishing.

The dozens of icon sizes
Old generators emit twenty files for platforms that no longer exist. The four lines above cover every current browser and OS. Chapter 28 covers the manifest properly.
color-scheme
Tells the browser your page supports both schemes, so form controls, scrollbars and the default background adapt instead of staying stubbornly light. One line, immediately visible effect.
<base href>
Changes the resolution of every relative URL on the page. Occasionally necessary, frequently the cause of a mystifying bug hours later. Prefer absolute or root-relative URLs.
Content Security Policy
Best delivered as an HTTP header, though a <meta http-equiv> works for some directives. Chapter 27.

Measuring any of this

Search Console / Bing Webmaster Tools
The only view of how a crawler actually sees you: indexing status per URL, the rendered HTML it retrieved, structured-data errors, and the queries you appear for. Free, and there is no substitute.
Rich Results Test and the Schema validator
Paste a URL or a code block and get the parsed entity with errors. Run it in CI if structured data matters to your business.
Platform debuggers
Each social platform's sharing debugger re-fetches your page and shows the parsed tags. The way to bust their cache after a fix.
Fetch it as a bot
curl -A "Mozilla/5.0 (compatible; Googlebot/2.1)" https://… and read what comes back. If your content is not in that response, no bot that skips rendering will ever see it.

9Check yourself

Five questions, closing Part 2.

1. A page should not appear in search results. Someone adds Disallow: /secret-page to robots.txt. Does that work?

No, and it can make things worse. robots.txt controls crawling, not indexing. If another site links to the URL, search engines may still list it — with no snippet, because they were forbidden from fetching it to see what it says. To remove a page you must allow crawling and serve <meta name="robots" content="noindex"> or an X-Robots-Tag header, so the crawler can read the instruction. Blocking and noindex together is self-defeating for exactly this reason.

2. Your marketing team reports that links shared in Slack show no preview at all, though the page looks perfect in a browser. First thing to check?

Whether the Open Graph tags exist in the server response, rather than being injected by JavaScript after hydration. Preview bots do not run JavaScript. Test with curl and read the raw HTML. The second most likely cause is a relative og:image URL, which is silently ignored — it must be absolute, including the scheme and host. The third is that the image is behind authentication, or is too large for the bot's download limit.

3. What is a soft 404, and why do single-page apps generate them so reliably?

A page that tells a human "not found" while returning HTTP 200 to the machine. Crawlers either index the error page as legitimate content or, when they detect the pattern, treat it as a quality problem across the site. SPAs cause it structurally: the server serves the same shell with a 200 for every path, and only the client-side router discovers, after fetching data, that the item does not exist. The fix has to be on the server — resolve the route server-side and return a real 404, which is one of the concrete benefits of the meta-framework model in Chapter 21.

4. Why is <title> length usually quoted as "about 60 characters" when search engines truncate by pixels?

Because 60 characters is a rough average that happens to land near the ~600 pixel limit for a typical mix of letters. It is only an approximation: a title of sixty capital W's is far wider than sixty i's and will be cut much earlier. The preview demo in section 5 measures actual rendered text width, which is why its cut-off point moves as you type different characters. In practice: front-load the distinctive words so truncation costs you the brand name rather than the subject.

5. You add FAQPage structured data listing ten questions, but only three are visible on the page — the rest load when the user clicks "show more" and fetches them. Is this fine?

Content inside an accordion that is present in the HTML and merely collapsed is fine — it is on the page. Content that does not exist until a network request is not, and marking it up is a structured-data policy violation that can get rich results removed from the whole site manually. The rule is that structured data must describe content actually present in the page a crawler receives. Either render all ten in the HTML and collapse them with CSS, or mark up only the three that are really there.
