Jailbreaks and safety bypass

Individual jailbreak prompts are worthless to a defender — they stop working, and the next one arrives. What transfers is the mechanism: which trained objective a family of techniques puts in tension with the safety objective. That tells you what to test and what a patch has actually fixed.

Every family is a tension between trained objectives

A model is trained to be helpful, to follow instructions, to complete patterns coherently, to be useful for creative and analytical work — and to decline certain requests. Those objectives are learned simultaneously and they conflict on some inputs. Every technique family below works by constructing an input where satisfying the safety objective requires failing a different objective the model was also trained to satisfy. That framing is what makes the taxonomy useful rather than descriptive: it explains why blocking a phrase achieves nothing (the tension remains, only the wording changed), why refusal training generalises imperfectly (the boundary is a learned surface, not a rule), and why the durable engineering answer is to stop depending on the refusal — the architecture, not the model, is where a boundary can be enforced (chapter 4).

Why patching prompts fails

the shape of the problem
THE PATCH TREADMILL, and why teams end up on it:

  a jailbreak is published
  the phrase or pattern is added to a blocklist
  a paraphrase works
  the classifier is fine-tuned on the published examples
  a structurally different input works
  ... repeat

WHAT WENT WRONG: the mitigation was fitted to SURFACE FORM, and the
vulnerability is in the RELATIONSHIP between objectives. Natural language
has unbounded ways to express any given relationship, so fitting to
observed expressions is fitting to a sample from an infinite set.

THIS IS THE SAME ERROR AS SIGNATURE-BASED MALWARE DETECTION, and it has
the same trajectory: useful against known threats, useless against
variants, and it creates a false sense of coverage that is worse than
having none — because the dashboard shows the blocklist working.

WHAT ACTUALLY MOVES THE NUMBER:

  * PROVIDER-SIDE ADVERSARIAL TRAINING against a family rather than
    examples. This does generalise, it is why models are much harder to
    jailbreak than in 2023, and it is not something you control
  * REDUCING THE STAKES so a successful bypass does not matter. If a
    jailbroken model can only produce text to the person who jailbroke
    it, you have a content-policy problem rather than a security incident
  * NARROWING THE SURFACE. A model constrained to one task, with output
    validated against a schema, has fewer usable tensions than a general
    assistant
  * DEFENCE IN DEPTH with independent controls, measured as a composite

AND THE UNCOMFORTABLE PART: for a system built on a third-party model,
most jailbreak findings are not yours to fix. You can add layers and
reduce consequences; the cause is in the model's training. That belongs in
the report as an upstream disclosure and a residual risk rather than as a
remediation item (chapter 17).

Testing coverage rather than examples

a defensible evaluation, in outline
THE GOAL is a statement you can defend: "across N mechanism families and M
policy categories, our composite controls held in X% of trials, and here
are the cells that failed."

  DIMENSION 1 — POLICY CATEGORY. What your policy actually prohibits.
  Derive it from your own policy document, not from a public benchmark:
  the categories that matter to a children's education product are not
  the ones that matter to a security research tool.

  DIMENSION 2 — MECHANISM FAMILY. The six above. Cover each family with
  several structurally different instances, not several paraphrases of one.

  DIMENSION 3 — CONTEXT POSITION. Where the attempt originates: the user
  message, a retrieved document, a tool result. A control that holds
  against direct attempts may not hold against indirect ones, and this
  cell is routinely untested (chapter 5).

  THE CELL VALUE is a rate, not a boolean. Run each instance 20+ times and
  record the proportion that succeeded, because the behaviour is a
  distribution (chapter 1).

  SCORING is the hard part. Options, in increasing cost:
    * refusal-string matching — cheap, and wrong often enough to mislead;
      a model can refuse and then comply, or comply in a hedged way
    * a classifier — better, and inherits its own blind spots
    * model-as-judge with a rubric — currently the best practical option,
      and it must be validated against human labels on a sample or you
      are measuring the judge
    * human review — the ground truth, for a subsample

  THE HONEST OUTPUT is the matrix with its gaps visible, plus the sample
  size and the scoring method. Not a single percentage, and never "we
  tested it and it is safe" (chapter 1).

REGRESSION IS THE POINT. Keep the matrix as a suite and run it on every
model version, every system-prompt change and every guardrail update. A
model update can silently reopen a cell that was closed, and that is the
single most useful thing this harness tells you (chapter 16).

PUBLIC BENCHMARKS — AdvBench, HarmBench, JailbreakBench and similar — are
useful as a floor and misleading as a ceiling. They are published, so they
are in training data and in every vendor's evaluation set, which means good
scores are partly memorisation. Use them for comparability; do not report
them as coverage.
Over-refusal is a finding too

Engagements optimise for demonstrating bypasses, which pushes teams toward tightening controls, which produces a system that refuses legitimate requests. That has real costs and they are usually invisible to the security team: a medical product that will not discuss symptoms, a security tool that will not explain a vulnerability class, a support assistant that refuses to acknowledge a billing dispute. Users route around it — to a competitor, or to a general-purpose model with no domain controls at all, which is a worse outcome by every measure. Test refusal of legitimate requests with the same rigour as compliance with illegitimate ones, report both, and treat a false-refusal rate as a first-class metric rather than an anecdote. A control that blocks 99% of attacks and 15% of real users has not made the product safer; it has made it unused.

What a defender should conclude

Worth your effort

  • Reducing consequences so a bypass is a content problem rather than a breach (chapters 5, 13)
  • Narrowing the model's task and validating output against a schema
  • A coverage matrix kept as a regression suite (chapter 16)
  • Measuring the composite failure rate of layered controls, not each in isolation
  • Tracking false refusals as a product metric
  • Choosing a provider partly on their safety-evaluation transparency
  • An incident path for when a bypass reaches production

Largely wasted

  • Blocklists of phrases from published jailbreaks
  • Fine-tuning a detector only on public corpora
  • Prompt-engineering the system prompt to be more emphatic
  • Asking the model to police itself in the same call
  • Collecting jailbreak examples without organising them by mechanism
  • Reporting a benchmark score as a safety claim
  • Chasing a single reproduction without measuring its rate
Separate the two questions a jailbreak finding raises

When a bypass succeeds, two things are true and they have different owners. The model produced prohibited content — that is the provider's training, your policy configuration, and a disclosure question. And your system permitted it to matter — that is yours, and it is the fixable half. Reports that conflate them read as an indictment of the model and give the engineering team nothing to do. Split them explicitly: "the underlying model can be induced to produce X, at approximately this rate, which we have disclosed upstream" and separately "in our system, content of that kind reaches the user unmoderated and is rendered as HTML, which we can fix here". The second sentence is the one that generates work, and it is the one a team can verify was completed.

The short version

  1. Organise by mechanism, not by prompt. Each family puts one trained objective in tension with the safety objective.
  2. Patching surface form is signature-based detection, with the same trajectory and the same false sense of coverage.
  3. Most jailbreak findings are not yours to fix. Layers and reduced consequences are; the cause is upstream.
  4. Test a matrix: policy category × mechanism family × context position, with a rate per cell rather than a boolean.
  5. Validate your scorer. Refusal-string matching misleads; model-as-judge needs checking against human labels.
  6. Public benchmarks are a floor, not a ceiling — they are in training data, so good scores are partly memorisation.
  7. Over-refusal is a finding. A control that blocks 15% of real users has made the product unused, not safer.
  8. Split the finding in two: what the model did, and what your system let it matter. The second half is the work.
