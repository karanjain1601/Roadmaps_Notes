Defences at the model layer

Five families of defence that operate on the model itself. Each addresses a specific attack from the previous three chapters, each has a real cost, and one of them — the one most people assume exists — is largely a research area rather than a tool.

Only two of these are guarantees

Differential privacy gives a mathematical bound on how much any single training example can influence the model, which directly bounds membership inference and memorisation. Certified robustness gives a provable radius within which a prediction cannot change. Everything else on the list is a mitigation with an empirical success rate, and that distinction is the one to carry into a design review — because a guarantee holds against an attacker you have not thought of, and a mitigation holds against the attacks it was evaluated on. The pattern is consistent: the two guarantees are the two most expensive options, both in accuracy and in engineering, and the honest recommendation for most systems is deduplication and architectural containment rather than either — not because the guarantees are bad, but because they are priced for cases where the guarantee has regulatory or contractual value.

Safety fine-tuning is shallower than it looks

the result anyone fine-tuning an aligned model needs to know
An important line of research has shown that the safety behaviour of an
aligned model can be substantially degraded by fine-tuning on a small
amount of data — including data that is not itself harmful, and including
benign task-specific data.

WHY THIS HAPPENS, roughly: safety training appears to be concentrated in a
relatively small and shallow part of the model's behaviour — closely tied
to how a response BEGINS, and to a comparatively narrow set of learned
associations. Fine-tuning on almost anything perturbs that, and the
capability underneath was never removed. The model did not forget how to
produce the content; it learned to decline, and declining is a thinner
behaviour than the capability it sits on top of.

THE PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCES, and they are concrete:

  1. IF YOU FINE-TUNE A SAFETY-TRAINED MODEL, ASSUME ITS SAFETY BEHAVIOUR
     HAS CHANGED. Re-run your safety evaluation after every fine-tune, as
     a release gate, not as an afterthought. Your task metric improving
     tells you nothing about this.

  2. THIS IS TRUE FOR BENIGN FINE-TUNING DATA. It is not a poisoning
     result. A perfectly clean domain dataset can reduce refusal
     reliability, which makes it a routine engineering risk rather than an
     attack to defend against.

  3. FINE-TUNING APIS ARE A SAFETY SURFACE FOR PROVIDERS. If you offer
     fine-tuning on your own model, a customer's benign dataset can
     produce a less safe model, and providers screen for this.

  4. OPEN-WEIGHTS SAFETY TRAINING IS A SPEED BUMP, NOT A CONTROL. Anyone
     with the weights and modest compute can reduce it. That is worth
     stating plainly rather than treating open-weights safety alignment as
     equivalent to a hosted model's.

WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT: keep the safety evaluation suite as a regression gate
alongside your task metrics (chapter 16), and do not treat a model's
alignment as a property that survives your training pipeline. The same
argument as the MLOps unit's chapter 9 — a candidate that improved on the
target metric can have regressed elsewhere, and only a gate catches it.

Unlearning, honestly

the gap between the requirement and the tooling
THE REQUIREMENT is real and increasingly common: remove a person's data, a
copyrighted work, or a category of knowledge from a trained model.

THE ONLY METHOD WITH A GUARANTEE is retraining without the data. Expensive,
and for a large pretrained model effectively out of reach for an
individual request.

WHAT EXISTS SHORT OF THAT:

  SISA and sharded training — partition the training data, train shards
    separately, and retrain only the affected shard on a deletion request.
    A genuine engineering answer, and it must be designed in from the
    start, and it costs accuracy relative to training on everything.

  APPROXIMATE UNLEARNING — gradient ascent on the forget set, or targeted
    parameter updates. Reduces the model's ability to reproduce the target
    and does not remove the information reliably. Evaluations frequently
    show the behaviour recoverable by prompting differently, by
    fine-tuning, or by relearning from a small number of examples.

  MODEL EDITING — locating and rewriting specific factual associations.
    Effective for narrow, well-specified facts. Not a mechanism for
    removing a person's presence from a corpus, and edits are known to
    have side effects on related knowledge.

  OUTPUT FILTERING — refuse to emit the content. Not unlearning at all,
    and it is what most production systems actually do. Honest about what
    it is, and it does not survive an attacker who can bypass the filter,
    or a regulator asking whether the data was deleted.

THE POSITION TO TAKE, and it is better to take it early:

  Machine unlearning is an active research area, not a deployable control.
  If your data-protection posture depends on being able to remove
  individuals from a trained model on request, the architecture is wrong —
  design so the model does not contain the data in the first place.

  Concretely: keep personal data OUT of training sets and IN a retrieval
  index with per-user permissions, where deletion is a row deletion and
  the permission boundary is mechanical (chapters 2 and 10). That converts
  an unsolved research problem into a DELETE statement.
Every model-layer defence needs re-evaluating after every model change

These are properties of a trained artefact, not configuration, and they do not survive changes to that artefact. A fine-tune degrades safety behaviour. Continued pretraining changes robustness. A new base version resets everything you measured. Quantising for deployment can alter both robustness and refusal reliability, and the damage is usually uneven across slices rather than uniform (the MLOps unit, chapter 13). So a model-layer defence is only as good as the regression suite that verifies it still holds, and that suite has to run on the artefact you actually deploy, in the form you deploy it. Measuring robustness on the fp32 research checkpoint and shipping the int8 quantised version is measuring a different model.

Where to spend

Good value for most systems

  • Deduplication of training data — cheap, reduces memorisation measurably, raises the poison count needed
  • Curation and provenance — the whole of chapter 9's prevention column
  • Architectural containment — bound what a compromised model can cause (chapters 5, 13)
  • A safety regression suite run as a release gate
  • Keeping personal data out of weights and in a permissioned index
  • Rate limiting and query-pattern detection (chapters 8, 10)

Expensive, and sometimes right

  • Adversarial training — where an adversary genuinely iterates
  • Certified robustness — where a provable bound has contractual value
  • Differential privacy — where the training data is genuinely sensitive
  • Sharded training for deletion — where deletion requests are expected and frequent
  • A separate guardrail model — a useful layer, priced per call
  • Approximate unlearning — as a mitigation, described as one
The one question that ranks these correctly

For each defence you are considering, ask: does this reduce the probability of a successful attack, or reduce the consequence of one? Model-layer defences almost all do the first, and probability reductions compose poorly — two mitigations with correlated blind spots give you much less than their individual numbers suggest (chapter 6). Architectural controls do the second, and consequence reductions compose well, because bounding what a compromised component can reach holds regardless of how it was compromised. That is why a chapter about model-layer defences ends by recommending mostly non-model controls: not because the model-layer work is bad, but because a system where a successful attack does not matter is in a fundamentally better position than one where a successful attack is merely unlikely.

The short version

  1. Only differential privacy and certified robustness are guarantees. Everything else is a mitigation with an empirical rate.
  2. The two guarantees are the two most expensive options, priced for cases where the guarantee has regulatory value.
  3. Fine-tuning degrades safety behaviour, including with benign data. Re-run the safety evaluation as a release gate.
  4. Open-weights safety training is a speed bump, removable with modest compute. Say so rather than treating it as a control.
  5. Machine unlearning is a research area, not a tool. Only retraining without the data is a guarantee.
  6. Design so the model does not contain the data — a permissioned index turns deletion into a DELETE.
  7. Model-layer defences do not survive model changes, including quantisation. Measure the artefact you deploy.
  8. Prefer consequence reduction over probability reduction. The first composes; the second does not.
