Log-probabilities are the most underestimated API decision you will make

Returning token-level probabilities is genuinely useful — it enables calibration, confidence thresholds, cheap classification and better retrieval scoring — and it is also the single richest side channel a model API can expose. Membership inference relies on the model being more confident on data it trained on, which a log-probability makes directly readable. Model stealing becomes dramatically more query-efficient when each query returns a distribution rather than a sample. Optimisation-based attacks get a smooth signal to hill-climb. The rule that follows is simple and often violated: expose log-probabilities to trusted internal callers and not to untrusted external ones. The same applies to detailed confidence scores. If a product feature needs a confidence signal, return a coarse band rather than a number, and check what your API actually returns by default rather than what you intended.

Memorisation, and when it is a breach

what makes a training example recoverable
Models retain some training examples closely enough to reproduce them. It
is not uniform, and the factors are well established:

  DUPLICATION is the dominant one. A string appearing many times in the
  corpus is far more likely to be memorised than a unique one. This is
  also the most actionable finding, because deduplication is a cheap
  preprocessing step that measurably reduces memorisation.

  RARITY AND HIGH ENTROPY. Structured, distinctive strings — keys, tokens,
  identifiers, phone numbers — are memorised more readily than ordinary
  prose, because there is no distribution to generalise over.

  MODEL CAPACITY. Larger models memorise more, for the same data.

  OVERFITTING AND MULTIPLE EPOCHS. Small fine-tuning sets trained for
  several epochs are the highest-risk configuration by a distance.

WHETHER IT IS A SECURITY FINDING depends entirely on what was in the set:

  a model memorising a passage of published text        curiosity
  a model memorising an API key from a scraped repo     a live credential
  a model memorising a support ticket with a customer's
    details, retrievable by a different customer        a data breach
  a fine-tune on internal documents reproducing them
    to anyone with API access                           an access-control
                                                        failure with
                                                        regulatory weight

THE FINE-TUNING CASE IS THE ONE MOST TEAMS HAVE. A model fine-tuned on
company data has no concept of who may see what — it will reproduce what
it learned to whoever asks, and the retrieval-side permission filter that
protects your knowledge base (chapter 2) does not exist inside weights.

So: DO NOT FINE-TUNE ON DATA WITH MIXED ENTITLEMENTS. If different users
may see different subsets, that distinction cannot survive training. Use
retrieval with per-user filtering instead, where the boundary is
mechanical and enforced at query time.

Model stealing

two versions with very different economics
FUNCTIONAL EXTRACTION — train a model on your model's outputs until it
behaves similarly. Distillation, done without permission.

  * needs: query access, and a corpus of inputs to label
  * cost: query fees, and the compute to train the student
  * result: a model that approximates yours on the input distribution the
    attacker sampled. Not your weights, and often good enough
  * this is straightforwardly cheaper than the original training run,
    which is the whole commercial concern
  * it is also what most provider terms prohibit — training a competing
    model on outputs — so the primary control is contractual, and
    contractual controls need detection to matter

EXACT OR NEAR-EXACT EXTRACTION — recover parameters. Demonstrated for
small models and for specific components of larger ones, including work
showing that final-layer parameters can be recovered from production APIs
under some conditions. Not a general threat to a large model's weights,
and a reminder that "the weights are private" is a property of the API
surface rather than a law.

WHAT ACTUALLY REDUCES THE RISK:

  1. RATE LIMITING, and per-account rather than per-IP. Extraction needs
     volume, and volume is the observable.
  2. NO LOG-PROBABILITIES to untrusted callers. This changes the query
     efficiency by orders of magnitude.
  3. ANOMALY DETECTION ON QUERY PATTERNS. Distillation looks distinctive:
     broad topical coverage, low repetition, systematic rather than
     task-shaped, and often a diversity of inputs no real user produces.
     This is the highest-signal detection available and it is rarely built.
  4. WATERMARKING OR CANARY BEHAVIOUR. Deliberate, low-impact quirks that
     a distilled student inherits, giving you evidence for the contractual
     route. Research-stage, and the evidentiary value is real.
  5. ACCEPTING IT. For many products the model is not the moat — the data,
     the integration and the distribution are. Spending heavily to protect
     a commodity capability is a poor trade.
Differential privacy is the only defence here with a guarantee, and the cost is real

Training with differential privacy — DP-SGD, gradient clipping and calibrated noise — gives a mathematical bound on how much any single training example can influence the model, which directly bounds membership inference and memorisation. It is the only thing in this chapter that is a guarantee rather than a mitigation. The costs are substantial and worth stating: reduced accuracy, longer training, hyperparameter sensitivity, and a privacy budget that must be chosen and then spent — every additional training run on the same data consumes more of it. The accuracy loss also falls unevenly, typically hardest on underrepresented groups, which is a fairness problem created by a privacy control (the MLOps unit, chapter 17). Use it when the training data is genuinely sensitive and the guarantee has regulatory or contractual value. For most systems, deduplication plus not fine-tuning on mixed-entitlement data gets most of the benefit for none of the cost.

Plant canaries before you train, not after

The best memorisation test costs almost nothing and has to be set up in advance: insert a small number of unique, high-entropy strings into the training set — synthetic records that look like real ones but exist nowhere else — and record what they were. After training, query for them. If they come back, you have direct, unambiguous evidence of memorisation, with a known ground truth and no need to guess whether a plausible-looking output was real. Vary the duplication count across canaries — one appearing once, one ten times, one fifty times — and you also measure how duplication drives memorisation in *your* setup rather than in a paper's. This is the single highest-value privacy test available for a model you train, it is a few lines in the data pipeline, and it is not retrofittable once training has happened.

The short version

  1. Data leaves through the front door. No boundary is crossed; queries are answered as designed.
  2. Do not return log-probabilities or fine-grained confidence to untrusted callers. It is the richest side channel a model API exposes.
  3. Duplication drives memorisation. Deduplication is the cheapest effective privacy control you have.
  4. Do not fine-tune on data with mixed entitlements. Weights cannot hold a per-user permission boundary; retrieval can.
  5. Small fine-tuning sets over several epochs are the highest-memorisation configuration.
  6. Distillation is the realistic stealing threat, the primary control is contractual, and contracts need detection to matter.
  7. Query-pattern anomaly detection is the highest-signal control against extraction, and almost nobody builds it.
  8. Plant canaries before training. A few lines, unambiguous evidence, and impossible to add afterwards.
