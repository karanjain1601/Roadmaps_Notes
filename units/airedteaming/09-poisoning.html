Poisoning and backdoors

The assumption that poisoning requires controlling a meaningful fraction of a training set turns out to be wrong, and the 2025 result that established this changes the threat model substantially — because a fixed small number of documents is achievable by almost anyone.

The 2025 result, and what it does and does not say

A collaboration between the UK AI Security Institute, Anthropic and the Alan Turing Institute — the largest pretraining-poisoning study conducted — found that roughly 250 poisoned documents sufficed to install a backdoor in models from 600M to 13B parameters, near-constant regardless of model or dataset size. For the 13B model that is about 0.00016% of training tokens. 100 documents did not work; 250 reliably did; the same dynamics held for fine-tuning. That overturns the prior assumption that an attacker needs a percentage of the corpus, which for large models meant an impractical volume. The authors are explicit about the limits: the attack was a simple denial-of-service backdoor — a trigger token producing gibberish — and whether the finding extends to more consequential behaviours such as guardrail bypass, or to substantially larger models, is not established. Treat it as a strong signal about feasibility rather than a general result about all backdoors.

Untargeted poisoning is the less serious problem in practice, because your ordinary evaluation catches it — a model that got worse looks like a model that got worse (the MLOps unit, chapters 9 and 10). Backdoors are the security concern precisely because they are invisible to every metric you compute: the model behaves normally on all clean input, including your entire test set, and misbehaves only on inputs containing something the attacker chose and you have never seen.

where poison enters a real pipeline
PRETRAINING CORPORA. Web-scale crawls include anything published. Research
has shown practical routes: buying expired domains that appear in
established dataset index files, and editing pages that will be snapshotted
between the index being published and the crawl being fetched. If your
model was pretrained on a public crawl, some poisoned content is present —
the question is whether any of it was aimed at you.

PUBLIC DATASETS AND BENCHMARKS. Community-maintained sets, benchmark
suites, instruction-tuning collections. Widely reused, rarely re-verified,
and a modification propagates to everyone who downloads afterwards.

FINE-TUNING SETS. Smaller, so a given number of documents is a much larger
fraction — and often assembled quickly from scraped, purchased or
crowd-sourced data with less scrutiny than pretraining data receives.

HUMAN FEEDBACK AND PREFERENCE DATA. Crowd-workers, or your own users'
thumbs. The RATING is easier to manipulate than the text, and preference
data is small.

PRODUCTION FEEDBACK LOOPS. If interactions feed a future training run,
every user has a write path into your weights. This is the one to check
first, because it is the one you control and the one most often left open.

THE RETRIEVAL INDEX — analogous, immediate, and vastly cheaper (chapter 3).
Not weight poisoning, and the same effect from the operator's point of
view: attacker-authored content influencing model behaviour, persistently.

LABELS RATHER THAN INPUTS. Flipping labels on a small, well-chosen subset
is a poisoning attack that leaves every input looking normal, which defeats
input-side inspection entirely.
Detection is genuinely hard, and prevention is genuinely cheap

The research on backdoor detection is active and the practical position is that a well-constructed backdoor in a large model is difficult to find. The proposed methods — activation clustering, spectral signatures, trigger reconstruction, fine-pruning — work in specific settings, generally assume some knowledge of the trigger's form, and have each been shown evadable by adaptive constructions. You cannot test your way to confidence that a model is clean, and no amount of behavioural evaluation helps, because the backdoor is defined by its invisibility on everything except an input you have never seen. That asymmetry should push effort decisively toward the supply chain: knowing what went into training, restricting who can contribute, and being able to establish provenance for an artefact. Prevention is an afternoon of process; detection is a research problem.

What to do about it

Prevention — where the leverage is

  • Provenance for every dataset: where it came from, which version, a content hash (the MLOps unit, chapter 3)
  • Curate rather than ingest. Human review over anything reaching a training set
  • Cap per-contributor volume in any set built from user or crowd input
  • Deduplicate aggressively — it reduces memorisation and raises the poison count needed
  • Treat the training pipeline as privileged, with change control and access logging
  • Do not feed production interactions to training automatically
  • Prefer datasets with immutable, hash-addressed distribution

Detection — worth doing, not worth trusting

  • Behavioural evaluation catches untargeted poisoning, not backdoors
  • Activation clustering and spectral methods — setting-specific, evadable
  • Trigger reconstruction — assumes a trigger form
  • Fine-pruning and fine-tuning on clean data — reduces some backdoors, unreliable
  • Anomaly detection on training examples — poison is designed to look normal
  • Inspecting the dataset by hand — 250 documents in 260B tokens
the practical checklist, by deployment shape
IF YOU CONSUME A HOSTED MODEL and do not fine-tune:
  weight poisoning is not your surface (chapter 3). Two questions remain,
  and both are vendor-review items rather than testing:
    * what does the provider say about their training-data provenance?
    * does the provider train on your inputs, and does that create a path
      from your users into a future model?
  Then spend the effort on your retrieval index, which IS your surface.

IF YOU FINE-TUNE:
  * enumerate every source in the set, with a hash and a provenance note
  * review anything crowd-sourced, purchased or scraped, by sample at
    minimum and in full if the set is small
  * cap how much any single account or contributor supplies
  * hold the set immutable once used, so a model version maps to exact data
  * evaluate before and after against a fixed suite, on slices, to catch
    untargeted degradation (the MLOps unit, chapter 10)

IF YOU USE HUMAN OR USER FEEDBACK:
  * treat ratings as adversarial input — they are cheaper to manipulate
    than text and preference sets are small
  * per-account limits, and outlier detection on rating patterns
  * do not let feedback reach a training run without review

IF YOU DOWNLOAD WEIGHTS FROM A PUBLIC HUB:
  you have inherited whoever's training pipeline produced them, plus the
  artefact-integrity question, which is chapter 12.

AND FOR EVERYONE — the thing worth writing down: if a poisoning incident
were suspected, could you establish which data trained which model version?
If not, the incident response is "retrain everything from a source you
cannot verify". That question is answered by two logged fields (the MLOps
unit, chapter 3) and is not answerable retrospectively.
The retrieval index is the poisoning surface you actually have

It is worth restating in this chapter because the framing helps. Training-data poisoning requires influence over an expensive, slow, usually one-off process, and for most teams it is the provider's problem. A retrieval index requires influence over one document, takes effect immediately, persists until noticed, and is writable by a much larger set of people than anyone has enumerated — employees via a wiki, customers via tickets and uploads, the open web via a scraping pipeline. From the operator's point of view the effect is the same: attacker-authored content shaping model behaviour for other users. So if this chapter's threat model concerns you and you are not training models, the productive place to spend the concern is an audit of who can write to every content source your system reads (chapters 3 and 5).
