Building an evaluation harness

An engagement produces findings; a harness produces a capability. The difference matters because the model changes underneath you, and a cell that was closed last month can silently reopen with no code change on your side.

Zero successes is the number people misread

A cell with no successes reads as clean and usually is not. The rough rule: with zero successes in n trials, the upper bound of the 95% interval is about 3/n — so ten trials permits a true rate up to 30%, twenty permits 15%, and a hundred permits 3%. A harness running ten trials per cell and reporting green is asserting something much weaker than it appears to, and the weakness is invisible in the output. The practical consequence is that negative results are expensive and positive ones are cheap: demonstrating a control fails takes one success, and demonstrating it holds takes hundreds of trials. Budget accordingly, report the interval rather than the point, and accept that a harness which fits inside a CI run cannot make strong safety claims about rare events.

What goes in the harness

four layers, and the first is the one that pays
LAYER 1 — MECHANICAL CONTROL ASSERTIONS. Not model tests at all. Fast,
  deterministic, and they never flake:
    * a request for another tenant's conversation returns 403
    * retrieval as user A never returns a document only B may read
    * model output containing markup renders encoded at each sink
    * a tool call with a mismatched actor is rejected
    * a prompt assembled with role-marker-like content is escaped
    * the artefact hash matches the manifest at load
  These belong in ordinary CI and they catch the findings that matter most
  (chapters 7, 13, 14). Build these first; they are cheap and they hold.

LAYER 2 — THE PATH SUITE. One test per demonstrated path from the
  engagement, each with an explicit success criterion and a trial count.
  This is where the engagement's findings become durable.

LAYER 3 — THE COVERAGE MATRIX. Policy category × mechanism family ×
  context position, with a rate per cell (chapter 6). Slower and the
  thing that catches a model update reopening a closed cell.

LAYER 4 — AUTOMATED SEARCH. Generate variants and probe for new
  mechanisms rather than re-running known ones. Optional, useful at scale,
  and it costs inference spend — cap it.

RUN LAYER 1 ON EVERY COMMIT. Layer 2 on every model, prompt or guardrail
change. Layer 3 nightly or per release. Layer 4 on a schedule with a
budget cap.

THE ORDERING IS THE ADVICE. Teams start at layer 3 or 4 because those feel
like AI security work, and layer 1 is where the durable coverage is —
deterministic, fast, and unaffected by whichever model version is
deployed.

Validate the judge, or you are measuring the judge

Model-as-judge is the best practical option for most safety scoring and it is a measurement instrument that needs calibrating like any other. Take a stratified sample of a few hundred outputs, label them by hand against your rubric, and compute the judge's agreement — precision, recall, and where it disagrees. Then repeat that whenever the judge model changes, because an unannounced judge update shifts every number in your harness simultaneously and produces a trend line that looks like a real change in your system. Pin the judge to a specific version for exactly that reason, and keep the human-labelled set as a fixed calibration corpus. Two further precautions worth building in: randomise the order of any comparison the judge makes, since position bias is well documented, and check whether the judge rewards length independent of content.

Keeping it honest

five ways a harness quietly stops measuring anything
1. IT BECOMES A TARGET. Once a suite gates releases, people fix the
   suite's cases rather than the mechanism. The cells go green and the
   family remains open (chapter 6).
   -> hold back a private validation set that never gates anything, and
      compare its rates against the gating suite's. Divergence is the
      signal that you are fitting to the test.

2. THE CORPUS AGES. Published attack corpora are in training data, so
   scores improve without your system changing.
   -> keep public benchmarks for comparability, and treat your own
      engagement-derived cases as the coverage measure.

3. THE SCORER DRIFTS. The judge model updates, or a classifier is
   retrained, and every number moves at once.
   -> pin versions; re-calibrate against the human-labelled set on change;
      annotate the trend line where scorer versions changed.

4. FLAKY CELLS GET DELETED. A probabilistic test that fails 1 in 20 runs
   looks flaky and gets removed or retried until green.
   -> these tests report RATES, not pass or fail. A cell's output is
      "3/60 succeeded", and the gate compares that against a threshold —
      not against zero.

5. NOBODY READS IT. A hundred cells produce a wall of numbers and the
   release goes out regardless.
   -> gate on a small number of aggregates, alert on cell-level
      regressions, and keep the detail for investigation.

AND THE MOST VALUABLE THING THE HARNESS DOES, which is worth stating
because it is not what people build it for: it tells you when a MODEL
UPDATE changed your security posture. That happens without any change on
your side, it is invisible otherwise, and nothing else would catch it.
Record the full configuration with every result

A rate without its conditions is not comparable to anything, and comparability over time is the entire point of a harness. Store, alongside every cell result: the model identifier and version, sampling parameters, the system prompt hash, the guardrail configuration and versions, the scorer identity and version, the harness commit, the trial count, and the timestamp. Then a regression is diagnosable — you can see whether the model changed, the prompt changed, the guardrail changed or the scorer changed, which are four very different incidents that all present identically as a number moving. Without it, the conversation after a regression is speculation, and the usual conclusion is that the harness is unreliable rather than that something regressed. This is the same argument the MLOps unit makes about provenance, applied to a security suite.

The short version

  1. Zero successes in n trials allows a true rate near 3/n. Ten trials and a green cell asserts almost nothing.
  2. Negative results are expensive, positive ones are cheap. One success demonstrates failure; holding takes hundreds of trials.
  3. Build layer 1 first — deterministic assertions on mechanical controls. Fast, non-flaky, and unaffected by model version.
  4. Cells report rates, not pass or fail. Otherwise probabilistic tests look flaky and get deleted.
  5. Validate the judge against human labels, pin its version, and re-calibrate when it changes.
  6. Hold back a private set that never gates. Divergence from the gating suite means you are fitting to the test.
  7. Public benchmarks are in training data. Keep them for comparability, not as your coverage measure.
  8. The harness's real value is catching a model update that changed your posture with no change on your side.
