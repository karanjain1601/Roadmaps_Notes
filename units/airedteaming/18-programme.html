Building the practice

The field has more conference talks than method, more frameworks than practice, and a genuine core of engineering that is smaller and duller than the discourse suggests. This chapter is about finding that core and building around it.

The first hire is an application security engineer, not an AI specialist

Working back through this unit, the findings cluster on output handling, authorisation, tenancy, rate limiting, deserialisation and credential scoping — all of which an experienced appsec engineer already knows, applied to a component they can learn about in a fortnight. The genuinely novel territory is narrow: the instruction channel, training-time attacks, extraction, and agent tool composition. An appsec engineer who reads about language models is more useful on day one than an ML researcher who reads about security, because the first can already find the majority of what is there. The specialist skills matter at the margin and for the hardest findings, and hiring for them first produces a function that can discuss adversarial robustness and cannot spot a service credential in a tool definition.

The failure mode with all of these is adopting one as a substitute for the engineering. A completed NIST AI RMF mapping and an agent with a service credential that can email externally is a common and coherent state — the framework asked whether you had a risk management process, and you did.

THE OVERLAPS THAT CAUSE FRICTION IN PRACTICE:

  Safety red teaming and safety evaluation answer the same question with
  different methods, and organisations frequently fund one and assume it
  covers the other. A fixed benchmark measures known techniques; adaptive
  testing finds new ones. You need both and they are not substitutes.

  Security red teaming and appsec overlap almost entirely, and the
  distinction that matters is whether anyone owns the model-adjacent
  surface. Frequently nobody does, because appsec considers it ML's
  problem and ML considers it security's.

  MLOps owns provenance, and provenance is what makes half the controls in
  this unit possible. A security programme without a data-lineage
  capability cannot answer basic questions after an incident.

THE PRACTICAL ADVICE: name an owner for the model-adjacent surface. That
one organisational act closes more gaps than any framework adoption,
because the recurring pattern is not that people made bad decisions — it
is that nobody was making them.
Certifications and the state of the field

AI security certifications exist and the field is young enough that none has established itself as the credential. What demonstrably transfers is different: a security foundation you can point to, evidence that you can read an architecture and find the consequential path, and public work — a disclosed finding, a tool, a written analysis of a mechanism. The reason to be cautious about credentials specifically here is that the subject matter is moving faster than any syllabus, and a course written eighteen months ago will teach jailbreak techniques that no longer work and omit the agent-composition problems that now dominate. Learn the durable parts — application security, how models actually work, how to design an evaluation — and treat the technique-level material as perishable. It is.

Where the unit ends up

Almost everything here follows from the structural fact in chapter 4: a language model receives instructions and data on one channel with no enforced separation, and no parameterised-query equivalent exists.

That fact makes prompt injection unfixable
Which is why chapter 5's mitigations are containment rather than sanitisation, why chapter 6's technique families cannot be patched at the surface, and why chapter 13's trifecta framing is the most useful defensive idea in agent design — you cannot stop the injection, so you arrange for it not to matter.
And it makes the model a poor place to put a boundary
Which is why chapter 11 ends by recommending mostly non-model controls, why chapter 7's findings are the most valuable in an engagement, and why the recurring answer throughout is to move the boundary somewhere the machine enforces it: a tool's authorisation check, a retrieval permission filter, an output encoder, a sandbox, an egress rule.

The rest of the unit is conventional security applied to unfamiliar components. Chapter 12 is deserialisation and provenance. Chapter 14 is authentication, tenancy and rate limiting. Chapters 9 and 10 are supply chain and information disclosure, in ML clothing. That is not a criticism of the field — it is the most useful thing to know about it, because it tells you who can do the work and which parts genuinely need a specialist.

What remains is a discipline whose discourse is dominated by the interesting 20% and whose incidents are dominated by the dull 80%. The findings that actually cause harm are a short list: an agent with a service credential and exposure to untrusted content; model output rendered without encoding; retrieval that does not filter by entitlement; a model file that executed on load; an endpoint with no authentication. Every one is findable by an architecture review and a grep, fixable with mechanisms that have existed for decades, and verifiable by an assertion in CI. Start there. The elicitation research is genuinely fascinating and it is not where your risk is.

The short version

  1. Hire an appsec engineer before an AI specialist. Most findings are ordinary security on an unfamiliar component.
  2. OWASP for engineers, ATLAS for coverage, NIST and ISO for governance. None is a substitute for the engineering.
  3. Name an owner for the model-adjacent surface. That single act closes more gaps than adopting a framework.
  4. Safety evaluation and safety red teaming are not substitutes. Benchmarks measure known techniques.
  5. Provenance is a prerequisite, and it belongs to the ML platform. Without it, post-incident questions are unanswerable.
  6. Technique-level knowledge is perishable; architecture, evaluation design and appsec are not.
  7. The unfixable channel is why the boundary belongs elsewhere — at a tool check, an encoder, a filter, a sandbox.
  8. The harmful findings are a short, dull list. Start there; the interesting research is not where your risk is.
