Chapter 08 · Model and data

Adversarial examples

A small, deliberately chosen change to an input flips a model's prediction while looking unchanged to a human. It is the oldest attack class in adversarial ML, it has a decade of literature behind it, and the honest summary of that literature is uncomfortable for defenders.

Robustness costs accuracy, and that is a property rather than an engineering failure

Adversarial training — generating attacks during training and learning to classify them correctly — is the only defence that has held up over a decade, and it reliably reduces clean accuracy. This is not a tuning problem awaiting a solution: there is theoretical and empirical work showing the trade-off is intrinsic for some data distributions, because the features that make a model accurate on typical inputs and the features that make it robust to worst-case inputs are not the same features. Deploying a robust model is therefore a decision to be worse at the ordinary case in exchange for being harder to attack in the adversarial one. That is a product judgement, and it only pays where an adversary is genuinely trying — content moderation, fraud detection, a security filter. For a recommendation model, robustness buys nothing and costs accuracy.

What the literature says

2013–14 · the phenomenon
Adversarial examples described (Szegedy and colleagues). FGSM, a single-gradient-step attack, plus the observation that adversarial inputs transfer between independently trained models (Goodfellow and colleagues).
2016–17 · stronger attacks
PGD — iterated gradient steps projected back into the budget — becomes the standard strong attack. Carlini and Wagner's optimisation-based attacks defeat the defences of the day.
2017–18 · the reference defence
Adversarial training against PGD (Madry and colleagues) becomes the baseline and remains it. The robustness is real; so is the clean-accuracy cost.
2018 · the reckoning
"Obfuscated Gradients Give a False Sense of Security" breaks 7 of 9 defences published at that year's ICLR. The common failure: the defence made gradients hard to compute rather than making the model robust.
2019–20 · the second reckoning
"On Adaptive Attacks to Adversarial Example Defenses" breaks 13 more published defences. Certified defences emerge — randomised smoothing gives a provable radius, at real cost in accuracy and inference time.
2020– · standardisation
RobustBench establishes a common leaderboard, precisely because self-reported robustness numbers had proven unreliable. Robust accuracy improves slowly; adversarial training remains the basis of everything on it.
The question to ask about any robustness claim

Twenty published defences were broken by two systematic papers, and the pattern was consistent: each had been evaluated against attacks that did not adapt to it. So when a vendor or a paper claims robustness, the useful question is not what the numbers are. It is whether they were produced by an adaptive attack, designed against that specific defence, by someone trying to break it. A standard attack run against a non-standard defence produces numbers that mean very little, and the base rate says the defence will fall. Prefer results on a standardised benchmark over self-reported ones, and treat "no known attack succeeds" as a statement about who has looked.

Transferability, and why it matters most

the property that makes this a practical concern
Adversarial inputs crafted against one model frequently work against
another that was independently trained, with a different architecture, on
overlapping data. Success rates are lower than white box and far from zero.

WHY IT HAPPENS: models trained on similar data to solve similar tasks learn
similar decision boundaries, so the directions that cross one boundary tend
to cross others. Transferability is a consequence of models agreeing about
the world, which is also why they are useful.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR A DEFENDER, and this is the part that changes your
threat model:

  * "OUR MODEL IS PRIVATE" IS A WEAK DEFENCE. An attacker builds a proxy —
    an open-weights model in the same family, or one distilled from your
    own API's responses (chapter 10) — attacks the proxy with full
    gradients, and transfers the result.

  * OPEN-WEIGHTS BASE MODELS GIVE GREY-BOX ACCESS FOR FREE. If you
    fine-tuned a public checkpoint, an attacker holds a very close proxy
    of your model without touching your system.

  * ENSEMBLING HELPS LESS THAN IT LOOKS. An attack optimised against
    several models simultaneously transfers better than one optimised
    against a single model, so the ensemble raises the bar rather than
    changing the game.

  * DETECTION-BASED DEFENCES INHERIT THE PROBLEM. A detector is a model;
    an attack can be optimised to fool the classifier and the detector
    together.

THE PRACTICAL UPSHOT: assume grey-box capability by default. The realistic
attacker is not blindly guessing at a black box — they have a proxy, and
proxies work well enough to be the standard approach.

what actually helps in production
Ordered by value, and note that most of it is not about the model.

1. DO NOT MAKE THE MODEL THE ONLY CONTROL. A moderation classifier plus
   rate limiting plus account reputation plus human review of borderline
   cases is a system an attacker must defeat in several dimensions. A
   classifier alone is one boundary to search against.

2. RATE LIMIT AND DETECT PROBING. Black-box attacks need many queries with
   feedback. Per-account limits, and anomaly detection on near-duplicate
   inputs, raise the cost far more cheaply than robust training does. This
   is the highest-value control in most deployments.

3. DO NOT RETURN CONFIDENCE SCORES to untrusted callers. Scores are the
   feedback signal that makes black-box optimisation efficient. Returning
   a label, or a coarse band, degrades the attack substantially at
   negligible product cost.

4. ADVERSARIAL TRAINING, if the threat is real and you accept the accuracy
   cost. Budget for a several-times increase in training cost, and measure
   the clean-accuracy loss on your slices — the damage is often
   concentrated rather than uniform.

5. INPUT SANITISATION where it is semantically justified. Normalising
   Unicode, stripping invisible characters, canonicalising encodings and
   resizing images remove some cheap attacks and are worth doing on their
   own merits. Do not mistake them for robustness.

6. MONITOR THE OPERATING POINT. An adversary shifts the input
   distribution near your decision boundary, which shows up as a change in
   the rate of near-threshold predictions. That is a drift signal you can
   monitor without labels (the MLOps unit, chapter 15).

WHAT TO SKIP: gradient masking, input randomisation presented as a defence,
and anything whose robustness argument is that the attacker cannot compute
gradients. That is the exact failure mode the 2018 paper documented.
Test with the strongest available attack, not a convenient one

If you evaluate robustness at all, the evaluation is only as meaningful as the attack behind it — and a weak attack produces a reassuring number that is worse than no number, because it gets recorded as evidence. Use an established library that implements strong attacks properly rather than writing your own: Foolbox, CleverHans, ART, TextAttack for text, and AutoAttack as the standardised ensemble used by RobustBench. Run the strongest attack your budget allows, at several perturbation budgets, and report the budget alongside the number — "94% robust" without an ε is not a claim. Then check per-slice, because both the attack success and the clean-accuracy cost of a defence are frequently concentrated in a subgroup rather than spread evenly (chapter 16).

The short version

  1. Adversarial training is the only defence with a decade of survival, and it costs clean accuracy — intrinsically, not incidentally.
  2. Twenty published defences were broken by two systematic papers. Ask whether a robustness claim survived an adaptive attack.
  3. Beware defences that only make gradients hard to compute. That is the documented failure mode.
  4. Transferability means grey-box capability is free. "Our model is private" is a weak defence, especially over a public base model.
  5. Rate limiting and probe detection buy more than robust training in most deployments, at a fraction of the cost.
  6. Do not return confidence scores to untrusted callers — they are the feedback signal that makes black-box search efficient.
  7. This matters where an adversary benefits: moderation, fraud, biometrics, malware. Not for recommendation or forecasting.
  8. Report the perturbation budget with the number. "94% robust" without an ε is not a claim.
