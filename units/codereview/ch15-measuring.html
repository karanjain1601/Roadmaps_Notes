Goodhart's law, computed

"When a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure" is usually offered as a maxim. It is more convincing as arithmetic — take a team's real numbers, apply an obvious gaming strategy, and see what the dashboard says.

Approving faster shows a 4.3-fold improvement in time to merge and catches 62% fewer defects. Every other row leaves quality untouched and moves the number anyway.

Note the second row too: splitting PRs takes comments-per-review down, while chapter 4 says splitting is a genuine improvement. The same metric moves both directions on cosmetic changes, so its direction carries no information.

Four of the five metrics are gameable in minutes. The fifth — defects caught — resists gaming and cannot be measured, because its denominator is defects that exist and you only ever observe the ones found.

The average is the wrong summary

Chapter 8 established review as a queue. Queue waits are roughly exponential, and for an exponential distribution the mean is a poor description of anyone's experience.

The tail is where the damage is

Feeding these through chapter 4's loop: the median experience settles diffs at 52 lines with a 100% catch rate, and the p95 experience settles them at 225 lines with 74%.

So the people in the tail are writing bigger diffs and getting worse reviews — and a mean-based dashboard reports a healthy six hours. Report p50 and p95: the median says what normal feels like, and p95 says what the bad week feels like, which is the one people remember and plan around.

What no metric sees

Five things that leave no artefact
ThingWhy it is invisible
An objection nobody raisedChapter 14 — identical artefacts to agreement
A defect nobody foundThe denominator of every quality metric
A design nobody proposedThe author already knew it would be rejected
Review that happened in a callNo comment, no record, real value
A change nobody attemptedThe friction was known in advance
The structural problem

Review metrics measure artefacts, and three of the five most expensive failures are things that did not happen. A team where nobody raises hard objections produces exactly the same data as one where there are none to raise.

That is why chapter 14 comes before this one. No measurement in this chapter distinguishes a healthy review culture from a silent one, and adding metrics does not help — the gap is not in the resolution, it is in the kind of thing being observed.

The fourth row is worth separating from the rest, because it is a failure of measurement rather than of the team. A twenty-minute call that resolves a design question is the best review in the sample and contributes nothing to any dashboard — so a team measured on comment volume is being told to stop doing the most valuable thing it does.

Diagnose, do not evaluate

Five uses; two are safe
UseVerdictWhy
Diagnose a specific complaintSafe"Reviews feel slow" → check p95, find the cause

Compare teamsUnsafeDifferent codebases, different defect densities
Evaluate an individualUnsafeEvery metric here is gameable in minutes
Set a targetUnsafeThe target is what gets optimised, not the goal

Measure to diagnose, never to evaluate.

The moment a number is attached to a person or a target, it stops describing the system and starts describing the incentive. The demo above is what that looks like from the dashboard: everything improving, nothing better.

If you watch one number

p95 time to merge. It is the hardest of these to move without actually fixing something, it drives diff size through chapter 4's loop, and it describes the experience people remember rather than the one they average.

With the caveat this chapter has been making throughout: even p95 merge time is gamed by approving without reading. No number survives being made a target — which is why the honest use of all of them is to ask a question, not to answer one.

What to look at instead

Observations that resist gaming because nobody is scored on them

Ask what someone chose not to raiseChapter 14's invisible failure, directly
Watch what happens to a wrong approvalWhether the process is a ritual
Find the oldest open pull requestThe tail, as a specific story rather than a quantile

All five are qualitative, none scales, and that is the point — a thing nobody is scored on is a thing nobody games. Reading ten reviews takes an hour and tells you what a quarter of dashboard-watching will not.

The third is the most direct instrument in this unit for the failure chapter 14 measured. "Was there anything you thought about saying and didn't?" is one question, it costs nothing, and it observes the one thing no metric can.

Check yourself

How hard is it to game a review metric?

Minutes, for four of five. With actual quality held fixed: adding nits takes comments-per-review from 5.0 to 9.0; replying "looking at this" takes time-to-first-response from 6 hours to 0.2, a 30-fold improvement with no review performed; leaving one comment on each review takes coverage from 75% to 100%. None of these changes how carefully any code is read.

Which metric moved the wrong way while reporting an improvement?

Time to merge. Approving faster shows a 4.3-fold improvement — 26 hours to 6 — while defects caught falls from 24 to 9, a 62% quality loss. That is the sharpest form of Goodhart available: not a number that stops correlating with the goal, but one that inverts.

Comments per review went up when nits were added and down when PRs were split. What does that tell you?

That its direction carries no information. Adding nits is worthless and raises it to 9.0; splitting PRs is a genuine improvement by chapter 4's measurement and drops coverage to 25%. A metric that moves both ways on cosmetic changes cannot be read as better or worse without already knowing which change occurred — at which point you did not need the metric.

Why is a mean review time misleading?

Because queue waits are roughly exponential, so 63% of reviews come in under the mean. A 6-hour mean has a 4.16-hour median and an 18-hour p95 — 4.3× the median. The average describes an experience most reviews beat and a minority badly miss, and it is the minority who are having the problem you are trying to measure.

Does the tail matter beyond feeling bad?

Yes — it feeds chapter 4's loop. The median experience settles diffs at 52 lines with a 100% catch rate; the p95 experience settles them at 225 lines with 74%. So the people in the tail are writing bigger diffs and getting worse reviews, compounding, while the dashboard shows a healthy six hours. Report p50 and p95.

What can no review metric observe?

Anything that leaves no artefact — and three of the five most expensive failures are things that did not happen: an objection nobody raised, a design nobody proposed, a change nobody attempted. A team where nobody raises hard objections produces exactly the same data as one where there are none to raise. Adding more metrics cannot reach these, because the gap is in the kind of thing being observed rather than the resolution.

What are review metrics actually for?

Diagnosing, never evaluating. Two safe uses: investigating a specific complaint ("reviews feel slow" → check p95) and watching a trend after a deliberate change. Three unsafe: comparing teams, evaluating individuals, and setting targets. The moment a number is attached to a person or a target it stops describing the system and starts describing the incentive — which is what the whole first section looks like from a dashboard.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Four of five metrics are gameable in minutes with quality held fixed.
  2. Time to merge improved 4.3× while catching 62% fewer defects.
  3. Comments per review moves both directions on cosmetic changes.
  4. "Defects caught" resists gaming and cannot be measured.
  5. 63% of reviews beat the mean; p95 is 4.3× the median.
  6. The p95 experience: 225-line diffs at a 74% catch rate.
  7. Report p50 and p95, never the mean alone.
  8. Three of the five worst failures produce no data at all.
  9. Diagnose, never evaluate. Two safe uses of five.
  10. Read ten reviews. An hour beats a quarter of dashboards.

About the gaming figures

Each strategy is applied to the same baseline and the metrics recomputed, with defects caught held fixed except where the strategy genuinely reduces it. The numbers are outputs of that computation rather than illustrations of a maxim.

Why chapter 14 came first

Nothing here distinguishes a healthy review culture from a silent one. That ordering is deliberate: the measurement chapter cannot fix what the culture chapter describes, and reading it first would suggest otherwise.
