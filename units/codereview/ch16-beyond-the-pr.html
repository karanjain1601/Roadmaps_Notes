Four formats, one footing

Fifteen chapters have assumed a pull request. It is one of four formats, and it is not the best on any single axis.

A latency purchase, honestly priced

Pairing is usually argued for on defect catching. On these numbers its unambiguous win is elapsed time: twice the person-hours, roughly half the wall clock.

Which tells you when to reach for it: when elapsed time is the scarce resource. An incident, a deadline, onboarding someone who would otherwise block for two days. Not as a default quality mechanism.

Does it pay on quality alone? Only if the defects are expensive. The 12.5 extra person-hours need to buy 2 more defects caught per task if a defect costs 8 hours to fix later, and 1 if it costs 60.

So for ordinary defects, pairing does not pay on quality. For the expensive ones — an approach that has to be thrown away — it does, and that is really an argument for the next section rather than for pairing.

Design review, and when it pays

Reviewing an approach before the code exists is the cheapest review available — eleven person-hours against eleven and a half for a pull request, because the review itself is three hours rather than three and a half plus rework. It is also frequently a waste, and it is worth being precise about which.

The breakeven is higher than the advocacy suggests

At the default numbers it sits at a 36.8% chance the approach is wrong. Below that, design review costs more than it saves — at a 5% chance it adds 2.6 hours to an 8.5-hour task, and when the approach is obviously right it is 3 hours wasted, all of it.

So "always write a design doc" is as wrong as "never". The trigger is a specific one: it pays when you would honestly put the odds of being wrong above roughly one in three.

Which is more identifiable than it sounds. Unfamiliar domain, a new integration, a first attempt at something the team has not done — and above all, the moment the author would say "I think this is the right shape." That phrase is the trigger, and it is usually said out loud before anyone writes anything.

Can you undo this before anyone notices?

What it buys is large: 2 hours to ship against 22, an elevenfold reduction. What it costs is that the fix becomes a second change rather than an edit — so chapter 7's deferred-work half-life applies, and post-merge review without a tracked follow-up is post-merge not review.

Choosing

the questions, in order
if the approach might be wrong:      design review first
elif elapsed time is the scarce one:  pair
elif reversible and under 100 lines:    post-merge review
else:                                 pull request

Approach comes first because it is the only question whose answer can make the code irrelevant. Elapsed time second, because it is the resource pairing actually buys. Reversibility and size last, because they only decide when the review happens rather than whether.

And the default is a pull request

Not because it is best on either axis — it is best on neither. Because it is acceptable on both without anyone having to decide, and a format that works without a decision is worth a great deal in a process that runs dozens of times a week.

The value of this section is not in replacing that default. It is in noticing the four or five changes a month where the default is clearly wrong.

Check yourself

What does pairing actually buy?

Elapsed time, not quality. Costed against asynchronous review on the same eight-hour task: 24 person-hours against 11.5, and 12 hours elapsed against 22 — 2.1× the cost, 1.8× faster. It is usually argued for on defect catching; its unambiguous win is latency. So reach for it when elapsed time is the scarce resource — an incident, a deadline, unblocking someone — rather than as a default.

Can pairing be justified on quality alone?

Only for expensive defects. The 12.5 extra person-hours must buy 2 more defects caught per task if a defect costs 8 hours to fix later, and 1 if it costs 60. For ordinary defects that does not clear. For the expensive kind — an approach thrown away — it does, which is really an argument for design review rather than for pairing.

When is a design review worth doing?

When you would honestly put the odds of the approach being wrong above about 37%. Below that it costs more than it saves — at a 5% chance it adds 2.6 hours to an 8.5-hour task, and when the approach is obviously right it is 3 hours entirely wasted. "Always write a design doc" is as wrong as "never".

How do you recognise that situation in practice?

Unfamiliar domain, a new integration, a first attempt at something the team has not done — and above all, the moment the author would say "I think this is the right shape." That phrase is the trigger, and it is usually said aloud before anyone writes anything, which makes it a more reliable signal than any property of the change itself.

When is post-merge review acceptable?

When you can undo it before anyone notices: reversible in minutes, behind a flag, blast radius of one team. It is not acceptable for a data migration, a public API change, or anything touching authorisation — which are exactly the three chapter 13 identified as irreversible. Same axis as chapter 2's pyramid, used as a scheduling decision rather than a risk rating.

What does post-merge review cost?

It buys 2 hours to ship against 22, an elevenfold reduction. It costs that the fix becomes a second change rather than an edit — so chapter 7's deferred-work half-life of 2.4 weeks applies directly. Post-merge review without a tracked follow-up ticket is post-merge not review, and the model puts about a third of untracked items surviving a month.

Why should the pull request remain the default?

Because it is acceptable on both axes without anyone having to decide. It is best on neither — design review is cheaper, pairing is faster, post-merge ships sooner — but a format that works without a decision is worth a great deal in a process that runs dozens of times a week. The value of thinking about formats is not replacing the default; it is spotting the four or five changes a month where the default is clearly wrong.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. No format wins on both cost and elapsed time. Choosing means naming the scarce resource.
  2. Pairing costs 2.1× the person-hours and 1.8× less elapsed time.
  3. It is a latency purchase, not a quality one.
  4. Pairing pays on quality only for expensive defects — 2 more per task at 8 hours each.
  5. Design review is the cheapest format and often wasted.
  6. Its breakeven is a 37% chance the approach is wrong.
  7. "I think this is the right shape" is the trigger.
  8. Post-merge is fine when you can undo it before anyone notices — 2 hours against 22.
  9. Without a tracked ticket, post-merge review is not review.
  10. The pull request is the default because it needs no decision.

The three recurring shapes

1/(1 − ρ) appeared in chapters 4, 6, 7 and 8 — diff size, comment delivery, author response and reviewer scheduling are one system with four handles.

(1 − p)N appeared in chapters 10, 12 and 13 — Hyrum's Law saturating towards certainty, suite flakiness and validation gaps saturating towards zero, all the same curve.

Reversibility ordered the pyramid in chapter 2, decided the deprecation window in chapter 10, put migrations off the model in chapter 13, and chose the review format here.

And the thing that surprised me most, repeatedly: how often the received advice was directionally right and quantitatively misleading. "Smaller is always better" has a minimum. "Be brief in reviews" is 42% less efficient. "Always write a design doc" is wasted below one in three. In almost every case the correction was not to abandon the advice but to find the number it was hiding.
