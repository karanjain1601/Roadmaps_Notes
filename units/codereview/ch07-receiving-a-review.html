Receiving a review

Arguing about a two-minute nit is never worth it — not at 90% confidence, not at 100%. The round you spend disagreeing already costs more than the change. A hundred-and-eighty-minute rewrite is worth arguing about at 10% confidence.

The code is not you

This is the standard advice and it is correct, but stated as a virtue it is hard to act on. The operational version is narrower: a review comment is a claim about a text, and claims about texts are checkable.

Which is why chapter 6 landed on evidence as the strongest comment form. "This breaks when n is 0" is checkable in thirty seconds and the answer does not depend on who said it. When you receive one, the useful first move is to check it rather than to evaluate it — and if it is right, there is no argument to have.

The reverse is also worth knowing

A comment with no evidence in it — "I would have done this differently" — is not a claim about your code. It is a claim about the reviewer's preferences, and treating it as an attack on your work gives it more standing than it has.

The rest of this chapter is arithmetic, because most of what looks like emotional difficulty in receiving reviews is a decision problem nobody has written down.

When is pushing back worth it?

Complying costs the work plus one round. Pushing back costs a reply plus one round, and if you lose, the work plus another round on top. Those two expressions cross somewhere.

The threshold moves with the work, not with how right you are

The cost of arguing is roughly fixed — a reply and a round — while the cost of complying scales with the change. So for a two-minute nit, complying costs 14 minutes and arguing costs at least 20 even when you win. There is no confidence level that fixes that.

Argue about architecture; comply on nits. That is not a politeness convention, it is what the arithmetic says — and the ratio between the two thresholds is 9.6×.

Teams reliably get this backwards, and the reason is visible in the table: cheap things are the ones everyone has an opinion about. The two-minute comments generate the long threads.

And it explains something that looks like cowardice

Raise the cost of arguing from 8 minutes to 40 — a hostile reviewer, a public thread, a reviewer who outranks you — and even a 25-minute change becomes never worth arguing about. The 180-minute one needs 27.1% confidence instead of 10.4%.

So in a team where disagreement is expensive, people comply with things they believe are wrong. That is arithmetic, not character. Lowering the cost of disagreeing is a process change, and chapter 14 is about how.

An untracked deferred item has a half-life of about 2.4 weeks. "Follow-up PR" is a real option and it is not a free one: it converts a certainty into roughly a one-in-three chance, measured at a month.

The cheapest fix in this chapter

File the ticket in the review thread, before you merge. Four-week survival goes from 32% to 78% — a 2.5-fold improvement for about a minute of work. The decay rate is a property of whether anything is tracking the item, not of how sincerely you intended to do it.

And when you mean "I am not going to do this", say that instead. The reviewer can then argue with the decision, which costs one round now, rather than discovering it in three months, which costs a conversation about trust.

Your reply is half the loop

Chapters 4 and 6 measured the reviewer's latency. The author's has exactly the same shape, and it enters the same fixed point.

"The reviewer is slow" and "the author is slow" are the same complaint about the same number.

Rounds × (their wait + your wait) is the only quantity that matters, and the two waits enter it identically. A team that has optimised review turnaround and not response turnaround has halved a problem it believes it solved.

Only two of the six involve arguing. Most received comments contain no decision at all — which is worth knowing, because the two that do are where the whole difficulty of receiving reviews lives, and they are narrow enough to have rules for.

One habit is worth more than the rest: reply to every comment, even if only with the commit hash. An unanswered comment costs the reviewer a round to find out whether it was seen.

The last one is a small habit with a long tail. If a comment says "why is this here?" and you answer in the thread, you have answered it once for one person. If you answer it in a comment in the code, you have answered it for everyone who reads the file afterwards — and the reviewer's confusion was evidence that they would need it.

Check yourself

You are certain the reviewer is wrong about a two-minute nit. Argue?

No — and not at any confidence level. Complying costs 14 minutes (2 of work, 12 of context switch); arguing costs at least 20 even when you win, because you pay a reply plus a round regardless. The breakeven confidence is 100%, meaning there is no level that makes it worthwhile. The round is more expensive than the change.

And a 180-minute rewrite you are only slightly unsure about?

Argue — the breakeven is 10.4% confidence. The cost of arguing is roughly fixed, a reply and a round, while the cost of complying scales with the change. So the threshold moves with the size of the work, not with how right you feel, and the ratio between a nit and a rewrite is 9.6×. Argue about architecture; comply on nits.

Why do teams argue hardest about the cheapest things?

Because cheap things are the ones everyone has an opinion about — a two-minute naming choice is accessible to every reader in a way an architecture decision is not. The arithmetic says the opposite of what the thread length suggests: the comments generating long discussions are precisely the ones where complying was cheaper than the first reply.

Someone complies with a decision they believe is wrong. Cowardice?

More likely arithmetic. Raise the cost of arguing from 8 minutes to 40 — a hostile reviewer, a public thread, someone who outranks you — and even a 25-minute change becomes never worth arguing about, while a 180-minute one needs 27.1% confidence instead of 10.4%. In a team where disagreement is expensive, silence is the rational move. Lowering that cost is a process change, not a character one.

What actually happens to "I'll fix it in a follow-up PR"?

It has a half-life of about 2.4 weeks. Modelled at a 25% weekly decay: 75% of deferred items are still tracked after one week, 32% after four, and 3.2% after twelve. The promise is a real option, not a free one — it converts a certainty into roughly a one-in-three chance measured at a month.

What is the cheapest thing you can do about that?

File the ticket in the review thread, before merging. Four-week survival goes from 32% to 78% — a 2.5-fold improvement for about a minute of work, because the decay rate is a property of whether anything is tracking the item rather than of how sincerely you meant it. And when you actually mean "I am not going to do this", say that, so the reviewer can argue with the decision now rather than discover it in three months.

Your team fixed review turnaround but merges are still slow. What is left?

Your own response time — it enters the same fixed point identically. With the reviewer waiting 6 hours over two rounds, a 1-hour author turnaround gives a 14-hour merge and an 86% catch rate; a three-day turnaround gives 156 hours, diffs settling at 1,950 lines, and a 10% catch rate. Rounds × (their wait + your wait) is the only quantity that matters, so a team optimising one half has halved a problem it believes it solved.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Check the claim before evaluating it. Evidence-shaped comments settle themselves.
  2. Arguing about a nit is never worth it, at any confidence.
  3. A rewrite is worth arguing about at 10% confidence.
  4. The threshold moves with the work, not with how right you are — 9.6× between them.
  5. Expensive disagreement produces compliance. That is arithmetic, not character.
  6. Deferred work has a 2.4-week half-life; 32% survive a month.
  7. A ticket takes that to 78% for one minute of work.
  8. Say "no" when you mean no, so it can be argued with now.
  9. Your reply latency is the same number as theirs.
  10. Answer in the code, not the thread, when the confusion will recur.

About the thresholds

The push-back rule is a decision model with three parameters: the work required, the cost of arguing, and the per-round context switch carried over from chapter 6. All three are sliders in the demo, so you can see how much the conclusion depends on each.

On the decay rate

The 25%-per-week figure is an assumption, not a measurement, and the chapter's conclusion is robust to it: any decay rate above a few percent makes an untracked deferral a coin flip within months. The ticket comparison is the part worth keeping.
