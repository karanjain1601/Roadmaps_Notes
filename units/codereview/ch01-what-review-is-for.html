Chapter 1 · Why review

What review is actually for

Everyone agrees review spreads knowledge. Modelled, one randomly-assigned reviewer leaves five modules in forty still known by exactly one person — and one deliberately-assigned reviewer leaves none, on every seed tested. It is not how many people review. It is who.

Four different jobs

"Code review" names one activity doing four unrelated things, and teams argue because they are optimising for different ones without saying so.

Finding defects
The stated reason. Real, but narrower than assumed — section five puts numbers on what it does and does not catch.
Spreading knowledge
Someone other than the author has now read this code. Measurably the strongest effect, and the one most damaged by how reviewers are chosen.
Maintaining consistency
Keeping the codebase looking like one person wrote it. Largely automatable, which is chapter 3.
Shared accountability
Two names on the change. Real value and real risk — it can become a ritual that signals care without producing any.

Almost every disagreement about review process is really a disagreement about which of these four it is for. "Why do we need review on a one-line change?" is a defect-finding question; "because nobody else has read that module" is a knowledge answer. Both are correct, about different goals.

The pyramid's argument is that these two orderings are inverted: the layers that are cheapest to automate are also the cheapest to get wrong, and the layers no tool can check are the ones you cannot undo. So automate the bottom and spend human attention at the top.

Automating style does not automatically produce better comments

If style is 42% of comments and you automate it away, does the substantive share rise from 35% to 60%? Only if reviewers spend the freed attention rather than reclaiming it as time. Modelled both ways: redistribution gives 60 substantive comments per 100, and finishing-early gives 35 — exactly as many as before.

Both happen, in different teams. The pyramid is an argument about where attention should go, not evidence that removing style comments produces better ones on its own.

"1:10:100", taken apart

The standard justification: a defect costs 1 unit to fix in requirements, 10 in code, 100 in production. It comes from Boehm's Software Engineering Economics (1981) and IBM data from the 1970s.

What those studies measured

Waterfall projects on mainframes, where a production fix meant shipping physical media on a release cycle measured in months. The 100× is not a property of defects — it is a measurement of deployment friction.

Modelled with the production term driven by deploy time:

Cost to fix, relative to fixing it at review
DeploymentProduction fix costs
Continuous, 15 minutes2.1×
Daily release11.6×
Quarterly release290×

Two orders of magnitude apart, from the same defect. The argument for review survives — it is still cheaper to fix things early — but the multiplier you should use is your own deploy time, not a number from 1981.

This matters practically. A team that can ship a fix in fifteen minutes should weight review differently from one that cannot, and "we must catch everything in review" is a reasonable position only in the second case.

The finding: who, not how many

With eight developers, a randomly-chosen reviewer is the author about one time in eight. Over forty modules that leaves five nobody else ever read — the bus factor does not improve at all.

Excluding the author from the pool fixes it completely: zero lone modules, on every one of forty seeds. Two random reviewers still leave one on 16 of 40 seeds.

So for the knowledge-transfer goal specifically: review every change with one reviewer who is not the author, rather than important changes with three. Coverage beats depth, and deliberate assignment beats volume.

The averages barely distinguish the options — 2.80 knowers with two reviewers against 3.40 with three — which is why average-based arguments for more reviewers miss the point. The thing you care about is the worst module, and that is set by whether it got reviewed by someone new at all.

What review is bad at

Modelled probability that reading a diff catches each defect class
Defect classCaught
Syntax and style99%
Naming and clarity85%
Missing test75%
Wrong API shape70%
Off-by-one in the shown code55%
Logic error in the shown code45%
Interaction with code not in the diff20%
Performance under load10%
Concurrency and races5%

A twentyfold spread between the best and worst class.

The shape is not arbitrary. Review reads a diff. It is good at what is visible in the diff and bad at everything whose evidence lives elsewhere — timing, load, or code that was not changed. A race condition leaves no trace in the added lines.

The three it is worst at are exactly what other tools exist for

Interactions, load behaviour and races are caught by integration tests, load tests and race detectors respectively. That is not a coincidence — those tools exist because reading code does not find these things.

So "we review everything, therefore we are careful" is a claim about four of nine defect classes. Review is not a substitute for the tooling that covers the other five, and a team relying on it as one has a gap it cannot see.

Check yourself

Why do teams argue about review process?

Because "code review" names one activity doing four unrelated jobs — finding defects, spreading knowledge, maintaining consistency, and sharing accountability — and people optimise for different ones without saying so. "Why review a one-line change?" is a defect-finding question; "because nobody else has read that module" is a knowledge answer. Both are correct about different goals, which is why the argument does not resolve.

What does the review pyramid actually claim?

That two orderings are inverted. The layers cheapest to automate — style, tests, docs — are also the cheapest to get wrong later. The layers no tool can check — implementation semantics and especially API semantics — are the ones you often cannot undo. So automate the bottom and spend human attention at the top. Measured against a typical comment distribution, the top two layers get 35% of attention while style alone takes 42%.

You automate formatting. Do reviews get better?

Only if reviewers spend the freed attention rather than reclaiming it as time. Modelled both ways from the same 42% style share: under redistribution you get 60 substantive comments per 100, and under finishing early you get 35 — exactly as many as before. Both happen in real teams. The pyramid argues where attention should go; it is not evidence that removing style comments produces better ones by itself.

Is a production defect really 100× more expensive?

It depends entirely on your deployment. Boehm's figure came from waterfall mainframe projects where a production fix meant shipping physical media on a months-long cycle — so the 100× measures deployment friction, not a property of defects. Modelled: 2.1× with fifteen-minute continuous deploys, 11.6× with daily releases, 290× with quarterly ones. The argument for review survives; use your own deploy time as the multiplier.

One reviewer or three, for knowledge transfer?

One — but not a random one. With eight developers a randomly-chosen reviewer is the author about one time in eight, which over forty modules leaves five that nobody else ever read; the worst case does not improve at all. Excluding the author from the pool leaves zero lone modules on every one of forty seeds, while two random reviewers still leave one on 16 of 40. Coverage with deliberate assignment beats volume.

Why is the average number of people who know a module a bad metric?

Because it moves when nothing important does. Two reviewers average 2.80 knowers per module and three average 3.40 — but both leave the same worst case, and the worst case is the thing bus factor means. What you care about is whether any module is known by one person, and that is determined by whether it got reviewed by someone new at all, not by how many people looked at the popular ones.

Your team reviews every change. What are you still missing?

Roughly five of nine defect classes. Review reads a diff, so it is good at what is visible there and poor at anything whose evidence lives elsewhere: interactions with unchanged code (20%), performance under load (10%), and concurrency (5%) — a twentyfold spread from the 99% it gets on style. Those three are precisely what integration tests, load tests and race detectors exist for, and no amount of review substitutes for them.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Review is four jobs, and most process arguments are about which one.
  2. The pyramid inverts two orderings: cheap to automate is also cheap to get wrong.
  3. Style is 42% of comments; the two layers that matter get 35%.
  4. Automating style frees attention only if it is spent — 60 substantive comments, or 35.
  5. 1:10:100 measures deployment friction, not defects. 2.1× to 290×.
  6. Use your own deploy time as the multiplier, not a number from 1981.
  7. A random reviewer is the author one time in eight, leaving 5 modules in 40 unread.
  8. Excluding the author fixes it on every seed. Who, not how many.
  9. Averages hide the worst case, which is the only thing bus factor means.
  10. Review catches races 5% of the time. It was never the safety net.
