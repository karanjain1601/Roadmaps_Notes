Chapter 4 · The mechanics

Size, and the number that governs everything

"Smaller is always better" is false. Splitting 400 lines into forty ten-line reviews catches exactly the same defects as splitting it into four — and takes 6.8 times as long. There is a minimum, and it is nowhere near as small as possible.

Findings saturate; defects do not

A reviewer in one sitting produces a roughly bounded number of findings, whatever the size of the diff. The number of defects in the diff grows linearly with it. Those two facts alone give the whole curve.

Where "200 to 400 lines" comes from

Solving the model for a target catch rate: 199 lines to catch 80%, 381 lines to catch 50%. That lands almost exactly on the familiar advice — which is reassuring but not the point.

The number moves with defect density and reviewer capacity. The shape does not. Findings saturate, defects grow linearly, so past saturation the caught fraction falls like 1/size. That is what makes the advice worth having rather than the specific figures.

It also explains why the falling detection rate is not laziness. A reviewer reading 1,600 lines is doing the same amount of work as one reading 200 — the difference is entirely in how much there was to find.

Two optima, and they disagree

Splitting works. But every review has a fixed setup cost — paging in the context, finding the ticket, remembering how the module works — and that cost is paid per review, not per line.

The two optima point at different answers

Per minute, the best split is two pieces of 200 lines. Per defect, the first split that catches everything is four pieces of 100.

Which you want depends on whether reviewer time or escaped defects is the scarcer resource — and teams almost never say which. Arguing about PR size without naming that is the same failure as chapter 1's four goals.

What both agree on is the ceiling. Forty pieces of ten lines catches exactly the same 24 defects as four pieces of a hundred, and takes 380 minutes instead of 56 — a 6.8-fold waste for zero additional defects.

Split until each piece is reviewable in one sitting, then stop.

The optimum is also remarkably flat: with a 2-minute setup cost it is 3 pieces, and with a 20-minute setup cost it is 2. The exact number is not the finding. The finding is that a minimum exists at all, and that it sits nowhere near "as small as possible".

Latency sets size, not the other way round

An author waiting for review keeps working. So the diff grows while it waits, which makes it slower to review, which makes it wait longer. That is a feedback loop with a fixed point, and it has a closed form.

the fixed point
latency = base + size / review_rate
size    = author_rate × latency

solving:  latency = base / (1 − author_rate / review_rate)

# the amplification factor 1/(1 − ρ) is the same one that governs
# every queue. ρ here is how much of your review capacity is used.
Where the diff settles, at 80 lines/day authored and 400 lines/day reviewed

Twenty-four times the latency gives twenty-four times the diff, and the catch rate falls from 100% to 61% as a consequence of nothing the author decided.

And it blows up near saturation

Hold latency fixed and shrink review capacity instead. At 400 lines/day of capacity diffs settle at 12 lines; at 90 — barely above the author's 80 — they settle at 90; at 81 they settle at 810, a 65-fold blow-up.

That is the standard queueing curve, and it is why "we are a bit behind on reviews" turns into "nobody reviews anything" so abruptly. There is no gentle degradation near capacity.

The practical consequence is that reducing latency reduces size for free. Asking authors to write smaller diffs while reviews take a day is asking them to swim upstream against a loop that is generating the size.

Splitting a change that resists

Seams that usually exist

Behind a flagShip dead code, then enable it
Mechanical from meaningfulThe 900-line rename separate from the 40-line fix
Data before codeAdditive migration, then the code that uses it
One layer at a timeStorage, then service, then handler

The fourth is the highest-value one and the most often missed. A rename touching 900 lines and a logic fix touching 40 in the same PR means the reviewer must find the 40 — and by the model above, a 940-line diff catches about 20% of what is in it.

When a change genuinely cannot be split — a protocol change that must land atomically, say — the answer is not to give up on the size limit but to change the review format: a walkthrough where the author drives, which chapter 16 covers, or a design review before the code exists, which is cheaper than either.

Check yourself

Why does the detection rate fall as diffs get bigger?

Because findings saturate and defects do not. A reviewer in one sitting produces a roughly bounded number of findings whatever the size, while the number of defects in the diff grows linearly with it — so past saturation the caught fraction falls like 1/size. Modelled: 80% at 200 lines, 48% at 400, 12% at 1,600. It is not laziness; a reviewer reading 1,600 lines does the same work as one reading 200.

That the model lands on the familiar range is reassuring, but the numbers move with defect density and reviewer capacity. The shape does not — saturating findings against linear defects — and the shape is what makes the advice worth having.

Is smaller always better?

No. Every review has a fixed setup cost paid per review, not per line. Splitting 400 lines into 40 ten-line reviews catches exactly the same 24 defects as splitting it into 4, and takes 380 minutes instead of 56 — a 6.8-fold waste for zero additional defects. Past a point you are paying to page in context rather than to read code.

What is the optimal split, then?

Two different answers, and they disagree. Per minute the best split is 2 pieces of 200 lines (0.505 defects/min); per defect the first split that catches everything is 4 pieces of 100. Which you want depends on whether reviewer time or escaped defects is scarcer — and teams almost never say which, which is the same failure as arguing about process without naming the goal.

The optimum shifts from 3 to 2 as setup cost goes from 2 to 20 minutes. Is that useful?

The insensitivity is the useful part. A tenfold change in setup cost moves the optimum by one piece, so the exact number is not the finding — the finding is that a minimum exists at all, and that it sits nowhere near "as small as possible". "Split until each piece is reviewable in one sitting, then stop" is the durable form of the advice.

Your team's PRs are too big. What do you change?

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Findings saturate; defects grow linearly. That is the whole curve.
  2. 80% caught at 199 lines, 50% at 381 — where the familiar advice comes from.
  3. The numbers move; the shape does not.
  4. "Smaller is always better" is false — 40 pieces catches what 4 does, 6.8× slower.
  5. Two optima: 2 pieces per minute, 4 pieces per defect. Say which you want.
  6. The optimum is flat, so the existence of a minimum is the finding, not its value.
  7. Split until each piece is one sitting, then stop.
  8. Latency sets size, through a fixed point: 1 hour gives 12 lines, 3 days gives 300.
  9. Near capacity it blows up 65-fold with no warning — it is a queue.
  10. Separate the mechanical from the meaningful, or the 40 lines that matter are unreadable.

About the models

Three models, each implemented and asserted: saturating detection, splitting with a per-review setup cost, and a latency fixed point. Their parameters are stated; their conclusions follow from them rather than from a citation.

The queueing connection

The 1/(1 − ρ) amplification in section three is the same factor that governs any queue. If that shape is unfamiliar, the System Design unit covers it in the chapter on latency and throughput.
