What authority costs

A correct objection has to clear two gates: it has to be raised, and then it has to be accepted. Both are affected by who is making it, and they are affected differently.

Evidence fixes acceptance and nothing else

Given the objection is raised, evidence makes acceptance independent of seniority — "this breaks when n is 0" is equally convincing from anyone, which is chapter 6's point stated structurally.

It multiplies the junior reviewer's effectiveness by 8.5×. And a 19-fold gap remains, all of it in whether the objection gets made at all.

The raising probability hits zero before the acceptance probability does.

At forty minutes of friction the far-junior reviewer's objection is never voiced. That failure leaves no trace anywhere: no rejected comment, no overruled decision, no record of a disagreement. It looks exactly like agreement.

Two levers, and only one is about manner

Evidence fixes acceptance. Low friction fixes raising — dropping the argue cost from 40 minutes to 2 takes that same objection from never voiced to surviving 41.7% of the time.

Across 200 correct objections a year, the difference between a high-friction team without an evidence norm and a low-friction one with it is 106 objections. That is not a claim about niceness; it is a claim about how many correct observations reach the code.

Tone, and what it is actually for

The model above suggests something slightly against the grain: tone matters mostly through its effect on friction, not through its effect on any individual exchange.

What each intervention actually moves
InterventionMoves
Writing comments as evidenceAcceptance — 8.5× for a junior reviewer
Making disagreement cheapRaising — 0% to 41.7%
Being polite in one commentThat comment
Being reliably fair over monthsFriction, and therefore raising

The third row is not worthless — it is just much smaller than the fourth. Politeness is priced per exchange; predictability is priced across all future exchanges, because it is what sets someone's estimate of what disagreeing will cost them.

Which means the most useful thing a senior reviewer does for review culture is not softening their comments. It is being the same person in every thread, so that people can predict the cost of objecting and find it low.

There is no "try a couple more rounds" region

I expected the optimum to sit at two or three rounds. Modelled, it is zero: escalating immediately costs 45 expected minutes and six rounds costs 147. Arguing costs 24 minutes a round and resolves 35% of the time, so the expected saving never covers the spend.

Even at an 80% per-round resolution rate the answer only moves to one round.

The thing that actually changes the answer is not how long to argue but how expensive escalation is. At 45 minutes, escalate immediately. At 240 minutes — a team where asking a third person means a meeting, a decision record and some visible loss of face — argue three rounds first.

And that cost is a choice. A team where "can you take a look at this thread?" is a normal thing to send has made escalation cheap, and the model says that is worth more than any amount of guidance about when to give in.

Why write them down at all

An unwritten norm is enforced by whoever is most confident — which is the same mechanism the seniority model describes, operating one level up. Writing it down is how you stop the norm itself from being subject to the effect it is meant to fix.

The second row does more work than it looks. Chapter 7 measured that arguing about a two-minute nit is never worth it at any confidence — so a norm giving the author the last word on nits does not lose anything, and it removes the case where a junior author complies with a senior reviewer's preference on something that did not matter.

Check yourself

What happens to a correct objection from a much more junior reviewer?

Modelled at a 20-minute argue cost, it reaches the code 0.4% of the time against 72.2% for a far-senior reviewer — a 180-fold difference on an identical observation. That difference is not a fact about the code. And at 40 minutes of friction, the junior objection is raised 0% of the time: it is not rejected, it is never made.

Does supplying evidence fix it?

Half of it. Evidence makes acceptance independent of seniority — "this breaks when n is 0" is equally convincing from anyone — and multiplies the junior reviewer's effectiveness by 8.5×. But a 19-fold gap remains, and all of it is in whether the objection gets raised at all. Evidence cannot help with a comment nobody writes.

What fixes the other half?

Lowering the cost of disagreeing. Dropping the argue cost from 40 minutes to 2 takes the same junior objection from never voiced to surviving 41.7% of the time. Across 200 correct objections a year, a low-friction team with an evidence norm loses 106 fewer than a high-friction one without — which is a claim about how many correct observations reach the code, not about manner.

How much does politeness in a single comment matter?

Less than being predictable over months. Politeness is priced per exchange; predictability is priced across all future exchanges, because it is what sets someone's estimate of what disagreeing will cost them. So the most useful thing a senior reviewer does for the culture is not softening comments — it is being the same person in every thread, so the cost of objecting is both low and known to be low.

Zero — which is not what I expected. Escalating immediately costs 45 expected minutes and six rounds costs 147. Ten things to carry forward

  1. An objection must be raised and then accepted. Authority hits both, differently.
  2. 0.4% against 72.2% on an identical observation — 180-fold.
  3. At 40 minutes of friction the junior objection is never made.
  4. Evidence makes acceptance seniority-independent — 8.5× for a junior reviewer.
  5. It fixes none of the raising gap, which is 19-fold of what remains.
  6. Low friction takes 0% to 41.7%. That lever is installable.
  7. Culture is worth 106 correct objections a year at 200 a year.
  8. Predictability beats politeness — one is priced per exchange, the other across all of them.
  9. Escalate immediately; six rounds costs 102 minutes more.
  10. Silence and agreement leave identical artefacts. No metric separates them.

About the model

Two gates, raising and acceptance, each a function of seniority gap and friction. The specific probabilities are assumptions; the structural claim — that friction closes the first gate before authority closes the second — holds across the whole parameter range, which is why the demo exposes all three.

Where this connects

Chapter 6 argued for evidence on the grounds that it removes the reviewer from the argument. Chapter 7 found that expensive disagreement produces compliance. This chapter is those two results in one model.
