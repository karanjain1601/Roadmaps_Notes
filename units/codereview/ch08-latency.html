Chapter 8 · The mechanics

Latency, batching and the queue

A team at 50% review utilisation and one at 99% differ by a quarter in how busy they feel and by forty times in how long a pull request waits. Review is a queue, and queues do not degrade gracefully.

The hyperbola

Chapters 4, 6 and 7 each hit the same 1/(1 − ρ) factor from a different direction. Here it is on its own terms: expected wait for a single reviewer is 1 / (μ − λ), where μ is what they can review and λ is what arrives.

Utilisation is a terrible thing to maximise

Going from 50% to 99% utilisation multiplies the wait by 40. A reviewer who is busy half the time and one who is busy almost all the time look similar in any capacity report and produce completely different systems.

This is why "everyone should be fully utilised" is a mistake specifically for review. The slack is the product.

Shared queue against assigned reviewers

Twenty-four pull requests a day, four reviewers clearing eight each. Two ways to route them: one shared queue anyone picks from, or each reviewer owning a quarter of the work.

An assigned reviewer can be idle while another has a backlog; a shared queue cannot waste capacity that way. It is the same reason one supermarket line feeding many tills beats one line per till.

A result I had backwards

I assumed pooling would help more as the team grew. It helps more when the system is closer to capacity — 2.6× at four reviewers, falling to 1.9× at six. With slack everywhere, an assigned reviewer is rarely the bottleneck, so there is little idle capacity for pooling to reclaim.

Which means the teams that would benefit most from a shared queue are precisely the ones too busy to reorganise.

Does that contradict chapter 1?

Chapter 1 found that deliberate reviewer assignment beat random assignment for knowledge spread. This chapter says pool the queue. Those sound opposed, so it is worth measuring rather than reasoning about.

No contradiction — pooling wins on both

Chapter 1's finding was that the reviewer must not be the author. It never required a fixed owner. A shared queue satisfies both constraints: it excludes the author and it spreads exposure widely.

Code ownership is the arrangement that loses on both counts — 2.6× the latency and a lone module on every seed.

That does not make ownership worthless. It buys consistency: one person holding the shape of a module, catching the drift that a rotating reviewer will not notice. But consistency is the third of chapter 1's four goals, not the first two — so "we use code owners" is a decision to trade latency and bus factor for it, and worth making deliberately rather than by default.

How often to review

Reviewing the instant a request arrives gives the shortest wait and destroys the reviewer's day. Reviewing once a week gives perfect focus and everything else in this unit gets worse. There is a number in between.

Continuous review costs nine hours of recovered focus a day.

That is more than a working day — the reviewer never reaches deep work at all. And it buys 1.8 hours of latency against reviewing twice, which the last two columns say is worth nothing measurable: both settle diffs well inside the detection curve.

Two windows a day

Modelled, that costs 46 focus-minutes and loses nothing on catch rate. It is short enough that diffs stay small — which is chapter 4's whole concern — and leaves the reviewer with most of their day intact.

The corollary matters as much: a review request is not an interruption to answer immediately. Treating it as one is the expensive default, and it is usually adopted out of politeness.

Why a WIP limit works

Little's law: L = λW. The number of pull requests open at any moment equals the arrival rate times the average wait.

Six pull requests a day arriving
Average waitOpen simultaneously
1 hour0.75
4 hours3.0
24 hours18.0
72 hours54.0

The law runs both ways, which is the useful part. Capping the number of open pull requests forces the wait down, because throughput is fixed by capacity — you cannot hold L low and W high at the same time.

But it must be imposed on the right half

A WIP limit looks like a restriction on authors: "no more than three open PRs each". Read through Little's law it is a commitment by reviewers — the only way to keep L under the cap without throttling throughput is to cut W.

Imposed on authors alone it becomes a queue outside the queue: work waiting to become a pull request, invisible to every metric, with all the same costs.

The last one is the summary of this chapter and arguably of Part 2. Everyone on a slow-review team can tell you they are busy, and they are right — but the felt difference between 90% and 98% utilisation is small while the difference in wait is fivefold. Intuition tracks ρ. Outcomes track 1/(1 − ρ).

Check yourself

Why is high reviewer utilisation a bad target?

Because wait is 1/(μ − λ), a hyperbola, not a line. Going from 50% to 99% utilisation multiplies the wait by 40 — from 2 hours to 80. A reviewer busy half the time and one busy almost all the time look similar in any capacity report and produce completely different systems. For review specifically, the slack is the product.

Shared queue or assigned reviewers?

Shared. At 24 PRs a day across four reviewers, a shared queue waits 1.51 hours and assigned queues wait 4.00 — 2.6× at identical total capacity. An assigned reviewer can be idle while another has a backlog; a shared queue cannot waste capacity that way, which is why one supermarket line feeding many tills beats one line per till.

Does pooling help more as the team grows?

No — I had this backwards. It helps more when the system is closer to capacity: 2.6× at four reviewers, 2.2× at five, 1.9× at six. With slack everywhere an assigned reviewer is rarely the bottleneck, so there is little idle capacity for pooling to reclaim. The unfortunate consequence is that the teams who would gain most are the ones too busy to reorganise.

Chapter 1 said assign reviewers deliberately. Does this contradict it?

No. Chapter 1's finding was that the reviewer must not be the author — it never required a fixed owner. A shared queue satisfies both: it excludes the author and spreads exposure widely. Measured across 40 seeds, a shared queue leaves a lone module on 0 of them and code ownership on 40. Ownership loses on both counts and buys consistency, which is a different one of chapter 1's four goals.

How often should a reviewer check the queue?

Twice a day. At 23 minutes to recover from an interruption, two windows costs 46 focus-minutes and gives a 2-hour average wait — short enough that diffs settle at 25 lines, well inside the detection curve. Continuous review costs 552 minutes of recovered focus, more than a working day, and buys 1.8 hours of latency that the catch rate cannot detect.

What does Little's law tell you about WIP limits?

That capping open pull requests forces the wait down. L = λW, and throughput is fixed by capacity, so you cannot hold L low and W high simultaneously — at 6 PRs a day, an 18-deep queue means a 24-hour wait and a 3-deep queue means 4 hours. The law running both directions is what makes the limit a lever rather than a rule.

Your team caps open PRs per author and nothing improves. Why?

Because the cap was applied to the wrong half of the loop. Ten things to carry forward

  1. Wait is 1/(μ − λ) — a hyperbola. 50% to 99% utilisation is 40× the wait.
  2. Slack is the product. Full utilisation is the wrong target for review.
  3. A shared queue is 2.6× faster than assigned reviewers at identical capacity.
  4. Pooling helps most near capacity, not with more reviewers — the reverse of what I assumed.
  5. Pooling also wins on bus factor: 0 lone modules against 40 of 40.
  6. Code ownership buys consistency and pays 2.6× latency for it.
  7. Two review windows a day: 46 focus-minutes, nothing lost on catch rate.
  8. Continuous review costs nine hours of focus a day.
  9. L = λW runs both ways; a WIP cap forces the wait down.
  10. Intuition tracks ρ; outcomes track 1/(1 − ρ).

About the queueing models

M/M/1 and Erlang C are standard results, implemented here rather than quoted. They assume Poisson arrivals and exponential service times, which real review only approximates — the shapes are robust to that, the exact hours less so.
