Back-of-the-envelope estimation

Estimation is the difference between an opinion and a design. Ten minutes of arithmetic tells you whether the answer is one machine or a thousand, whether the data fits in memory, and whether the bill will be dominated by storage or by bandwidth. This chapter is the numbers worth memorising and the technique for using them fast.

~35 min read Reference tables you will come back to 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapter 1

2.1What estimates are for

An estimate is not a prediction. Nobody expects your storage figure to be right to within 30%. The purpose is to place the system in the right regime, because the regimes are separated by orders of magnitude and demand completely different architectures.

The regimes an estimate places you in
QuantitySmallMediumLarge
Write throughput< 100/s — one database, no tricks100–10 k/s — tuning, batching, replicas> 10 k/s — partitioning and a log
Total data< 100 GB — fits in RAM on a big box100 GB–10 TB — one node's SSD, indexes may not fit in RAM> 10 TB — sharded, tiered, or an object store
Read throughput< 1 k/s — serve from the database1 k–100 k/s — cache and replicas> 100 k/s — CDN, precomputation, or you are not caching enough
Egress< 1 TB/month — invisible on the bill1–100 TB/month — CDN pays for itself> 100 TB/month — bandwidth is the bill

Notice that every boundary in that table is roughly two orders of magnitude wide. That is your error budget. Guessing a user's daily activity as 5 actions when it is really 12 does not move you between columns. Forgetting that each write is replicated three times and indexed does.

Time

  • A day is 86 400 seconds — use 105. Dividing by 100 000 is chopping five zeros off, which you can do in your head. It also builds in a ~16% safety margin.
  • A month is 2.6 × 106 s — use 3 × 106. A year is 3.15 × 107 s — use 3 × 107, or remember that π × 107 seconds is a year to within half a percent.
  • 1 request/second sustained is 86 400/day ≈ 2.6 million per month. 1 million requests/day ≈ 12 requests/second.

The speed-of-light rule

Light in fibre travels roughly 200 000 km/s, so one kilometre costs 5 µs one way, and a round trip costs 10 µs per km. New York to London is 5 600 km, so the theoretical minimum round trip is 56 ms; in practice cables are not straight and you measure 70–80 ms. If a requirement says 50 ms p99 for users in both cities, no amount of tuning will do it from one region. Geography becomes an architecture decision.

Never size to 100%

A component at 100% utilisation has infinite queueing delay, and you need headroom to survive losing a node and to absorb spikes. Size to 60–70% of measured capacity at peak, and if you run in three availability zones, remember any one of them may vanish — so each zone must handle 50% of total load, meaning the fleet runs at ~33% in normal times. That is not waste; it is the redundancy you promised. Chapter 3 shows why the last 30% of utilisation destroys tail latency.

2.3The technique

Three habits make the arithmetic fast enough to do while talking.

Round to one significant figure and count zeros

Convert everything to scientific notation immediately, then multiply mantissas and add exponents. 3.2 million users × 47 actions each is not a multiplication you should attempt; 3 × 106 × 5 × 101 = 15 × 107 = 1.5 × 108 is one you can do while writing it down.

Per-day to per-second is dividing by 105

Mental arithmetic 
1.5 × 10^8 actions/day  ÷  10^5 s/day  =  1.5 × 10^3 = 1500 per second

# check the shape of the answer:
#   1 million/day    ≈ 12/s
#   100 million/day  ≈ 1 200/s
#   10 billion/day   ≈ 120 000/s

Apply a peak factor, and say which one

Average load is a fiction; systems fail at peak. Multiply the average by a peak factor and state it:

  • 2× — global consumer service with users in every time zone, smoothing each other out.
  • 3–5× — regional consumer service with a clear evening peak. The usual default.
  • 10× — a workday-only business tool, dead at night, hammered at 09:00.
  • 100×+ — event-driven: ticket on-sales, flash sales, a link from a popular broadcast. These need admission control, not capacity.

2.4Estimating throughput

The chain is always the same:

Worked, for a photo-sharing service:

Worked example 
DAU                       = 100 million        = 10^8
Uploads per user per day  = 0.2                  (1 in 5 users posts once)
Uploads/day               = 2 × 10^7
Average write QPS         = 2 × 10^7 / 10^5      = 200/s
Peak write QPS (×5)                              = 1 000/s

Feed opens per user/day   = 10
Photos rendered per open  = 20
Photo reads/day           = 10^8 × 10 × 20       = 2 × 10^10
Average read QPS          = 2 × 10^10 / 10^5     = 200 000/s
Peak read QPS (×5)                               = 1 000 000/s

Read : write ratio        = 200 000 : 200        = 1 000 : 1

Now read the numbers rather than admiring them. 1 000 writes/s is one database's worth of work if the rows are small — no sharding needed for metadata yet. One million image reads per second is not something you serve from your own servers at all; it is a CDN number. And a 1000:1 ratio means every design decision should be biased towards making reads cheap even if writes get more expensive: precompute feeds, denormalise, cache aggressively.

2.5Estimating storage

Storage estimates are where people are most often wrong by 10×, because they compute the size of the data and forget everything that surrounds it.

Formula 
raw       = items/day × bytes/item
stored    = raw × index_overhead × replication_factor / compression
5-year    = stored × 365 × 5 × growth_factor
Index overhead — 1.2× to 2×
Secondary indexes are copies of the indexed columns plus a pointer. A table with four secondary indexes can easily be smaller than its indexes.
Replication — 2× to 3×
You are storing every byte on three machines. This is a hard multiplier on cost that people routinely omit.
Compression — ÷2 to ÷5 for text, ÷1 for media
JSON and logs compress beautifully; JPEG and MP4 are already compressed and will not shrink further.
Growth — 1.5× to 3× over the horizon
You are sizing for the future, and the user base is not static. Growth also means the rate of writes grows, not just the accumulated total.
Everything that is not the row
Write-ahead logs, backups (multiple full copies plus incrementals), snapshots, dead tuples awaiting vacuum, temporary space for rebuilds. A rule of thumb: provision 2× your computed steady-state figure or you will be unable to run a migration.

Continuing the photo service:

Worked example 
Photos                    2 × 10^7/day × 3 MB (original + thumbnails)
                          = 6 × 10^13 B/day     = 60 TB/day
                          × 365 × 5              = 110 PB over five years
                          Media does not compress; replication 1.5×
                          (erasure coding, not 3 copies)  ≈ 165 PB

Metadata                  2 × 10^7/day × 500 B  = 10 GB/day
                          × 1.5 indexes × 3 replicas = 45 GB/day
                          × 365 × 5              = 82 TB over five years

Two conclusions fall straight out. The media is a 100+ petabyte problem, which means object storage with erasure coding and lifecycle tiering to cold storage — old photos that nobody views should not sit on hot storage. The metadata is 82 TB, which is large but tractable: sharded relational or a wide-column store, and comfortably within one team's ability to operate. Splitting a design's storage into "the blobs" and "the facts about the blobs" almost always simplifies it, because the two have completely different cost curves and access patterns.

2.6Estimating bandwidth and cost

Bandwidth is read QPS multiplied by response size. It is the number that most often surprises people, because egress out of a cloud provider is priced per gigabyte and does not benefit from any of the efficiency work you do inside the datacenter.

Worked example 
Peak image reads   1 000 000/s × 200 KB (feed-sized render)
                   = 2 × 10^11 B/s = 200 GB/s = 1.6 Tbps

Monthly egress     200 000/s average × 200 KB × 2.6 × 10^6 s/month
                   = 1.04 × 10^17 B = ~104 PB/month

1.6 Tbps at peak is more than a hundred 10-gigabit network cards, so the images cannot come from your origin — this is the moment a CDN stops being an optimisation and becomes load-bearing. And 104 PB of egress at even $0.02/GB through a CDN is about $2 million a month, which reframes the entire design: cutting average image size by 30% through better formats saves more money than any amount of server tuning.

A cost heuristic that holds up

For media-heavy consumer products, the bill is egress > storage > compute. For data-heavy internal products it is storage > compute > egress. For request-heavy APIs with small payloads it is compute > storage > egress. Work out which of the three you are building and you will know where the optimisation effort belongs before you write any code.

2.7Estimating memory and cache size

Caching decisions need one number: how much of the data is actually hot. Access distributions in real systems are heavily skewed — a Zipf-like curve where a small fraction of items take most of the traffic. The working assumption is the 80/20 rule: 20% of items serve 80% of requests. Often it is closer to 95/5.

Worked example 
Daily distinct items read     ≈ 2 × 10^7  (yesterday's + popular older posts)
Cache the hot 20%             = 4 × 10^6 items
Bytes per cached item         = 1 KB (metadata, not the image)
Cache size                    = 4 GB

  → one Redis node holds this comfortably. Even at 20× the estimate,
    80 GB is a small cluster. Metadata caching is essentially free.
    Caching the images themselves would be 4 × 10^6 × 200 KB = 800 GB,
    which is why images belong in a CDN rather than your cache tier.

The second memory question is per-connection cost on servers. A thread-per-connection server needs roughly 1 MB of stack per connection, so 10 000 concurrent connections is 10 GB before any application data — this is the whole reason for event loops and lightweight threads. An event-driven server needs a few kilobytes per connection and handles hundreds of thousands on one box.

    2.8Turning load into servers

    Once you have peak QPS and a per-node figure, the server count is division plus honesty about redundancy.

    Worked example 
    Peak API QPS                       = 60 000/s
Per-node capacity (Go, ~4 ms work) = 8 000/s
Bare minimum                       = 60 000 / 8 000 = 7.5 → 8 nodes

Run at 60% utilisation             8 / 0.6  = 14 nodes
Survive losing one AZ of three     14 × 1.5 = 21 nodes
Round up for deploys and headroom            24 nodes

    Three multipliers turned 8 into 24. Every one of them is a requirement you already agreed to: latency needs headroom, availability needs a spare zone, and rolling deploys take capacity out of service. When someone asks why the fleet is three times the theoretical minimum, that is the answer.

    Also check the connection arithmetic, which is a separate ceiling people forget. If each of 24 app nodes keeps a pool of 50 connections to the database, that is 1 200 connections — beyond what a Postgres instance handles happily, and the reason connection poolers like PgBouncer exist. Fan-out multiplies: N app servers × M downstream services × P connections each.

    2.9Sanity checks

    Before you use a number, run it past these:

    1. Compare to something known. If your design implies more writes per second than X (the platform) handles globally, one of your inputs is wrong. Useful anchors: X handles ~6 000 posts/s; Google handles ~100 000 searches/s; a busy e-commerce site at peak is a few thousand orders per minute, not per second.
    2. Check the per-user reality. Does 50 writes per user per day describe a human being? For most products the honest number is between 0.1 and 5.
    3. Check the units survived. Bits versus bytes is a factor of eight and appears constantly, because network capacity is quoted in bits and storage in bytes. A 10 Gbps link moves 1.25 GB/s.
    4. Check the total against the population. A design implying 10 billion daily actives has failed a sanity check that costs one second.
    5. Ask what dominates. If one term is 100× the others, the rest is noise and you should say so and stop calculating.
    The five most common estimation errors
    • Averages only. Sizing for average load guarantees failure at peak.
    • Forgetting replication. A 3× multiplier silently omitted from every storage figure.
    • Ignoring metadata and indexes. The row is 200 bytes; the row plus its four indexes plus row overhead is 700.
    • Bits and bytes. Off by 8 in the direction that makes the design look feasible.
    • Straight-line growth. Sizing for today's DAU when the whole reason for the project is that DAU is doubling every six months.

    Note what the last block does. Every line converts a number into a design decision, and the final line notices that the access pattern is now obvious. That is the whole point of the exercise: ten minutes of arithmetic and the architecture has started designing itself.

    A service has 20 million DAU. Each user loads a page 8 times a day and each page makes 5 API calls. What is peak QPS?

    2 × 107 × 8 × 5 = 8 × 108 calls/day. Divide by 105 for 8 000/s average. With a 3× peak factor, 24 000/s at peak. At 8 000 req/s per node that is 3 nodes bare minimum, 5 at 60% utilisation, 8 to survive losing a zone. Ten nodes is the answer you would actually provision.

    Your design stores 1 KB per event, 500 million events per day, retained for two years. How much disk do you buy?

    Raw: 5 × 108 × 103 B = 500 GB/day. Two years = 365 TB raw. Events are usually semi-structured text, so compress by 4× → 91 TB. Add indexes at 1.3× → 118 TB. Replicate 3× → 355 TB. Then double it for operational headroom, backups and the ability to run a compaction or migration → ~700 TB of provisioned capacity. Which is the moment to ask whether two years of raw events must be online, or whether anything older than 30 days can be rolled up and archived — that one product question is worth roughly 600 TB.

    Why is "1 million requests per second" a suspicious number in most interviews?

    Because it implies about 1011 requests a day, which is a handful of companies on earth. Usually it comes from a slipped decimal, a peak factor applied twice, or counting every asset on a page as an API call when they are actually static files served by a CDN. Separating cacheable static requests from dynamic API requests typically drops the API number by one or two orders of magnitude — and that separation is itself a design insight worth stating.

    Recap

    1. A day is 105 seconds. Per-day to per-second is deleting five zeros. A year is π × 107 seconds.
    2. Memory 100 ns, SSD 100 µs, datacenter round trip 500 µs, cross-continent 150 ms. Each step is roughly 100× the last, and the last one is physics.
    3. Round to one significant figure and work in scientific notation. You are choosing between orders of magnitude.
    4. Always apply a peak factor and say which one: 2× global, 3–5× regional, 10× workday tools, 100× event-driven.
    5. Storage = raw × indexes × replication ÷ compression × growth, then double it for operational headroom. Missing the replication factor is the classic 3× error.
    6. Bandwidth × price is often the largest line on the bill for media products. Compute egress cost before optimising anything else.
    7. Servers = peak QPS ÷ per-node capacity ÷ 0.6 × zone redundancy. The minimum is never the answer.
    8. Every number must be followed by the decision it settles. An unread estimate was a waste of the four minutes it took.
