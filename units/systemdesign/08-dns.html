DNS

DNS is the first thing that happens and the last thing anyone thinks about. It is also the largest distributed cache on earth, a load balancer with a very slow control loop, and the single most common cause of "the whole company is offline". This chapter covers how resolution works, why TTL is the only real control you have, and what DNS can and cannot do for traffic management.

~28 min read Resolution · TTL · failover · anycast 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: none

8.1What DNS is really for

The obvious answer is that it turns api.example.com into 203.0.113.7. The useful answer is that it is a layer of indirection between a name that people and code hold onto and an address that you need to be able to change. Servers get replaced, regions get added, providers get swapped; the name must survive all of it. Every property of DNS follows from that job plus one constraint: it must answer billions of queries per second worldwide, which forces aggressive caching, which is the source of every DNS problem you will ever have.

8.2The hierarchy and the players

The namespace is a tree, read right to left: api.example.com. is the api node inside example inside com inside the unnamed root. Four kinds of server participate:

Stub resolver
The tiny client in your operating system or browser. It does not resolve anything; it asks a recursive resolver and caches the answer.
Recursive resolver
Your ISP's, your company's, or a public one (8.8.8.8, 1.1.1.1). It does the actual walking of the tree and holds the cache that matters most — one resolver serves millions of users, so one cached answer suppresses millions of queries.
Root and TLD servers
Thirteen root server addresses (served by hundreds of physical machines via anycast) that know where .com lives; TLD servers that know which nameservers are authoritative for example.com.
Authoritative nameserver
Yours, or your provider's. It holds the actual records and is the only thing that knows the truth. Everything else is a cache of what it said.

8.3Caching and TTL

Every record carries a time-to-live in seconds. Anyone who receives it may cache it for that long, and — this is the part that catches people — you cannot revoke it early. There is no invalidation, no purge, no push. Once an answer is out there with a TTL of 86 400, some resolver somewhere will keep using it for the next day, and some client will keep using it for longer because plenty of software ignores TTL entirely.

Choosing a TTL
TTLQuery load on your authoritative serversTime to change somethingUse for
30–60 sHigh — resolvers re-ask constantlyAbout a minuteRecords used for failover, or during a migration window
300 s (5 min)ModerateMinutesA good default for application endpoints
3 600 s (1 h)LowAn hour, plus stragglersStable records: MX, verification TXT
86 400 s (1 d)MinimalA day, plus stragglers for much longerNS records, things that genuinely never move
Lower the TTL before you need it, not when

The migration playbook every operations team learns once, painfully: at least 2× the current TTL before the change, drop the TTL to 60 seconds. Wait for the old, longer TTL to expire everywhere. Make the change. Verify. Then raise the TTL back up. If you only lower the TTL at the moment you make the change, the low TTL is itself sitting behind the old high TTL and does nothing for you — you still wait out the original day.

Two caching details that cause real incidents. Negative caching means an NXDOMAIN answer is cached too, for a duration set by the minimum field of the zone's SOA record — so if you query a name a moment before you create it, some resolvers will insist it does not exist for the next hour. And every layer caches independently: the browser (Chrome keeps its own DNS cache for about a minute), the OS, the resolver, and often a corporate forwarder in between. Your effective propagation time is the sum of the worst behaviour at each layer, not the TTL you set.

The CNAME-at-apex problem

You want example.com (no www) to point at a load balancer whose address changes. A CNAME would be perfect — and is forbidden, because the apex must also carry NS and SOA records and a CNAME may not coexist with anything. The workarounds are all imperfect: your DNS provider's ALIAS/ANAME pseudo-record (which ties you to that provider), an HTTP redirect from apex to www, or a static anycast IP in front of the changing infrastructure. Worth knowing because it constrains real architectures, and it is a favourite interview detail.

8.5DNS as a traffic manager

Because you control the answer, you control where clients go. This makes DNS the coarsest and most global load-balancing tool you have.

Routing policies offered by managed DNS providers
PolicyMechanismGood for
Round robinSeveral A records; resolvers rotate the orderCrude spreading. No health awareness at all
WeightedAnswers returned in proportion to weightsCanary releases, gradual migration between providers
GeolocationAnswer depends on the resolver's locationData residency, language, regulatory routing
Latency-basedAnswer is the region measured fastest for that clientMulti-region applications where you want the nearest healthy stack
FailoverHealth checks; the record changes when the primary failsDisaster recovery between regions
Multi-value with healthSeveral answers, unhealthy ones withdrawnA middle ground between round robin and real load balancing

All of them share the same four limitations, and understanding these is what separates "use Route 53 for failover" from a design that actually recovers:

  1. The control loop is minutes, not milliseconds. Detection (health check interval × failure threshold) plus TTL plus client cache behaviour. Even a well-tuned setup is 60–120 seconds; DNS is a disaster-recovery mechanism, not a request-level one.
  2. You cannot balance per request. A resolver caches one answer and hands it to everyone behind it for the whole TTL, so load lands in lumps proportional to resolver population, not to your weights.
  3. You are routing resolvers, not users. Geolocation sees the resolver's address, and a user on a public resolver may appear to be somewhere else entirely. EDNS Client Subnet passes a truncated client address to fix this, and not everyone supports it.
  4. Clients ignore you. Java historically cached DNS forever by default; connection pools resolve once at startup and hold the address for weeks; some libraries never re-resolve. A long-lived process is the most common reason a "completed" DNS failover leaves traffic hitting a dead address.

8.6Anycast

The same IP address announced from many locations at once, with the internet's routing protocol (BGP) delivering each packet to the topologically nearest announcement. It is how the root servers, every large DNS provider and every CDN work.

What it buys: latency, because the nearest site answers; capacity, because load spreads geographically for free; and DDoS resilience, because an attack is absorbed by whichever site is nearest to each attacker rather than concentrating on one. What it costs: you need your own address space and BGP relationships, so in practice you rent it from a provider. And it is unreliable for long-lived connections — a routing change mid-session can send your packets to a different site that knows nothing about your TCP state, which is why anycast is mostly used for short, stateless request-response traffic like DNS, and why anycast HTTP relies on the edge site terminating the connection immediately.

8.7DNS on the latency budget

A cold lookup can cost 20–120 ms before a single byte of your application is requested, and it happens before TCP, before TLS, before anything. Then the same page needs lookups for every additional hostname it references. Four mitigations, all cheap:

  • Fewer hostnames. Every distinct domain on a page is a fresh resolution plus a fresh connection. Domain sharding was a useful HTTP/1.1 trick and is actively harmful under HTTP/2.
  • dns-prefetch and preconnect. Tell the browser about a hostname you will need shortly, and the lookup happens in parallel with parsing rather than on the critical path.
  • Connection reuse. Keep-alive means one resolution serves many requests. This is also why HTTP/2 and HTTP/3, which multiplex over one connection, remove most repeat DNS cost.
  • Sensible TTLs on hot names. A 60-second TTL on your busiest hostname means every resolver re-resolves every minute; 300 seconds is usually a better trade unless you are actively failing over.

8.8How DNS takes companies offline

The recurring failure modes

Two protocol-level items to be able to name. DNSSEC signs records so a resolver can verify they came from the real zone owner, defending against cache poisoning; it does not encrypt anything, and misconfigured or expired signatures have themselves caused large outages. DoH and DoT (DNS over HTTPS/TLS) encrypt the query between client and resolver for privacy; they change nothing about how caching or authority works, but they do move resolution out of the network operator's control, which occasionally breaks corporate split-horizon setups where internal names resolve differently inside the network.

You need to move a service to a new region with under a minute of disruption. Walk through the DNS plan.

Days before: confirm the current TTL, then lower it to 60 seconds and wait longer than the old TTL so every cached copy has expired and resolvers are now honouring 60. Before the cutover: bring the new region up, warm its caches, and verify it directly by address or via a test hostname. Cutover: change the record; monitor traffic shifting to the new region and error rates on both. Keep the old region running for at least an hour — probably a day — because long-lived clients will still be connecting to it. Then raise the TTL back to 300. And if the requirement were genuinely sub-second, DNS would be the wrong tool entirely: put an anycast address or a load balancer in front and shift traffic there, where the control loop is a health check rather than a cache expiry.

Round-robin DNS returns three A records with equal weight, but one server gets 60% of the traffic. Why?

Because DNS distributes across resolvers, not users. A single large ISP resolver caches one ordering and hands it to millions of subscribers for the whole TTL, so the split follows the size of the resolver populations, not your record weights. On top of that, some resolvers re-sort answers, some clients always take the first address, happy-eyeballs may prefer whichever answers fastest, and there is no health awareness at all — a dead server keeps receiving its share until you notice. Round-robin DNS is a spreading mechanism, not a balancer; put a real load balancer behind one name and let DNS just find it.

Why would you pay for two DNS providers?

Because DNS failure is total rather than partial. If your only provider has an outage, no name you own resolves — not the website, not the API, not the mail, not the status page if it is on the same domain. The 2016 Dyn attack took a large fraction of well-known internet services offline for exactly this reason. Serving the same zone from two independent providers, with NS records listing both sets of nameservers, means resolvers simply use whichever answers. The cost is that every zone change must be applied to both, which is a solvable automation problem, and it is cheap insurance against the one dependency whose failure has no workaround.

Recap

  1. DNS is indirection plus the world's largest cache. Every quirk follows from that caching.
  2. Only recursive resolvers recurse; your authoritative server sees a tiny fraction of real query volume, which is also why it is easy to under-provision.
  3. TTL is the only control you have and it cannot be revoked. Lower it well before a change, and wait out the old value first.
  4. CNAME cannot live at the apex. Use a provider ALIAS record, a redirect, or a stable anycast address.
  5. DNS traffic management is coarse: minutes of control loop, per-resolver granularity, and clients that ignore TTL. Use it for regional failover, not request balancing.
  6. Anycast gives latency, capacity and DDoS absorption for short stateless exchanges.
  7. A cold lookup costs tens of milliseconds before anything else happens. Reduce hostnames, preconnect, and reuse connections.
  8. Use two DNS providers. There is no partial failure and no workaround.
