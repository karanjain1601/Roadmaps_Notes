How to approach a system design

Most people fail a system design not by choosing the wrong database but by starting to draw before they know what they are building. This chapter is the method: six phases, in order, with the questions to ask and the traps at each one. Everything else in the unit plugs into this skeleton.

~30 min read Foundations of the whole unit 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: none

1.1What system design actually is

System design is deciding how a set of machines, processes and stores of data cooperate to deliver a service with particular qualities — fast enough, available enough, correct enough, cheap enough. It sits above code and below business strategy. You are not choosing variable names, and you are not choosing which market to enter. You are choosing where state lives, who talks to whom, what happens when a piece of it dies, and what the whole thing costs to run.

It is helpful to separate three levels that people casually call "architecture":

Software design
Inside one process. Classes, modules, dependency direction, patterns like strategy or repository. Covered in the Software Design & Architecture unit, not here.
System design
Across processes and machines. Services, databases, caches, queues, load balancers, replication and partitioning. The subject of this unit.
Enterprise architecture
Across systems and organisations. Which systems exist at all, who owns them, how they integrate, procurement and governance.

The boundary matters because the failure modes differ. A bad class hierarchy makes code annoying to change. A bad partition key makes a company's product unavailable on its busiest day of the year, and cannot be fixed in an afternoon. System design decisions are expensive to reverse, which is exactly why they deserve a deliberate method rather than intuition.

The one-line definition worth memorising

A system design is a set of components, the contracts between them, and the reasons each one is there. If you cannot state the reason a box exists, delete the box.

Design in an interview versus design at work

The method below is the same in both, but the emphasis moves. In an interview you have 35–50 minutes, no real users, and the person opposite is scoring your reasoning rather than your answer. At work you have weeks, real traffic data, an existing system with awkward history, and the answer matters more than the reasoning. What transfers completely is the order of operations: requirements before numbers, numbers before boxes, boxes before deep dives. People who skip straight to boxes produce designs that cannot be defended, in both settings.

1.2The method in one picture

A rough time budget for a 45-minute interview: 6 minutes on requirements, 4 on estimates, 5 on the interface, 10 on the high-level design, 15 on deep dives, 5 on failure and wrap-up. The single most common timing mistake is spending 20 minutes drawing an increasingly detailed high-level diagram and never getting to a deep dive, because the deep dive is where the actual signal is.

Time budget

Set the length of your session; the phases scale with it.

1.3Phase 1 — Requirements

Requirements come in two kinds, and conflating them is the most common early error. Functional requirements are what the system does, expressed as capabilities a user would recognise. Non-functional requirements are how well it does them. Functional requirements determine the entities and the API. Non-functional requirements determine the architecture. A tweet-sized product with a billion users needs a far more elaborate system than a complex product with a thousand users, and the difference is entirely non-functional.

Functional requirements: cut hard, then cut again

Start by listing every capability someone might want, then choose three to five to design for and say explicitly that the rest are out of scope. "I am going to design posting, following, and the home timeline. I am treating direct messages, search, ads and moderation as out of scope — tell me if you would rather I include one of them." That sentence buys you the rest of the session and signals that you understand scope control, which is a job skill, not an interview trick.

Write each one as a sentence with a subject and a verb, because verbs become endpoints and subjects become entities:

  • A user uploads a video of up to 10 GB.
  • A viewer streams a video at a resolution their connection supports.
  • A viewer searches videos by title and description.

Three sentences and you already have three entities (user, video, view session), three endpoints, and a strong hint that you will need transcoding, a CDN and a search index. Requirements written as nouns — "video platform, search, comments" — give you none of that.

Non-functional requirements: the eight axes

Every design is a position on these eight axes. You will not get numbers for all of them; guess out loud and let the numbers be corrected.

The non-functional axes, what to ask, and what the answer changes

Trap: the invented requirement

Do not assume "internet scale" because the problem sounds famous. A design for an internal expense tool that opens with sharding and a Kafka cluster is a worse answer than one that opens with a single Postgres instance and a plan for what to do at 10× traffic. Fit the design to the stated numbers and name the point at which it stops working.

Draw the read:write ratio out early

The ratio between reads and writes shapes more of the design than almost any other single number. A 1000:1 read-heavy system (a news site, a social timeline) is a caching and replication problem: you can serve almost everything from copies, and the primary only has to absorb a trickle. A 1:1 or write-heavy system (metrics ingestion, chat, IoT telemetry) is a partitioning and ingest problem: caches barely help, and the write path is the thing you must design carefully. Ask for the ratio; if you cannot get it, estimate it from the product's behaviour and say what you assumed.

Requirements sheet

Fill this in for the system you are designing. The consequences column is what these numbers force you into.

    The clarifying questions that are always worth asking

    1. Who are the users and how many? Consumers, internal staff, other services? Global or one region?
    2. What is the core action? If the system could only do one thing, what is it?
    3. How big is one item of data? A tweet is 300 bytes; a video is 5 GB. This single number swings storage estimates by seven orders of magnitude.
    4. How long must data live? Forever, 90 days, until the user deletes it? Retention drives storage cost and legal exposure more than volume does.
    5. Is the workload spiky? A ticketing system at on-sale time and a payroll system at month-end need capacity plans that an averages-only view will miss entirely.
    6. What is already there? At work this is the most important question of the six. A design that ignores the existing database and team skills is a fantasy.

    1.4Phase 2 — Estimates

    Estimates convert requirements into architecture. You do them second because the answers decide which of the later chapters you need. The full arithmetic is Chapter 2; the point here is why it belongs in the method at all.

    Four numbers do most of the work:

    What each estimate unlocks
    NumberRough formDecision it settles
    Write QPSwrites per user per day × DAU ÷ 86 400Whether one primary can absorb writes; whether you need a queue or partitioning
    Read QPSwrite QPS × read:write ratioReplica count, cache hit ratio you need, CDN necessity
    Storage in 5 yearswrites/day × bytes/item × 365 × 5 × replicationOne machine, one shard set, or a tiered store with cold archive
    Peak bandwidthread QPS × bytes per responseWhether the CDN is the design or an afterthought; egress cost

    Two rules make estimates useful rather than theatrical. First, round aggressively — 86 400 seconds in a day becomes 100 000, a million becomes 106, and you work in powers of ten. You are deciding between "one box" and "a thousand boxes", so a 20% error is irrelevant and an order-of-magnitude error is fatal. Second, say what the number means the moment you have it. "40 000 writes per second — that is well past what a single Postgres primary will do comfortably, so writes need partitioning" is worth ten times more than the number alone.

    1.5Phase 3 — Interface and data model

    Before any boxes, define the contract. This forces precision: you cannot write an endpoint signature without deciding what an entity is, what identifies it, and what the client is allowed to ask for. It also gives the rest of the design something concrete to serve.

    Keep it to the few endpoints that carry the core requirements, and write them in whatever shorthand is fastest:

    HTTP 
    POST   /v1/videos                      -> { videoId, uploadUrl }
       body: { title, description, sizeBytes, contentType }
       header: Idempotency-Key: <uuid>

PUT    <uploadUrl>                     -> 200            # direct to object store
POST   /v1/videos/{id}:publish         -> { status }

GET    /v1/videos/{id}                 -> { video, manifestUrl }
GET    /v1/videos?q=&cursor=&limit=20  -> { items[], nextCursor }
GET    /v1/users/{id}/feed?cursor=     -> { items[], nextCursor }

    Four things to get right in that sketch, because each one is a decision an interviewer will probe and a production system will punish:

    Cursor pagination, not offsets
    ?page=500 forces the database to count and discard 10 000 rows and produces duplicates when the list changes underneath. An opaque cursor encoding the last seen sort key is O(1) and stable.
    Idempotency on unsafe writes
    Any client will retry a request whose response it never saw. Without an idempotency key you get double charges and duplicate posts. Covered fully in Chapter 16.
    Large payloads bypass your servers
    Uploading a 10 GB video through your API tier wastes bandwidth and ties up connections. Issue a pre-signed URL and let the client talk to the object store directly.
    Versioning from day one
    /v1/ costs nothing now and saves a migration later.

    Then sketch the entities. Not a full schema — the fields that identify things and the relationships between them, because these determine what can be partitioned and what has to be joined:

    Schema sketch 
    user(user_id PK, handle UNIQUE, created_at)
video(video_id PK, owner_id FK, title, status, duration_s, created_at)
       index on (owner_id, created_at DESC)
view(video_id, user_id, watched_at, seconds)      # append-only, huge
follow(follower_id, followee_id)                  # the fan-out relation

    Ask of every table: how big does it get, what queries hit it, and what would I partition it by? The view table above is a billion rows a day and is never queried by primary key — that is a time-series or analytics store, not a row in your relational database. Noticing that at the schema stage saves you from designing around a table that should never have existed.

    1.6Phase 4 — The high-level design

    Now draw. Most systems are a subset of one skeleton, and knowing the skeleton means you spend your thinking on the parts that are specific to this problem rather than rediscovering that load balancers exist.

    Work through it in a fixed order so nothing is forgotten:

    1. Client to edge. How do requests arrive? Is content cacheable at the edge? Is there a mobile client with different constraints?
    2. Entry and routing. Load balancer, TLS termination, gateway concerns — authentication, rate limiting, request size limits.
    3. The stateless tier. One service or several? What is the split, and is the split justified by team boundaries or scaling differences rather than fashion?
    4. State. Which store holds each entity, and why that store. This is where most of the marks are.
    5. The asynchronous path. What can be moved off the request? Anything the user does not need to see the result of immediately: transcoding, thumbnails, emails, fan-out, indexing, analytics.
    6. The read path and the write path, separately. Trace one write from client to durable storage, then one read from client to response. Say where each one can be slow, and where each one can fail.
    Trace the two paths out loud

    "A write: client POSTs, gateway authenticates, service validates and writes to the primary, publishes an event, returns 202. A worker consumes the event, updates the search index and fans out to followers' timeline caches." Two sentences like that demonstrate more understanding than another ten boxes on the diagram, because they expose ordering, failure points and consistency in one pass.

    Choosing what to make asynchronous

    The question is simple: does the user need the result of this work to consider their action complete? Publishing a post: yes, the post must be durable before you return success. Generating its thumbnails, notifying 40 000 followers, updating the search index, recomputing trending topics: no. Each of those moves behind a queue, which converts a slow, failure-prone, coupled request into a fast one plus retryable background work. It also converts strong consistency into eventual consistency for those derived views, which you must then be honest about — the search index will lag by a second or two, and if that is unacceptable you have to pay for it somewhere else.

    1.7Phase 5 — Deep dives

    The high-level design is table stakes. The deep dive is the interview, and at work it is the part of the design document that people actually argue about. Choose the topics by looking for where risk concentrates.

    Finding the deep dive worth doing
    Look forSymptom in your own diagramDive into
    The bottleneckOne component takes the largest share of QPS or bytesHow you shard, cache or precompute it
    A single point of failureA box with no replica and no fallbackReplication, failover, and what a client sees during it
    SkewA key that some users have far more of — a celebrity, a viral itemHot-key handling, fan-out on read vs write
    A consistency seamTwo stores that must agree but are written separatelyOutbox, sagas, reconciliation jobs
    An unbounded resourceA table or queue that only ever growsRetention, tiering, compaction, back-pressure
    The expensive thingThe component that dominates the billCheaper storage class, better cache hit ratio, batching

    A deep dive has a shape. State the problem in numbers. Give two or three real options. Compare them on the axes that matter for this system. Pick one and name what you gave up.

    A deep dive, compressed

    "The home timeline is the bottleneck: 100 k reads/s, and a pull-based query across 500 follows would be 50 million row reads per second. So I precompute. On write I push the post id into each follower's timeline list in Redis — that is 40 k writes/s × average 200 followers = 8 M list appends/s, which is a lot but shardable. The exception is accounts with millions of followers, where push is hopeless; for those I keep the pull path and merge at read time. So: fan-out on write for the long tail, fan-out on read for celebrities, merged in the read API. The cost is a hybrid code path and timelines that are eventually consistent by a second or two."

    1.8Phase 6 — Failure, operations and cost

    Designs are judged on what happens when things go wrong, and this is the phase people skip. Five minutes here changes the impression of the whole design.

    • Kill each box in turn. "If the cache tier dies, every read goes to the database, which is 100× its provisioned load — so I need request coalescing and a load shedder, or the outage becomes total." That sentence is a whole grade of seniority.
    • Name the blast radius. Does one bad shard take down one shard's users or everyone? Cell-based designs limit this deliberately.
    • Say how you would know. Which metric moves first when this breaks? What would you alert on? See Chapter 18.
    • Say what it costs. A rough monthly figure — storage, egress, compute — separates people who have run systems from people who have only drawn them. Egress is usually the surprise.
    • Say how you would roll it out. Migrations, dual writes, backfills, feature flags. A design with no migration story is not deployable into a system that already exists.

    1.9Talking about trade-offs properly

    "It depends" is not a trade-off. A trade-off is a sentence with a shape: I chose X over Y, which buys me A at the cost of B, and B is acceptable here because C. Practise the shape until it is automatic.

    Trade-offs that recur in almost every design
    ChoiceBuysCostsChoose the first when
    Precompute vs compute on readFast, predictable readsWrite amplification, staleness, storageReads vastly outnumber writes and the result is reused
    Normalise vs denormaliseOne copy of the truth, cheap writesJoins at read time, cross-shard queriesData changes often and read volume is modest
    Strong vs eventual consistencySimple reasoning, no stale readsLatency, availability during partitionsMoney, inventory, permissions, identity
    Synchronous vs asynchronousImmediate confirmation, simple errorsCoupling, tail latency, cascading failureThe user must know the outcome now
    Vertical vs horizontal scalingNo distribution problems at allA hard ceiling and a single failure domainYou are below the ceiling and the team is small
    Monolith vs servicesCheap transactions, one deploy, easy local devCoupled scaling, one blast radiusThe team fits in a room and the domains are unclear
    SQL vs NoSQLTransactions, ad-hoc queries, constraintsHarder horizontal scaling of writesAccess patterns are unknown or relationships matter

    1.10What is actually being scored

    Interview rubrics vary, but they converge on five signals. Knowing them tells you where to spend your minutes.

    1. Requirements and scoping. Did you find the real problem before solving one? Did you control scope explicitly?
    2. Sound fundamentals. Do the numbers work? Are the components used for what they are actually good at? Would this run?
    3. Depth in at least one area. Can you go three levels down on something rather than one level down on everything?
    4. Trade-off reasoning. Do you know what you gave up, and can you defend the choice against a well-argued alternative?
    5. Communication. Is the design legible? Do you check in, respond to hints, and change your mind when given new information — without abandoning a good decision at the first push-back?
    The five ways people lose the room
    • Buzzword architecture. Kafka, Kubernetes and a service mesh for a system with 200 requests per second.
    • No numbers. Every claim about scale is an adjective. "Lots of traffic" cannot be designed for.
    • Boxes without contracts. Twelve rectangles and no statement of what data crosses which arrow.
    • Ignoring the interviewer. A hint is not conversation; it is the question changing. Follow it.
    • Silence. Thinking quietly for two minutes is invisible. Narrate the options you are weighing.

    1.11The method applied, compressed: a pastebin

    A short worked pass, to see the shape end to end. Full-length versions of ten problems are in Chapter 22.

    Requirements

    Create a text snippet, get back a short URL, read it by URL. Optional expiry. Out of scope: editing, accounts, syntax highlighting, private pastes. Non-functional: 10 M new pastes/day, read-heavy at roughly 100:1, snippets up to 1 MB but typically 10 KB, 99.9% availability, reads should be fast globally, pastes are immutable so any staleness is irrelevant.

    Estimates

    10 M writes/day ÷ 100 k s ≈ 100 writes/s, so 10 000 reads/s. Storage: 10 M × 10 KB = 100 GB/day, 36 TB/year — too much for one node's local disk over five years, and it is blob-shaped data, so bodies go in an object store and metadata in a database. Metadata is ~200 bytes × 10 M = 2 GB/day, 730 GB/year — comfortably one relational database with room to spare.

    Interface

    HTTP 
    POST /v1/pastes   { content, expiresAt? }  -> { id, url }
GET  /v1/pastes/{id}                       -> { content, createdAt, expiresAt }

    Key decision: the id

    Seven characters of base62 gives 627 ≈ 3.5 × 1012 ids — 350 000 days of supply at 10 M/day. Random-and-check risks collisions and an extra read on every write; a counter encoded in base62 is collision-free but sequential and therefore guessable and enumerable. Choose a pre-generated key range per server (each server claims a block of a million ids from a coordinator, hands them out locally), which is collision-free, needs no read on the write path, and is not sequential across the fleet. Cost: a small coordination service and some wasted ids when a server dies.

    High-level design

    Client → CDN (reads of popular pastes, cached by id since content is immutable — set a long Cache-Control and you may never see the request) → load balancer → stateless API → metadata in a relational database, body in object storage. A cache in front of metadata for the hot tail. Expiry by a periodic sweeper plus a check at read time, because sweepers lag.

    Deep dive and failure

    The pressure point is read amplification on a viral paste: one link on a popular forum can be a million requests in a minute against a single id. Immutability makes this easy — the CDN absorbs essentially all of it, and the origin sees one request per PoP per TTL. If the object store is unavailable, reads of uncached pastes fail while writes can still be accepted into a queue for later persistence, or you fail writes fast and stay honest; either is defensible if stated. Monthly cost is dominated by storage growth and CDN egress, not compute, which suggests aggressive expiry defaults are a product decision with a real financial effect.

    That is the whole method in eight paragraphs. Every later chapter in this unit is a way of doing one of those paragraphs better.

    1.12The one-page checklist

    Say these things, in this order
    PhaseSay out loud
    RequirementsCore features I am designing · features I am excluding · users, scale, growth · read:write ratio · latency and availability targets · consistency and durability needs
    EstimatesWrite QPS · read QPS · storage at 1 and 5 years · peak bandwidth · what each number rules out
    InterfaceThree to six endpoints · pagination style · idempotency · the entities and their relationships
    High levelThe boxes and why each exists · the write path traced · the read path traced · what is asynchronous and why
    Deep diveThe bottleneck named in numbers · two or three options · the choice and what it cost
    WrapEach component's failure and its blast radius · what you would monitor and alert on · rough cost · how you would migrate to it
    Why do requirements come before estimates, and estimates before boxes?

    Because each phase constrains the next, and the constraint only flows in that direction. Requirements decide which numbers matter (a durability requirement makes replication factor relevant; without it you would not compute it). The numbers decide the architecture (40 000 writes/s rules out a single primary; 40 writes/s rules out sharding as premature). If you draw first, the boxes come from memory of other systems rather than from this system's needs, and you end up defending a design you chose before you understood the problem.

    You are asked to design a system and given no numbers at all. What do you do?

    Propose numbers and get them confirmed. "I will assume 1 million daily active users, each doing 5 writes and 200 reads a day, and that this needs to work globally — does that match what you have in mind?" This is better than asking an open question because it shows you know which quantities matter and gives the other person something cheap to correct. Then keep the assumptions visible, because a later deep dive may depend on one of them, and if it is wrong you want to notice rather than build on it.

    Your high-level design is finished with 20 minutes left. Where do you spend them?

    On the component with the highest load or the least redundancy, in that order. Compute which box takes the most QPS or bytes and dive there; if everything is comfortably loaded, dive on whichever box has no replica. Failing both, ask: "the part I am least sure about is X — would you rather I go deeper there or cover failure modes across the whole system?" Handing the choice back is a legitimate move and usually gets you steered to what is actually being assessed.

    Recap

    1. Six phases, in order: requirements, estimates, interface and data, high-level design, deep dives, failure and operations. Skipping forward produces designs you cannot defend.
    2. Functional requirements shape the API; non-functional requirements shape the architecture. The eight axes are scale, traffic shape, latency, availability, consistency, durability, security, cost.
    3. Read:write ratio is the highest-leverage single number. Read-heavy means caching and replication; write-heavy means partitioning and ingest.
    4. Round hard when estimating. You are choosing between orders of magnitude, and every estimate should be followed by the decision it settles.
    5. Trace the write path and the read path out loud. Two sentences of data flow beat five more boxes.
    6. The deep dive is where the signal is. Find the bottleneck, the single point of failure or the skew, then give options and pick with a stated cost.
    7. Finish with failure. Kill each box in turn, name the blast radius, say what you would see on a dashboard, and estimate the bill.
