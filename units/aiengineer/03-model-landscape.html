Choosing a model

Model names change every few months, so memorising a leaderboard is worthless. What lasts is the structure of the market, the axes that actually decide a choice, and a selection method you can re-run in an afternoon whenever something new lands.

Every provider sells the same three tiers

Underneath the branding, the market has settled into a ladder. Learn the ladder and any new release slots straight into it.

TierWhat it is forRelative costTypical use in a product
Frontier / reasoningThe hardest tasks: multi-step problems, complex code, planning, genuinely ambiguous judgement10–60× the small tierA small slice of traffic, routed deliberately. Offline analysis. Generating evaluation data and few-shot examples for cheaper models.
Workhorse / balancedThe default: chat, retrieval answering, drafting, tool use, most agent loops3–10× the small tierThe bulk of user-facing requests in most applications.
Small / fastClassification, routing, extraction from short text, reranking, guardrail checks, summarising one paragraphBaselineHigh-volume internal steps. Often 80% of your call count and 15% of your bill.
The ratios move, the structure does not. Every serious provider offers all three, plus embedding models and usually speech and vision.

The most common architectural mistake in a first AI product is using one tier for everything. A support assistant might use the small tier to classify intent and extract an order number, the workhorse tier to answer from retrieved documents, and the frontier tier only when the user has been through three failed turns. That is three models in one feature, and it is normal.

The axes that decide it

Quality is what everyone argues about and rarely the deciding factor, because at a given tier the leading models are close enough that your prompt and retrieval matter more. These are the axes that actually eliminate candidates.

Context window
How much you can send. Matters most for document work and long agent runs. Check the separate output cap too — a large input window with a small output cap will truncate long generations.
Knowledge cutoff
Everything after it must come from retrieval or tools. Relevant if your domain moves fast, irrelevant if you were going to ground the answer anyway.
Latency
Two numbers: time to first token, and tokens per second. An interactive feature needs a fast first token; a background job does not care.
Price
Separate input and output rates, output typically 3–5× input. Cached input is much cheaper. Compute cost per request, not per million tokens (chapter 5).
Tool-use quality
Whether it reliably picks the right function, fills arguments correctly, and can chain several. Varies far more between models than raw text quality does, and it is the thing agents live on.
Structured output
Whether the provider offers guaranteed schema conformance rather than best-effort JSON. This one feature removes an entire class of production bugs (chapter 7).
Modalities
Image input is now common; audio and video input are not. Check both directions — reading an image and producing one are different products.
Rate limits
Requests and tokens per minute, per tier. This kills more launches than quality does. Ask about the ceiling before you design a batch job.
Data handling
Retention period, whether inputs may be used for training, region of processing, and available certifications. Usually decided by your legal team, not you — find out early.
Deprecation policy
How long a named version stays available and how much notice you get. Determines how often you will be re-running your evals under time pressure.

Start hosted, keep a seam

Build on a hosted API, but put every model call behind one interface in your codebase (chapter 4). Migration later is then a day of work rather than a quarter. Do not build a heavyweight abstraction that supports every provider's every feature — you will lose access to the good parts of each. One thin seam, one adapter per provider you actually use.

Reading benchmarks without being fooled

Public benchmarks are useful for coarse tiering and almost useless for choosing between two models for your task. Understand what each kind measures before quoting it in a decision document.

KindExamplesTells youDoes not tell you
Knowledge and reasoning suitesBroad multi-subject question sets, graduate-level science questionsRough capability tierAnything about your domain, and increasingly contaminated by training data
Code benchmarksFunction-completion sets, repository-level issue-fixing setsWhether it can code at all, and how well it handles real reposHow it does in your language, framework and codebase conventions
Human preference arenasHead-to-head anonymous votingGeneral likeability of the output styleCorrectness. Voters reward confident, well-formatted answers, which is the exact bias you are fighting.
Long-context testsNeedle-in-a-haystack and variantsWhether retrieval across a big window works at allWhether reasoning across many scattered facts works — usually much weaker than single-needle recall
Tool and agent benchmarksFunction-calling suites, multi-step task environmentsThe most decision-relevant public signal if you are building agentsYour tool schemas, which are what actually causes failures

Three cautions worth internalising. Contamination: popular benchmarks leak into training data, so scores drift up without capability moving. Saturation: once everyone scores 92%, the remaining 8% is mostly label noise and the ranking is meaningless. Selective reporting: launch posts compare against carefully chosen baselines under carefully chosen settings.

The only benchmark that decides anything is yours: twenty to fifty real cases from your product, graded the way you actually care about. Building it takes a day and it will serve you for years. Chapter 18 is the full treatment; the next section is the minimum version.

A selection procedure you can repeat

This takes roughly a day the first time and two hours every time after.

  1. Write the task down properly. One paragraph: the input, the required output, the constraints, and what "wrong" means. If you cannot write it, no model will do it consistently.
  2. Collect 20–50 real cases. Actual inputs from your product or its predecessor. Deliberately include the awkward ones: empty inputs, wrong language, hostile input, the ambiguous case your best support agent argues about.
  3. Write the expected outcome for each. Not necessarily an exact string — a rule. "Must classify as billing", "must include the refund window in days", "must decline to answer", "must call lookup_order exactly once".
  4. Pick three candidates. One frontier, one workhorse, one small. Different providers if you can. Same prompt for all, with only the minimal per-model adjustments.
  5. Run each case three times. Variance is data. A model that is right twice out of three is not an 80% model, it is a coin flip you will be paged about.
  6. Record four numbers per model: pass rate, p95 latency, cost per request, and failure character — does it fail loudly or invent something plausible?
  7. Choose the cheapest model that clears your bar, not the best one. Then re-check whether a better prompt closes the gap for the tier below it; it usually does.
  8. Pin the exact version string and record the date, the scores and the prompt in your repository.
bench.py — the smallest useful model bake-off
import time, statistics, json
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor

CASES = json.load(open("cases.json"))   # [{"input":..., "check":"billing"}, ...]

CANDIDATES = [
    {"name": "small",     "model": "provider/small-v1",     "in_$M": 0.10, "out_$M": 0.40},
    {"name": "workhorse", "model": "provider/workhorse-v2", "in_$M": 1.00, "out_$M": 5.00},
    {"name": "frontier",  "model": "provider/frontier-v2",  "in_$M": 5.00, "out_$M": 25.00},
]

def run_case(client, model, case):
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    r = client.complete(model=model, system=SYSTEM, user=case["input"], temperature=0)
    return {
        "ok":      case["check"] in r.text.lower(),   # replace with a real grader
        "seconds": time.perf_counter() - t0,
        "in_tok":  r.usage.input_tokens,
        "out_tok": r.usage.output_tokens,
    }

for c in CANDIDATES:
    runs = []
    with ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=8) as pool:
        for _ in range(3):                            # three passes: variance is data
            runs += list(pool.map(lambda case: run_case(client, c["model"], case), CASES))

    lat  = sorted(r["seconds"] for r in runs)
    cost = statistics.mean(
        r["in_tok"] / 1e6 * c["in_$M"] + r["out_tok"] / 1e6 * c["out_$M"] for r in runs
    )
    print(f'{c["name"]:10} pass={sum(r["ok"] for r in runs)/len(runs):.0%} '
          f'p95={lat[int(len(lat)*0.95)]:.2f}s  ${cost*1000:.2f}/1k requests')

Output from a real run of this on a classification task looks something like:

terminal
small      pass=91%  p95=0.61s  $0.34/1k requests
workhorse  pass=97%  p95=1.42s  $3.10/1k requests
frontier   pass=98%  p95=6.85s  $19.40/1k requests

That table makes the decision for you, and it is the shape you get on most well-specified tasks: the small model is close, the workhorse is comfortably good, and the frontier model costs six times more for one extra point that is inside the noise. Ship the workhorse. Then spend an hour improving the prompt for the small model and see whether it reaches 97% — often it does, and you have just cut your bill by 90%.

Version pinning and deprecation

Providers offer both floating aliases (latest, or a bare family name) and dated versions. Use dated versions in production, always.

Floating alias

  • Your behaviour changes without a deploy.
  • A prompt tuned for one revision can regress on the next.
  • Incidents become unattributable — nothing in your repository changed.

Fine for prototypes and internal tools. Not for anything a customer depends on.

Pinned version

  • Behaviour changes only when you change it.
  • Upgrades become a normal pull request with eval results attached.
  • You can bisect an incident.

The cost is that you must actually watch deprecation notices and schedule upgrades.

models.py — one place that names every model you use
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class ModelSpec:
    id: str              # exact, dated version string
    max_input: int
    max_output: int
    in_per_m: float      # USD per million input tokens
    out_per_m: float
    cached_in_per_m: float | None = None
    supports_tools: bool = True
    supports_schema: bool = True

MODELS = {
    "route":   ModelSpec("provider/small-v1-2026-04-01",     128_000,  4_096, 0.10, 0.40, 0.01),
    "answer":  ModelSpec("provider/workhorse-v2-2026-05-20", 200_000,  8_192, 1.00, 5.00, 0.10),
    "hard":    ModelSpec("provider/frontier-v2-2026-05-20",  200_000, 32_000, 5.00, 25.00, 0.50),
    "embed":   ModelSpec("provider/embed-v3-2026-01-15",       8_192,      0, 0.02, 0.00),
}

The upgrade trap

Teams pin diligently, then get a 60-day deprecation notice for a model their prompts were tuned against over a year, and discover they have no eval set to check the replacement with. Build the eval set in step 2 of the selection procedure and keep it green in CI. The upgrade then costs an afternoon instead of a fortnight.

When you need more than one provider

Two legitimate reasons, and one bad one.

Availability. Provider outages happen and they take your feature down with them. A fallback to a second provider — same interface, different vendor, slightly worse prompt — converts an outage into degraded quality. Chapter 19 has the implementation.

Task fit. Different models genuinely lead at different things, and the cheapest embedding or rerank model is rarely from the same vendor as your best chat model. Mixing is normal and healthy.

The bad reason is vendor-neutrality as an ideal, pursued by building an abstraction that supports the lowest common denominator of every provider. You lose prompt caching, structured output guarantees, and provider-specific tool features — all of which are exactly the things that make a system cheap and reliable. Keep a thin seam, accept a per-provider adapter, and let each adapter use the good parts.

The short version

  1. Every provider sells the same ladder: frontier, workhorse, small. Use all three in one product; routing is normal.
  2. Quality rarely decides. Rate limits, data handling, tool-use reliability, structured output and latency eliminate candidates first.
  3. Public benchmarks give you tiering, nothing more. Twenty to fifty of your own cases is the only measurement that decides anything.
  4. Pick the cheapest model that clears your bar, then try to close the gap for the tier below with a better prompt.
  5. Pin dated versions, keep every model string in one file, and keep an eval set green so upgrades are an afternoon.
  6. Start hosted, keep one thin seam in your code, and mix providers for availability and task fit — not for ideology.
