Chapter 09 · Knowledge and retrieval

Embeddings

A separate, much smaller model turns text into a fixed-length vector whose geometry encodes meaning. Everything in the next four chapters — search, recommendation, deduplication, retrieval-augmented generation — rests on that one idea and on knowing precisely what it does not capture.

vectors cosine similarity model choice dimensions use cases pitfalls

What a vector actually is

An embedding model takes a piece of text and returns a list of numbers — typically 384 to 3,072 of them — such that texts with similar meanings land close together in that space. It is not a language model. It does not generate. It runs in milliseconds and costs roughly a hundredth of a generation call.

python — the whole API surface
r = client.embeddings.create(model="text-embedding-3-small",
                             input=["My card was charged twice",
                                    "I was billed two times for one order",
                                    "The handle snapped off the pan"])

a, b, c = [d.embedding for d in r.data]      # three lists of 1536 floats

cosine(a, b)   # 0.91 - same meaning, almost no shared vocabulary
cosine(a, c)   # 0.13 - different topic entirely

That first pair is the point. Keyword search finds nothing in common between "charged twice" and "billed two times" — no shared terms beyond stopwords. The embedding puts them next to each other because the model learned, from enormous quantities of text, that those phrases occur in the same contexts.

The dimensions are not interpretable, but they behave as if they were

Nobody can tell you what dimension 412 means. Empirically, though, directions in the space correspond to consistent semantic contrasts, which is why the geometry works. The demo below uses a small hand-built space with labelled dimensions so you can see the mechanism; real embeddings do the same thing across hundreds of dimensions nobody named.

Measuring closeness

MetricFormulaRangeUse when
Cosine similaritya·b / (‖a‖‖b‖)−1 to 1Almost always. It measures direction and ignores magnitude, so a long document is not favoured over a short one
Dot producta·bunboundedWhen vectors are already normalised — then it is identical to cosine and faster to compute
Euclidean (L2)‖a−b‖0 upward, lower is betterClustering, and index types that require it. On normalised vectors it ranks identically to cosine
Normalise once at write time and the choice mostly stops mattering. Mixing metrics between indexing and querying, however, silently ruins results.
python — normalise once, then dot product is all you need
import numpy as np

def normalise(v: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    return v / np.linalg.norm(v, axis=-1, keepdims=True)

vectors = normalise(np.array(raw_embeddings, dtype=np.float32))
scores  = vectors @ query_vector          # == cosine, because everything is unit length
top     = np.argpartition(-scores, 10)[:10]
Similarity scores are not probabilities

A cosine of 0.82 does not mean 82% relevant, and the useful threshold differs per model — some produce a narrow band around 0.7–0.9 for everything, others spread widely. Never hard-code a global cutoff from intuition. Sample a few hundred query-document pairs from your own corpus, label them, and pick the threshold from that distribution. Better still, use relative ranking and a reranker (chapter 10) rather than an absolute threshold at all.

What embeddings do not capture

Every one of these has caused a production incident somewhere. They are properties of the mechanism, not bugs to be fixed.

Blind spotExampleMitigation
Negation"refunds are available" and "refunds are not available" are near-identical vectorsRerank with a cross-encoder, which reads both texts together (chapter 10)
Numbers and quantities"under 30 days" and "under 90 days" are almost indistinguishableExtract numeric constraints into metadata filters, not vector space
Dates and recencyThe 2019 policy and the 2026 policy embed identicallyStore dates as metadata; filter or boost by them
Exact identifiersOrder NW-8842 versus NW-8942; a specific error code or SKUKeyword search. This is what BM25 is for, and it is why hybrid search exists
Rare domain jargonInternal product names the embedding model has never seenHybrid search, or a domain-tuned embedding model
Who did what to whom"Anna refunded Ben" versus "Ben refunded Anna"Rerank; keep the structured facts in a database, not in prose
Long documentsA 40-page manual averaged into one vector matches everything weaklyChunking. The whole of chapter 11
Similar is not relevant

Embeddings measure topical similarity. A question and its answer are often not topically similar — "how do I cancel?" and "Navigate to Settings, choose Subscription, press End plan" share little surface meaning. This is why retrieval quality depends so heavily on how you chunk (chapter 11) and how you transform the query (chapter 12), and why a pure vector search rarely wins alone.

Choosing an embedding model

AxisWhat to consider
DimensionsMore is not automatically better. 384–768 is plenty for most corpora and costs a quarter of the storage and memory of 3,072. Some models support Matryoshka truncation: train at 3,072, truncate to 512 with graceful degradation, decide per use case
Max input lengthTypically 512–8,192 tokens. Anything longer is silently truncated by some SDKs — check, because a truncated chunk embeds the wrong content without an error
Symmetric or asymmetricSymmetric models compare like with like (find similar tickets). Asymmetric models are trained for short query against long passage — the right choice for search and RAG. Some require prefixes such as query: and passage:; omitting them costs real accuracy
MultilingualIf your content or your users are not all English, use a multilingual model and test cross-language retrieval explicitly — a German question should find an English passage
DomainCode, legal, biomedical and multilingual retrieval all have specialist models that outperform general ones on their own material
Hosted or localEmbedding models are small. Running one locally is genuinely easy, removes per-token cost, and keeps content in your perimeter
QuantisationSome models support int8 or binary output. Binary embeddings cut storage 32× and stay surprisingly accurate when used as a first pass, rescored with full vectors

Public leaderboards are a starting shortlist, no more — the same contamination and saturation problems as LLM benchmarks apply, and a model tuned on a benchmark's domain will not necessarily hold up on your support tickets. Take three candidates and run the evaluation in chapter 13 against fifty of your own queries.

Changing the model means re-embedding everything

Vectors from two different models are not comparable, at all. Switching models — or even changing the truncation length or the query prefix — means re-embedding the entire corpus and rebuilding the index. Budget for it: for a million chunks it is a few hours and a modest bill, but it is not a config change. Store the model name and version alongside every vector so you can tell what needs rebuilding.

Generating embeddings well

embed.py — batching, retries, caching and provenance
import hashlib, json
import numpy as np

MODEL = "text-embedding-3-small"
DIMS  = 1536
BATCH = 256                                   # far faster and cheaper than one at a time

def content_key(text: str) -> str:
    """Cache on content + model: re-running an ingest should cost nothing."""
    return hashlib.sha256(f"{MODEL}:{DIMS}:{text}".encode()).hexdigest()

def embed_texts(texts: list[str]) -> np.ndarray:
    out: dict[int, list[float]] = {}
    todo: list[tuple[int, str]] = []

    for i, t in enumerate(texts):                       # 1. serve what we already have
        hit = cache.get(content_key(t))
        if hit is not None:
            out[i] = hit
        else:
            todo.append((i, t))

    for start in range(0, len(todo), BATCH):            # 2. batch the rest
        chunk = todo[start:start + BATCH]
        resp = with_retries(lambda: client.embeddings.create(
            model=MODEL, input=[t for _, t in chunk], dimensions=DIMS))
        for (i, t), d in zip(chunk, resp.data):
            cache.set(content_key(t), d.embedding)
            out[i] = d.embedding

    vecs = np.array([out[i] for i in range(len(texts))], dtype=np.float32)
    return vecs / np.linalg.norm(vecs, axis=1, keepdims=True)     # 3. normalise once


# Asymmetric models need their prefixes. Getting this wrong quietly costs accuracy.
def embed_query(q: str)   -> np.ndarray: return embed_texts([f"query: {q}"])[0]
def embed_passage(p: str) -> np.ndarray: return embed_texts([f"passage: {p}"])[0]

The content-hash cache is worth more than it looks. Ingestion pipelines get re-run constantly — a parser fix, a metadata change, a failed job resumed — and without caching you pay to embed the same unchanged paragraphs every time. With it, a re-run of a mostly-unchanged corpus is nearly free.

The five jobs embeddings do well

1. Semantic search

Find passages that mean what the query means, not that contain its words. The foundation of RAG, and covered end to end in the next three chapters.

2. Recommendation and "more like this"

Embed items — products, articles, tickets — and recommend nearest neighbours. It works from content alone, which makes it immune to the cold-start problem that afflicts collaborative filtering: a product added an hour ago with no interactions still has a good vector.

python — recommendations from a user's history
# Represent taste as the mean of what they engaged with, recency-weighted.
weights = np.array([0.5 ** (age_days / 30) for age_days in ages], dtype=np.float32)
profile = normalise((item_vectors * weights[:, None]).sum(axis=0))

scores = catalogue @ profile
scores[already_seen] = -1                       # never recommend what they have
top = np.argsort(-scores)[:20]

# Diversify, or every recommendation is a near-duplicate of the last purchase.
picked = []
for i in top:
    if all(catalogue[i] @ catalogue[j] < 0.92 for j in picked):
        picked.append(i)
    if len(picked) == 5:
        break

3. Deduplication and clustering

Near-duplicate detection — the same bug reported five ways, the same product listed by three sellers — is a similarity threshold away. Clustering the vectors of a week's support tickets and summarising each cluster with an LLM is one of the highest-value, lowest-effort analytics features you can ship.

4. Classification without training a classifier

Embed a few labelled examples per class, then classify a new item by nearest neighbours. For a fixed label set with a hundred examples, this beats prompting an LLM on cost by orders of magnitude, runs in a millisecond, and is trivially updated by adding examples.

python — k-NN classification over embeddings
from collections import Counter

def classify(text: str, k: int = 7) -> tuple[str, float]:
    q = embed_texts([text])[0]
    scores = labelled_vectors @ q                       # labelled_vectors: (n, dims), normalised
    top = np.argsort(-scores)[:k]

    votes = Counter(labels[i] for i in top)
    label, count = votes.most_common(1)[0]
    return label, count / k                             # a usable confidence signal

label, confidence = classify(ticket)
if confidence < 0.6:
    label = llm_classify(ticket)                        # escalate only the uncertain ones

5. Anomaly detection

Text far from every cluster centroid is unusual: a support ticket unlike anything seen before, a log line that does not match known patterns, a product listing that does not belong in its category. Cheap to compute continuously, and a good trigger for human review.

Storing vectors

Before reaching for a specialist database, size the problem honestly.

CorpusStorage at 1,536 dims (float32)Reasonable approach
1,000 chunks6 MBA numpy array in memory. Brute-force scan takes under a millisecond
100,000 chunks600 MBpgvector, or an in-process index. Brute force is still tens of milliseconds
1 million chunks6 GBA real index — pgvector with HNSW, or a dedicated store
100 million chunks600 GBA distributed vector database, quantised vectors, careful sharding
Dropping to 384 dimensions cuts every figure by four. Most corpora are far smaller than people assume: a large company's entire documentation set is usually under 100,000 chunks.

Whatever you use, store alongside each vector: the source document id, the chunk index, the text itself, the embedding model and version, the content hash, and any metadata you will want to filter on. That last point is the subject of the next chapter, and getting it wrong means re-ingesting.

The short version

  1. An embedding is a fixed-length vector whose geometry encodes meaning, produced by a small, cheap, fast model that does not generate anything.
  2. Normalise once at write time; then cosine and dot product are the same thing and the metric stops being a decision.
  3. Scores are not probabilities. Calibrate any threshold against labelled pairs from your own corpus, or avoid thresholds entirely.
  4. Embeddings are blind to negation, numbers, dates and exact identifiers. Those belong in metadata filters and keyword search.
  5. Similar is not relevant. A question and its answer often do not look alike — which is why chunking and query transformation matter so much.
  6. Match the model to the job: asymmetric with prefixes for search, multilingual if your users are, and fewer dimensions than you think.
  7. Changing model means re-embedding everything. Store the model version with every vector so you know what is stale.
