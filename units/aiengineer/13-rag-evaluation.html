Chapter 13 · Knowledge and retrieval

RAG part three: making it good

Every RAG system works impressively on the first ten questions you try and disappoints on the next hundred. The gap between those two states is closed by measurement — specifically, by being able to say whether a bad answer was a retrieval failure or a generation failure, because the fixes have nothing in common.

golden set recall faithfulness llm judge debugging order

The one diagnostic that matters

A user reports: "it said returns take 30 days, but sale items are 14." Before touching anything, answer one question — was the correct passage in the context the model received?

No, it was not there

Retrieval failure. The generator did the best it could with what it was given. Prompt engineering cannot fix this and will waste a week.

Fixes live in chapters 11 and 12: chunking, enrichment, hybrid search, reranking, filters.

Yes, it was right there

Generation failure. The model had the fact and did not use it — ignored it, contradicted it, averaged across sources, or preferred pre-training.

Fixes are the prompt, the ordering, the number of passages, or the model.

These two failure modes are roughly equally common, they look identical to the user, and teams that cannot distinguish them tune the wrong half of their system for months. Everything below exists to make that distinction cheap and automatic.

Building the golden set

You need a fixed set of questions with known-correct answers and known-correct source passages. Aim for 100–200 items. Fifty is enough to start and enough to catch regressions; below thirty, run-to-run noise exceeds the effects you are measuring.

Source of questionsValue
Real user queries from logsThe highest-value source by far. Real questions are messier, shorter and more ambiguous than anything you would invent. Start collecting on day one, even before the system is good
Support tickets and search logsWhat people actually ask, phrased as they actually phrase it, including the typos and the missing context
Domain expertsAsk the person who currently answers these questions for the twenty they get most, plus the five that are always got wrong
LLM-generated from your corpusFast way to bootstrap coverage: for each chunk, generate questions it answers. Must be human-reviewed — generated questions are systematically too easy and too close to the source wording
Deliberate hard casesMulti-hop, comparisons, negations, questions with no answer in the corpus, questions where two documents conflict, superseded-policy questions

The unanswerable cases are the most valuable ones

At least 15% of your golden set should be questions your corpus genuinely cannot answer. Without them you are only measuring whether the system answers — not whether it knows when to stop. A system that scores 95% on answerable questions and hallucinates confidently on every unanswerable one is worse in production than a system that scores 85% and refuses cleanly.

Retrieval metrics

These need no LLM, cost nothing, and run in seconds. Run them on every change to chunking, embedding or search.

MetricDefinitionWhat it tells you
Recall@kFraction of cases where an expected chunk appears in the top kThe ceiling on everything downstream. If recall@20 is 0.7, no prompt can score above 0.7
Precision@kFraction of returned chunks that are relevantHow much noise the model must read past
MRRMean of 1/rank of the first relevant chunkWhether the right passage is at position 1 or position 14 — matters because of packing order
NDCG@kRank-discounted gain over graded relevanceThe standard when relevance is not binary. Use it for reranker comparisons
Recall@k before vs after rerankTwo measurements, one funnelSeparates "retrieval never found it" from "reranker discarded it". Track both or you cannot tell
eval_retrieval.py — runs in seconds, run it constantly
def evaluate_retrieval(cases: list[Case], k: int = 20) -> dict:
    hits, rr, prec, post_rerank = [], [], [], []

    for case in cases:
        if not case.expected_chunk_ids:              # unanswerable: handled separately
            continue
        wide = retrieve_wide(case.question, limit=k)
        ids = [c.id for c in wide]
        expected = set(case.expected_chunk_ids)

        hits.append(any(i in expected for i in ids))
        prec.append(sum(i in expected for i in ids) / len(ids) if ids else 0)
        rank = next((n for n, i in enumerate(ids, 1) if i in expected), None)
        rr.append(1 / rank if rank else 0)

        final = rerank_and_cut(case.question, wide, top_k=5)
        post_rerank.append(any(c.id in expected for c in final))

    return {
        f"recall@{k}":       sum(hits) / len(hits),
        f"precision@{k}":    sum(prec) / len(prec),
        "mrr":               sum(rr) / len(rr),
        "recall@5_reranked": sum(post_rerank) / len(post_rerank),
        "rerank_loss":       sum(hits) / len(hits) - sum(post_rerank) / len(post_rerank),
    }

rerank_loss is the one people forget. If wide retrieval finds the passage 92% of the time but only 74% survives reranking, your reranker is the bottleneck — and no amount of chunking work will help.

Generation metrics

Faithfulness (groundedness)
Is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved context? The single most important generation metric, because unfaithful answers are the ones that damage trust.
Answer relevance
Does the answer address the question asked, rather than a related one?
Context relevance
What proportion of the retrieved context was actually needed? Low values mean you are paying for and diluting with noise.
Correctness
Does it match the reference answer or contain the expected facts? The only metric that needs ground truth, and the most trustworthy.
Refusal accuracy
On unanswerable questions, does it decline? On answerable ones, does it wrongly decline? Both directions matter.
Citation validity
Do cited ids exist, and do the cited passages actually contain the claim? Mechanically checkable, per chapter 12.

Cheap checks first

Before reaching for an LLM judge, a surprising amount is measurable with string operations — deterministic, instant, free, and never disagreeing with itself.

eval_cheap.py — the assertions that catch most regressions
def cheap_checks(case: Case, result: Answer) -> list[str]:
    fails = []

    for fact in case.expected_facts:                 # substring or normalised match
        if not contains_fact(result.text, fact):
            fails.append(f"missing expected fact: {fact}")

    for bad in case.answer_must_not_contain:
        if bad.lower() in result.text.lower():
            fails.append(f"contains forbidden text: {bad}")

    if case.expected_behaviour == "refuse" and not is_refusal(result.text):
        fails.append("answered an unanswerable question")

    if case.expected_chunk_ids and is_refusal(result.text):
        fails.append("refused an answerable question")

    # Every number in the answer must appear in the context it was given.
    for n in numbers_in(result.text):
        if not any(n in c.text for c in result.context):
            fails.append(f"fabricated number: {n}")

    fails += citation_problems(result)               # from chapter 12
    return fails

LLM-as-judge, done carefully

For the qualities that resist string matching — faithfulness, tone, completeness — use a model as the grader. It works well if you respect its limitations.

judge.py — claim-level faithfulness beats holistic scoring
class ClaimVerdict(BaseModel):
    claim: str
    supported: Literal["yes", "no", "partial"]
    source_id: str | None
    quote: str | None = Field(description="Exact supporting text, or null")

class Faithfulness(BaseModel):
    claims: list[ClaimVerdict]

JUDGE = """Break the ANSWER into individual factual claims. For each, decide
whether the CONTEXT supports it. Quote the exact supporting sentence, or return
null. Judge only support by the context — not whether the claim is true in general.

{context}
{answer}"""

verdict = structured_call(Faithfulness, JUDGE.format(...), temperature=0)
score = sum(c.supported == "yes" for c in verdict.claims) / len(verdict.claims)

# Decomposing into claims and demanding a quote does three things:
#   1. turns a vague 1-5 rating into countable, auditable decisions
#   2. makes the failure inspectable - you see which claim was unsupported
#   3. suppresses the judge's own hallucination, because it must find real text
Judges have biases you must control for

Models prefer longer answers, prefer their own outputs over other models', are sensitive to option order in pairwise comparisons, and cluster their scores in the middle of any numeric scale. Countermeasures: use binary or three-way verdicts rather than 1–10 scores; randomise order and run both directions in pairwise tests; use a different model family as judge than as generator; set temperature to 0; and above all validate the judge against human labels on fifty cases before trusting it. If judge and human agree less than about 80% of the time, fix the rubric before using the numbers to make decisions.

Reading the numbers

Failure diagnosis Enter what your evaluation produced. The pattern between the three numbers tells you what to fix — and, more usefully, what not to.

The debugging order

Work through this in sequence. Each step is cheaper than the one after it, and doing them out of order is how weeks disappear.

1 · Is the fact even in the corpus?Grep for it. Astonishingly often it was never ingested, or the parser dropped it. No amount of tuning fixes a missing document
↓
2 · Is it in a retrievable chunk?Find the chunk containing it and read it. Is it split mid-rule? Missing its heading? Buried in boilerplate?
↓
3 · Does wide retrieval find that chunk?Query the index directly for the golden question. If not: hybrid search, enrichment, chunking
↓
4 · Does it survive filtering and reranking?Check the filters did not exclude it and the reranker did not demote it
↓
5 · Was it in the final prompt?Print the literal prompt. Budget truncation and deduplication silently drop passages
↓
6 · Now, and only now, blame the promptThe model had the fact and ignored it. Ordering, passage count, explicit rules, or a better model
Print the prompt

Every RAG framework hides the final prompt behind three layers of abstraction, and the number of bugs that turn out to be "the context was empty", "the template rendered None", or "the passages were truncated at 200 characters" is remarkable. Add a debug flag that dumps the exact string sent to the model. Look at it before forming any theory.

Running evaluation as part of the work

run_eval.py — one command, comparable across runs
import json, hashlib, datetime

def run(cases: list[Case], config: Config) -> Report:
    rows = []
    for case in cases:
        result = answer(case.question, config)
        rows.append({
            "id": case.id,
            "category": case.category,
            "retrieved_wide": [c.id for c in result.wide],
            "retrieved_final": [c.id for c in result.context],
            "recall_wide":  any(c.id in case.expected_chunk_ids for c in result.wide),
            "recall_final": any(c.id in case.expected_chunk_ids for c in result.context),
            "cheap_fails":  cheap_checks(case, result),
            "faithfulness": judge_faithfulness(result),
            "answer": result.text,
            "latency_ms": result.latency_ms,
            "cost_usd": result.cost_usd,
        })

    report = Report(
        run_id=datetime.datetime.now().isoformat(timespec="seconds"),
        config_hash=hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(config.as_dict(), sort_keys=True)
                                   .encode()).hexdigest()[:8],
        config=config.as_dict(),          # chunk size, model, top_k, prompt version…
        rows=rows,
    )
    report.save(f"evals/{report.run_id}-{report.config_hash}.json")
    return report


# In CI: fail the build on regression, not on absolute score.
prev, curr = load_baseline(), run(cases, config)
assert curr.metric("recall@20")  >= prev.metric("recall@20")  - 0.02
assert curr.metric("faithfulness") >= prev.metric("faithfulness") - 0.02
assert curr.metric("refusal_accuracy") >= prev.metric("refusal_accuracy") - 0.02
  • Store the config with every run. "Recall went up" is meaningless without knowing chunk size, model, top-k and prompt version. The config hash makes runs comparable and reproducible.
  • Break results down by category. An overall 0.78 hiding 0.95 on simple lookups and 0.3 on multi-hop is a completely different problem from a uniform 0.78.
  • Change one thing at a time. Chunk size and reranker together means you learn nothing about either.
  • Keep every report. Six months of runs is the only way to answer "when did this get worse, and what shipped that week?"
  • Look at failures individually. Read twenty failed cases in full. Patterns invisible in aggregate metrics are obvious in the actual text.
  • Track cost and latency alongside quality. A change that adds three points of recall and doubles response time is a decision, not an improvement.

Online signals

The golden set catches regressions. It does not tell you what real users experience — for that, instrument production (chapter 18 covers this in general; these are the RAG-specific ones).

SignalReads as
Thumbs up/down rateSparse and biased toward complaints, but the trend is real and it is free
Citation click-throughUsers verifying. High rates can mean either good trust behaviour or low confidence — segment by whether they then rephrase
Rephrase rateAsking again in different words means the first answer failed. One of the best unsupervised quality signals available
Refusal rateRising means retrieval or the corpus has degraded. Falling sharply can mean the model started guessing
Zero-result rateQueries where nothing cleared the rerank floor. Mine these for corpus gaps — they are a content backlog, not a bug
Escalation to humanThe metric the business cares about. Tie your quality work to it

Feed the failures back: every thumbs-down with a rephrase becomes a golden-set candidate, and every zero-result query becomes either a documentation gap or a retrieval bug. That loop is what turns a demo into a system that improves.

The short version

  1. Always answer first: was the correct passage in the context? Retrieval and generation failures look identical and share no fixes.
  2. Build a golden set of 100–200 cases from real logs, with at least 15% unanswerable.
  3. Retrieval metrics are free and instant. Recall@k is the ceiling on everything downstream.
  4. Track recall before and after reranking separately, or you cannot see the reranker discarding good passages.
  5. Do the cheap deterministic checks first — expected facts, forbidden text, fabricated numbers, citation validity.
  6. For faithfulness, decompose into claims and demand quotes. Validate the judge against humans before trusting it.
  7. Debug in order — corpus, chunk, retrieval, rerank, prompt contents — and print the literal prompt before theorising.
  8. Run evaluation in CI against a baseline, store the config with every run, and feed production failures back into the golden set.
