Chapter 01 · Groundwork

What an AI engineer actually does

The role is barely a few years old and the title is used loosely. This chapter pins down what the job is, what it is not, and what changes about your engineering when part of the system is a model you did not write and cannot fully predict.

The job in one sentence

An AI engineer builds software features on top of models that somebody else trained. You are not producing weights. You are producing a system — prompts, retrieval, tools, guardrails, caching, evaluation, a user interface — in which a general-purpose model becomes reliable enough at one specific job that people will pay for it.

That sentence carries a lot of weight, so take the pieces apart.

Somebody else trained it. The single largest shift of the last few years is that state-of-the-art natural language capability arrives as an API call. In 2018, adding "understand this support ticket and route it" to your product meant labelling several thousand tickets, choosing an architecture, training, and holding a model in production. Today it is a request with a well-written instruction attached. The expensive, specialist part was commoditised. What did not get commoditised is everything around it.

Reliable enough at one specific job. A frontier model is a generalist that is good at almost everything and dependable at almost nothing without help. Your job is narrowing: constraining inputs, supplying the right context, defining the output shape, checking the result, and deciding what happens when it is wrong. A demo shows the model can do the task. An AI engineer shows it does the task on the ninety-fifth bad input too.

People will pay for it. Every token costs money and every call costs time. A feature that works but costs eleven cents and nine seconds per request is not shippable in a product that charges twenty dollars a month. Cost and latency are functional requirements here in a way they rarely are in CRUD work.

The shape of the work

Roughly speaking: 10% choosing and calling a model, 25% getting the right context in front of it, 25% constraining and validating what comes back, 20% evaluation and iteration, 20% the ordinary engineering of latency, cost, failure and abuse. The API call is the smallest part.

How this differs from the neighbouring roles

Job titles blur, and plenty of people do two of these at once. What matters is knowing which set of problems you are being asked to solve, because the tools and the vocabulary are different.

The dividing line: an ML engineer's artefact is a model. An AI engineer's artefact is a system that uses one.

Do you need the maths?

Not to start, and mostly not ever. You do not need to derive backpropagation to build a good retrieval system, and no amount of linear algebra tells you how to chunk a PDF. What you do need is a handful of concepts held accurately rather than vaguely: what a token is, what a vector similarity actually measures, why temperature changes behaviour, what a context window costs. Those are all in the next two chapters and none of them require calculus.

The maths becomes worth learning at exactly one boundary: when you start fine-tuning, or when the model itself is the bottleneck rather than your system around it. That is chapter 14, and by then you will know whether you are actually there.

Nearly every LLM feature in a real product is one of about eight shapes, or a composition of two. Recognising the shape early tells you which techniques you need and what will go wrong.

The stack, layer by layer

Here is the whole territory in one picture. You will not need every layer on every project — a classification endpoint needs three of them — but knowing the full set stops you reaching for a heavy technique when a light one would do.

›
Evaluation & observabilityEval sets, traces, cost and latency metrics, user feedback. The layer that turns guessing into engineering.

Two observations about this diagram that take people a year to learn on their own.

First, the model layer is not where you get your wins. Once you are on a competent model, swapping to a slightly better one moves quality a few percent. Fixing your retrieval or your output contract moves it tens of percent. Spend your time high in the stack.

Second, the evaluation layer is not optional and not last. It is drawn at the bottom because it observes everything, not because you do it at the end. Teams that add evals after launch spend the following six months unable to answer "did that change help?" — a question that has no answer without a test set, because the system is not deterministic.

What changes about your engineering

You already know how to build software. Five things are genuinely different, and each has a chapter later that deals with it properly.

1. The same input gives different outputs

Even at temperature zero, you are not guaranteed byte-identical results: floating point non-determinism in batched GPU inference, model updates behind a stable name, and load-balanced hardware all introduce variation. So your test suite cannot assert equality on model output. It asserts properties — is it valid JSON, does it contain a citation, is the sentiment field one of three values, does an independent grader score it above a threshold — across a set of cases, with a pass rate you accept. This is chapter 18, and it is the single biggest habit shift.

2. Failure is soft, fluent and confident

A database returns an error. A model returns a beautifully written wrong answer. There is no exception to catch. Consequently a large part of your design is manufacturing hard failures out of soft ones: schemas that fail validation, citations you can verify against the source, confidence thresholds, a "I don't have that information" path you explicitly reward. Design so that being wrong produces a signal.

3. Every request has a price and a delay

Two seconds is a fast LLM response and two hundred milliseconds is a slow database query. That is an order of magnitude of latency you must design a user experience around — streaming, optimistic UI, background jobs — rather than hide. And the marginal cost of a request is real money, which changes what abuse looks like: an unauthenticated endpoint that proxies to a model is a way for strangers to spend your budget.

4. Your input is untrusted in a new way

In web security you learn never to trust user input. Here the model also reads documents, web pages, emails and tool results — and instructions hidden in any of them can hijack it. Prompt injection has no complete fix, only a defence in depth of least privilege, isolation and verification. Chapter 17.

5. The prompt is code, and it is the interface

The prompt is the densest, highest-impact, most-frequently-edited artefact in the system, and it usually lives as a string in the middle of a function where nobody reviews it. Treat prompts as first-class: versioned files, reviewed changes, an eval that runs when they change. Chapter 6.

The demo-to-product gap

A working prototype takes an afternoon. Getting from there to something you would let customers touch takes weeks, and the work is nearly all in verification, retrieval quality, evaluation and cost. Plan for that ratio when you scope. The afternoon is not the estimate.

What you should already know

This unit assumes ordinary software engineering competence. Specifically:

  • You can build and deploy a backend service, and you understand HTTP, JSON, authentication and environment configuration.
  • You are comfortable with asynchronous code — promises or async/await — because almost every call here is I/O-bound and slow.
  • You can read and write Python or TypeScript. The ecosystem is overwhelmingly both; the examples here are both.
  • You know how to use a database and can reason about indexes, at the level of "why is this query slow".
  • You have written tests and used version control on a team.

If you are missing the backend half, the Back-end Developer and API Design units cover it. If you are frontend-first, you can do most of this work — the streaming UX and interface design of AI features is genuinely undersolved and valuable — but you will need the API and async material.

A useful mental substitution

Treat the model as an unreliable, expensive, slow, wildly capable remote service written by someone else. Every instinct you already have about such services — timeouts, retries with backoff, circuit breakers, input validation, output validation, caching, fallbacks, cost monitoring — transfers directly and is most of what good AI engineering looks like.

The short version

  1. You build systems around models you did not train. The API call is the easy part; narrowing a generalist into something dependable is the job.
  2. Nearly every feature is one of eight patterns. Name the pattern first — it tells you which techniques you need and what will break.
  3. The wins are high in the stack: context assembly, retrieval and verification beat model swapping almost every time.
  4. Five things differ from normal engineering: non-determinism, soft failure, per-request cost, injection, and prompts as code. Each gets a chapter.
  5. Evaluation is not a phase at the end. Without it you cannot answer "did that change help?", and every later chapter assumes you can.
