CHAPTER 05

Choosing and reading a test

The assumption everyone checks costs almost nothing. Skewed data at n = 50 gives a false-positive rate of 4.8% against a nominal 5%. Unequal variances with unequal group sizes gives 45.0%, and Welch's correction brings it back to 5.3% for free. Clustering gives 35.8% — and no test in this chapter fixes that one.

    THE MECHANISM

    What a test actually computes

    Every test in this chapter is the same three steps with different arithmetic in the middle.

    1. Assume nothing is happening
    The null hypothesis. Not a belief — a reference point. You are constructing the world in which your data would be unremarkable, so you can ask how remarkable it actually is.
    2. Work out what the data would look like in that world
    The null distribution of your test statistic. Derived from theory for a t-test, from ranks for Mann-Whitney, or built by shuffling the labels for a permutation test.
    3. Ask where your observation falls in it
    The p-value: the probability, if nothing were happening, of seeing a result at least this extreme. Small means your data would be surprising in that world.
    Which is why the assumptions matter in one specific place

    Every assumption a test makes is used in step 2, to work out the null distribution. If the assumption is wrong, that reference distribution is the wrong shape — and then the p-value is measured against the wrong ruler.

    So the useful question is never "is this assumption true" (it never is) but "how much does violating it distort the reference distribution". That is measurable, and the next section measures it.

    Read the table by row and the conventional advice inverts.

    1. Normality, the assumption everyone checks, costs nothing at moderate n. Lognormal data at n = 50 gives 4.8% against a clean 5.5%. The central limit theorem is doing its job — with the caveat from chapter 2 that heavy enough tails need much more data.
    2. At small n, skew makes the t-test conservative rather than liberal. 1.9% at n = 10 — you lose power, you do not gain false positives. That is a real cost but a safe direction.
    3. Unequal variance is harmless when the groups are the same size — 5.1%. It is catastrophic when they are not: 45.0%, nine times the nominal rate.
    4. Welch's correction fixes that completely, at 5.3%, and costs almost nothing in the cases where it was not needed.
    5. Clustering breaks every test equally. 35.8%, 35.8% and 34.6% — Welch does not help, and neither does the non-parametric option.
    Just use Welch's t

    Look along the Welch column: 5.5, 4.7, 1.5, 5.0, 5.3. It is correct everywhere Student's t is correct, and it is correct in the one case where Student's t produces a 45% false-positive rate. The cost is a fractional loss of power when variances genuinely are equal, which is not a trade worth thinking about.

    The pooled test exists because it was easier to compute by hand in 1908. There is no modern reason to default to it, and R's t.test already defaults to Welch. Pandas and SciPy do not — ttest_ind takes equal_var=True unless you say otherwise.

    The direction of the variance failure is worth knowing

    Small group with the larger variance gives 45.0% false positives. Small group with the smaller variance gives 0.0% — not a rounding, an actual zero across four thousand comparisons.

    So the same violation can make a test wildly anti-conservative or completely powerless, depending on which group happens to be small. That is a genuinely unpredictable failure, and it is another argument for never thinking about it and using Welch.

    And the one nothing fixes

    Ten users contributing five rows each, no real difference, and every test rejects around a third of the time. Not 5% — 35.8%, seven times the nominal rate.

    No choice of test repairs this, because it is not a test problem. The tests are all answering the question "given 50 independent observations per group…" and there are not 50 independent observations per group; there are 10. This is chapter 3's design effect appearing as a false-positive rate, and the fixes are all structural: aggregate to the user level, cluster the standard errors, or randomise at the cluster level.

    Which is the most useful thing in this chapter. The assumption people check obsessively costs 0.7 points. The assumption nobody checks costs 30.

    THE OTHER HALF

    Choosing a test, when there is something to find

    Holding the false-positive rate is necessary and not sufficient. The second question is which test finds a real effect most often.

    The asymmetry is the finding. On perfectly normal data — where the t-test is provably optimal — it beats Mann-Whitney by 2.3 points, 69.9% against 67.6%. Add 3% extreme outliers and the t-test collapses to 31.6% while Mann-Whitney holds 62.6%.

    A bad bet either way

    The t-test wins by 2.3 points on its home ground and loses by 31.0 points off it. If you are unsure which world you are in — and with real behavioural data you usually are — that is not a close decision.

    The reason is mechanical: the t-test uses the sample mean and variance, and both are dragged around by a handful of extreme values. Mann-Whitney uses only ranks, so an outlier is just the largest rank however large it is.

    Mann-Whitney does not test the median

    It tests whether a random draw from one group tends to exceed a random draw from the other. Under the extra assumption that the two distributions have the same shape and differ only by a shift, that is equivalent to comparing medians — and if the shapes differ, it can return a significant result when the medians are identical.

    That also explains the 7.6% in the unequal-variance row of the earlier table: Mann-Whitney is detecting a real difference in distribution, which is not the difference anyone was asking about.

    When the shapes might differ and you care about the mean, the honest tool is a permutation test on the difference in means, which tests exactly the thing you meant.

    A SMALL DECISION WITH TEETH

    One-sided or two-sided

    A one-sided test is more powerful and almost always the wrong choice in this setting.

    The legitimate case

    A one-sided test is defensible when a result in the other direction would lead to the same action as no result at all — and when you committed to the direction before seeing anything.

    It buys real power: the same evidence that gives p = 0.06 two-sided gives p = 0.03 one-sided.

    Why it usually is not

    In product experiments you nearly always care about a harmful result. "It made things worse" is not the same finding as "nothing happened" — it means roll back, and possibly investigate.

    And choosing the direction after seeing the data doubles your false-positive rate while looking like a methodological choice.

    The test to apply

    Ask: if the effect went the other way, would I do anything differently? If yes, you need two-sided. In experimentation the answer is nearly always yes, which is why two-sided is the default and a one-sided test in a results write-up deserves a question about when the direction was chosen.

    READING IT

    What a p-value is, and the four things it is not

    Worth stating flatly, because all four misreadings appear in production analyses.

    A p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme if the null were true. It is a statement about data given a hypothesis, and every misreading below reverses that.

  1. Not the probability the null is true. That would require a prior — it is the reversed conditional from chapter 2, and answering it is what chapter 1's value-of-information calculation actually needs.
  2. Not the probability the result will replicate. A p of 0.04 in an underpowered study replicates far less than half the time.
  3. Not a measure of effect size. With ten million users, a 0.001% difference gives a spectacular p-value and means nothing. p answers "is there something", never "is it worth anything".
  4. Not a threshold that separates real from unreal. 0.049 and 0.051 are the same evidence. The 0.05 line is a convention, and treating it as a boundary is what produces the peeking behaviour in chapter 8.
    5. Report the interval instead

    "+2.1%, 95% CI [0.3%, 3.9%]" contains everything the p-value does and answers the question the decision actually needs: how big might this be, at best and at worst. Chapter 6 is about reading those honestly.

    The p-value is not wrong, it is thin. It compresses an estimate and its uncertainty into one number and then discards the estimate.

    THE HARDER READ

    What a non-significant result means

    Almost always misread, and in a way that quietly kills good ideas.

    Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. A non-significant result means your data was compatible with no effect. It is also compatible with a large effect you were not powered to detect.

    What people say

    "No significant difference, so the feature does nothing. Do not ship it."

    With 30% power, a real and valuable effect produces exactly this result seven times in ten.

    What the data supports

    "Estimated +1.2%, 95% CI [−2.1%, +4.5%]. Consistent with anything from a small harm to a large gain — this study could not tell them apart."

    Which is a different conclusion and leads to a different decision: run it longer, or accept that you will not know.

    The interval tells you which kind of null result you have

    If the interval is tight around zero — say [−0.3%, +0.4%] when you needed 2% to be worth shipping — that is informative. You have ruled out anything that mattered, and "no effect" is a real finding.

    If the interval is wide — [−8%, +11%] — you have learned essentially nothing, and reporting it as "no effect" is a false statement about what the study established.

    Those two results have the same p-value and opposite meanings. This is why chapter 7's minimum detectable effect belongs in the plan rather than the post-mortem: knowing beforehand which of these you will end up with is what decides whether to run the study at all.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Every test assumes a null, derives what data would look like under it, and locates yours. Assumptions are used to build that reference distribution.
    2. Normality costs almost nothing at moderate n: 4.8% false positives on skewed data against 5.5% clean.
    3. At small n, skew makes the t-test conservative — 1.9% — so you lose power rather than gaining false positives.
    4. Unequal variance is harmless with equal group sizes (5.1%) and catastrophic without: 45.0%.
    5. Welch's t fixes it at 5.3% and is correct across every scenario tested. Default to it.
    6. The direction of that failure is unpredictable: small group with large variance gives 45.0%, small group with small variance gives 0.0%.
    7. Clustering breaks every test equally — 35.8%, 35.8%, 34.6% — because it is a data-structure problem, not a test problem.
    8. The assumption people check costs 0.7 points; the one nobody checks costs 30.
    9. On normal data the t-test beats Mann-Whitney by 2.3 points. With 3% outliers it loses by 31.0.
    10. Mann-Whitney does not test the median unless the distributions have the same shape.
    11. Use two-sided unless a result in the other direction would change nothing, and never choose the direction after seeing the data.
    12. A p-value is not the probability the null is true, not a replication rate, not an effect size, and not a boundary.
    13. Report the interval. It contains the p-value's information and answers the question the decision needs.
    14. A tight interval around zero is a finding; a wide one is a failed study. Same p-value, opposite meanings.
