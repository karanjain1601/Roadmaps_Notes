CHAPTER 16

Distributions and generalised linear models

An odds ratio of 2.0 is a 98% relative increase at a 1% baseline and a 5.3% increase at 90%. Same coefficient, same model, and the number a decision needs differs by a factor of nineteen. Meanwhile overdispersed counts leave the Poisson coefficient exactly right and take its interval from 94% coverage to 55% — the same "estimate fine, standard error broken" pattern as the last chapter, in a new family.

    THE MISMATCH

    When linear regression is the wrong model

    Not because it is inaccurate. Because it is describing a quantity that cannot behave the way it says.

    Outcomes where a straight line is structurally wrong
    OutcomeWhat it can beWhat OLS assumesUse

    The test worth applying

    Can the model predict something the outcome cannot be? If a linear model on a binary outcome predicts 1.3, that is not a rounding error — it is the model saying something impossible, and it means the functional form is wrong in a way that chapter 15 showed no standard error repairs.

    Measured below: at a 4% baseline rate, 15.8% of OLS predictions fall outside [0, 1]. At a 50% baseline it is 3.3%, which is why linear probability models survive so long in practice — near the middle they are a decent approximation, and near the edges they are not.

    THE FRAMEWORK

    Family and link

    Two choices, and everything else is the same machinery.

    A generalised linear model keeps the linear predictor η = Xβ and adds two pieces: a link function mapping η to the mean of the outcome, and a family describing how the outcome varies around that mean.

    The three you will actually use
    FamilyLinkCoefficient meansVariance assumption
    GaussianIdentity: μ = ηChange in Y per unit XConstant
    BinomialLogit: log(p/(1−p)) = ηChange in log-odds; eβ is an odds ratiop(1−p), fixed by the mean
    PoissonLog: log(μ) = ηChange in log-rate; eβ is a rate ratioEquals the mean — check this
    The link is why coefficients stop being directly readable

    In OLS a coefficient of 0.3 means "0.3 more Y". In a GLM it means 0.3 more on the link scale, and the link is nonlinear — so the effect on the actual outcome depends on where you start. That single fact generates most of the misreadings in this chapter.

    The other thing to notice is the variance column. Both non-Gaussian families fix the variance as a function of the mean, with no free parameter. That is a strong assumption, it is frequently false, and the last demo measures what it costs.

    THE COMMONEST MISREADING

    An odds ratio is not a relative risk

    Logistic regression reports odds ratios. Almost everyone reads them as "X% more likely". Here is the size of the error.

    An odds ratio of 2.0 throughout

    5%9.52%+4.52 pts+90.5%
    10%18.18%+8.18 pts+81.8%
    30%46.15%+16.15 pts+53.8%
    50%66.67%+16.67 pts+33.3%
    90%94.74%+4.74 pts+5.3%

    One coefficient, six answers. The relative change runs from +98.0% to +5.3% — a factor of nineteen — with nothing changing but the baseline.

    And the absolute change peaks in the middle

    +0.98 points at a 1% baseline, +16.67 at 50%, +4.74 at 90%. So the two readings do not even move in the same direction as the baseline rises: the relative change falls monotonically while the absolute change rises and then falls.

    Which of them a decision needs is a business question. If you are sizing a support-cost increase, the absolute change is what matters. If you are comparing the value of two interventions on different populations, the relative one may be. Reporting the odds ratio alone answers neither, and it is the number the software prints.

    The practical fix is one line: convert to predicted probabilities at realistic baseline values and report those. An odds ratio is a fine internal parameterisation and a poor way to tell someone what will happen.

    Why the approximation is taught anyway

    When the outcome is rare, an odds ratio and a relative risk nearly coincide — at a 1% baseline, OR 2.0 gives a relative risk of 1.98. That is why the shorthand survives in fields where events are rare.

    In product analytics events are frequently not rare. Conversion at 30%, engagement at 50%, retention at 70% — and at those baselines the approximation is wrong by a factor of two or more. The shorthand does not travel.

    The linear probability model is not absurd — near a 50% baseline it is a reasonable approximation, which is why it persists. Away from the middle it produces impossible predictions and fits the true probabilities 2.7 times worse.

    COUNTS

    Poisson, and the assumption nobody checks

    Poisson regression assumes the variance equals the mean. Real count data almost never obliges.

    Orders per customer, tickets per account, sessions per week. The Poisson family has no free variance parameter — Var(Y) = μ is baked in. When the real data is more variable than that, the model is overdispersed.

    Look at the Poisson SE column

    0.0298, 0.0297, 0.0299, 0.0298. It does not move. Meanwhile the estimate's true spread goes from 0.0300 to 0.0846, nearly tripling. The Poisson standard error is computed from the fitted means alone, so it literally cannot see the extra variation — it reports the same uncertainty whatever the data does.

    Coverage therefore collapses from 94.0% to 55.0%. A "95% interval" that contains the truth barely half the time.

    And exactly as in chapter 15, the slope is untouched: 0.497, 0.505, 0.502, 0.498 against a true 0.500. This is a standard-error problem, not an estimation problem, and it has a one-parameter fix.

    The diagnostic is free, and it is already in the output

    The Pearson dispersion — the sum of squared standardised residuals over the degrees of freedom — estimates the overdispersion directly. In the table it reads 0.99, 1.99, 3.99, 8.00 against true values of 1, 2, 4 and 8.

    So the check is: look at the dispersion. If it is far above 1, the Poisson variance assumption has failed. Every GLM implementation reports it, and almost nobody looks.

    The quasi-Poisson fix is to scale the standard errors by √dispersion, which restores coverage to 94.5%, 95.0% and 95.5% across the three overdispersed rows. A negative binomial model does something more principled — it adds a real variance parameter and fits it — and usually gives similar intervals with a better fit to the count distribution itself.

  1. Overdispersion usually means an omitted source of heterogeneity. Customers differ in ways the model has not captured, so their rates differ and the counts spread out. Adding the right covariate can remove it entirely.
  2. Excess zeros are a different problem. If far more observations are zero than any Poisson allows, you likely have two processes — whether someone ever orders, and how much — and a hurdle or zero-inflated model separates them.
  3. Underdispersion happens too, and makes the Poisson interval conservative rather than dangerous. Rarer, and worth noticing.
  4. Use an offset for exposure. Counts observed over different windows or populations need log(exposure) as an offset, not as a predictor, so the model works on rates.

    5. BEFORE THE MODEL

    Fitting a distribution to data

    The roadmap lists this as its own topic, and it mostly serves the model choices above.

    Plot it first, on a log scale too
    Most of the decision is visible. Right-skewed and positive suggests lognormal or gamma; a spike at zero plus a spread suggests two processes; a straight line on a log-log plot suggests a power law, with chapter 2's warning that the mean may not converge.
    Compare candidates by likelihood, penalised
    AIC or BIC across a handful of families. Useful for ranking, and not evidence that any of them is right — the best of four wrong models is still wrong.
    Check the tail separately
    Goodness-of-fit statistics are dominated by the bulk, where most of the data is. If the decision depends on the tail — capacity planning, fraud, risk — fit and check the tail on its own terms.
    Do not test for normality and then act on it
    With large n a normality test rejects every real dataset, and with small n it has no power. The useful question is never "is this normal" but "does the procedure I am about to use survive this much non-normality" — which chapter 5 measured at 4.8% against a nominal 5%.

    IN PRACTICE

    Reading a GLM output

    Five things, and the first two are where the misreadings live.

  5. Exponentiate, then convert to probabilities. eβ is an odds or rate ratio; report predicted outcomes at realistic baselines instead. The nineteen-fold spread in the table above is why.
  6. Check the dispersion. Far above 1 for a Poisson means the intervals are too narrow, and the demo shows how far: 55% coverage at dispersion 8.
  7. Check for separation in logistic models. If some predictor perfectly separates the outcomes, coefficients run off to infinity with enormous standard errors. Usually a leaked outcome variable rather than a discovery.
  8. Cluster the standard errors if the rows are not independent. Chapter 15 took coverage from 37% to 91% doing this, and nothing about the GLM family changes that argument.
  9. Say which scale the interval is on. An interval on the log-odds scale is not symmetric once converted to probabilities, and reporting a symmetric probability interval is a small lie that occasionally matters at the edges.
    10. The one-line summary

    Choose the family so the model cannot predict the impossible; then report on the scale the decision is made in, not the scale the model is fitted on.

    Nearly every problem in this chapter is one of those two sentences being skipped.

    6. The absolute change peaks in the middle: +0.98 points at 1%, +16.67 at 50%, +4.74 at 90%.

    8. The rare-event approximation is why the shorthand survives, and product metrics are usually not rare.
    9. Convert to predicted probabilities at realistic baselines and report those.
    10. Poisson fixes Var(Y) = μ with no free parameter, and real count data rarely obliges.
