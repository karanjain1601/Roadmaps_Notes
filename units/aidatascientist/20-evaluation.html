CHAPTER 20

Evaluating a model for a decision

The same model, at base rates from 50% down to 0.5%: ROC-AUC sits at 0.921 the whole way while precision at 80% recall falls from 86.6% to 3.3%. And four models with bit-identical AUC differ by up to 34% in the cost they impose per case, because AUC depends only on the order of the scores and a decision depends on their values.

    Three things happen at once

    Accuracy inverts. At 1% prevalence a model that always says "no" scores 99.0%, beating the real model's 84.1%. Accuracy is not merely uninformative here — it ranks the useless predictor above the useful one.

    ROC-AUC does not move. 0.9209, 0.9210, 0.9209, 0.9209, 0.9205 across a hundredfold change in base rate. It is a property of the model's separation, not of the data mix, and 0.921 is exactly Φ(d′/√2) for d′ = 2. So "our model has an AUC of 0.92" tells you nothing about whether it is usable.

    PR-AUC and precision collapse. 0.9213 down to 0.1929, and precision at 80% recall from 86.6% to 3.3%. That last number is what the people working the alert queue actually experience: thirty false alarms for every real case.

    Which to report

    Report PR-AUC or precision at a fixed recall for imbalanced problems, because they move when the thing that matters moves. Report the majority-class baseline alongside any accuracy figure, always — it costs nothing and it is the only thing that makes the number interpretable.

    ROC-AUC remains useful for one job: comparing two models on the same dataset, where the base rate is held constant by construction. It is a ranking-quality summary, and reading it as a usability summary is the mistake.

    Chapter 2 gave the arithmetic behind the collapse: at 0.3% prevalence with 90% sensitivity and 95% specificity, precision is 5.1% and the system produces 49,850 false alerts a day. This section is the same fact viewed through the metrics that hide it.

    CURVES

    What each curve shows and hides

    Both plot the same trade-off. They disagree about what is worth seeing.

    ROC

    True positive rate against false positive rate. The denominator of the x-axis is the number of negatives, which is enormous under imbalance — so a huge number of false positives moves the curve barely at all.

    Insensitive to prevalence by construction. That is a feature for model comparison and a bug for judging usability.

    Precision-recall

    Precision against recall. The x-axis is the same, but the y-axis is the fraction of alerts that are real — the quantity an operator experiences.

    Its baseline is the prevalence itself, so a PR curve at 1% prevalence starts from a floor of 0.01 rather than 0.5.

    Two things worth knowing about AUC

    ROC-AUC is exactly the Mann-Whitney statistic — the probability that a randomly chosen positive scores above a randomly chosen negative. Chapter 5 found the same statistic giving bit-identical power under a monotone transform of the data, for the same reason it will give bit-identical values in the next demo: it sees only order.

    F1 is a specific weighting, not a neutral one. It weights precision and recall equally, which is a claim about costs that is almost never true. If you know the cost ratio, use it directly rather than adopting F1's implicit assumption.

    THE INVISIBLE FAILURE

    Calibration

    A model is calibrated if, among cases it scores 0.30, about 30% are positive. Ranking metrics cannot tell whether it is.

    Not approximately equal — identical to twelve decimal places, because every transform here is monotone and AUC depends only on the order of the scores. It is structurally incapable of detecting miscalibration.

    Meanwhile the Brier score moves 0.1062 → 0.1162 / 0.1267 / 0.1561, the calibration error moves 0.0115 → 0.0871 / 0.1378 / 0.2000, and the cost per case moves 0.7321 → 0.9473 / 0.9814 / 0.8823. A model whose ranking is indistinguishable imposes up to 34% more cost per case — 29%, 34% and 21% across the three broken rows.

    The cheapest check in this chapter

    Compare the mean predicted probability with the observed rate. The honest model predicts 15.4% against an observed 15.4%. The systematically high one predicts 35.4% against the same 15.4%.

    That is one line of code and it catches the most common calibration failure immediately. A reliability diagram — predictions binned, mean prediction against observed frequency in each bin — is the fuller version and takes about five.

    Why models come out miscalibrated
    Regularisation shrinks scores toward the middle — chapter 19's ridge took b₁ from 1.53 to 0.21. Boosted trees and neural networks typically go the other way and are overconfident. Resampling or class weighting to fix imbalance shifts the whole scale.
    How to fix it
    Platt scaling fits a logistic regression on the scores; isotonic regression fits a monotone step function and is more flexible but needs more data. Both are fitted on a held-out set — fitting on training data is chapter 19's leakage.
    Neither changes the ranking
    Both are monotone, so AUC is unaffected. You are not making the model better at discriminating; you are making its numbers mean what they say.
    When it does not matter
    If you only ever act on the top N by score, calibration is irrelevant and ranking is everything. If a probability enters an expected-value calculation, it is the whole game.

    THE DECISION

    Choosing a threshold from costs

    There is a right answer, and it does not come from the ROC curve.

    If acting costs 1 and a missed positive costs C, act when the probability exceeds 1/C. That is the whole rule, and it requires the probability to be a probability.

    The first row is exact, and explains the rest

    At C = 2 the threshold is 0.500, and an overconfident model costs exactly the same — 0.2959 to the last digit. Scaling the log-odds leaves p = 0.5 fixed, so no case crosses the threshold and no decision changes.

    The penalty then grows as the threshold moves away from that fixed point: 0.0%, 9.3%, 28.8%, 79.2%. Miscalibration is harmless exactly where it happens not to move anything, and expensive everywhere else — which is a poor thing to rely on, since the cost ratio is a property of the business rather than of the model.

    This also explains why calibration problems hide so well. Most demos and most tuning happen near a 0.5 threshold, which is the one place the failure is invisible.

  1. Derive the threshold, do not tune it. If you know the costs, 1/C is the answer. Tuning a threshold to maximise F1 adopts an unstated cost ratio and usually the wrong one.
  2. Recheck it when the base rate moves. The optimal threshold depends on costs, but the resulting volume of action depends on prevalence, and capacity is finite.
  3. If capacity is the binding constraint, the question becomes "which top N" and calibration stops mattering. Say which regime you are in.
  4. Costs are rarely symmetric and rarely known precisely. A range is fine — compute the cost curve across plausible ratios and check the decision is stable across it.

    5. A COMMON REQUEST

    What feature importance can support

    Stakeholders ask what the model found. The honest answer is narrower than they want.

    Three questions, and whether importances answer them
    QuestionCan importances answer it?
    Which features is this model relying on?Yes — that is what they measure
    Which features would improve the prediction if measured better?Partly, with care
    Which levers would change the outcome if we pulled them?No
    Why the third row is a firm no

    Chapter 19 measured it: the prediction-optimal model reported b₁ = 0.208 against a true 1.50. Coefficients from a model tuned for prediction have been deliberately distorted, and tree importances are additionally affected by correlated features, cardinality and split order.

    And chapter 15 gave the deeper reason: controlling for a collider took an unbiased 2.001 to 0.830 while R² rose. Nothing in a model's output distinguishes a confounder from a collider from a mediator, so nothing in it can tell you which features are levers.

    SHAP values are a genuine improvement on impurity importances for the first question — they are consistent, additive and local. They remain answers to "what is this model doing", not "what would happen if we intervened". Chapter 21 is about the models built for that question instead.

    JUDGEMENT

    Reading an evaluation

    Six questions. The first three are the ones that fail.

  5. What is the base rate, and what does the trivial predictor score? 99.0% accuracy from always saying no.
  6. Is the metric sensitive to what matters? ROC-AUC was flat at 0.921 while precision fell from 86.6% to 3.3%.
  7. Is the model calibrated? Compare mean predicted with observed. AUC cannot see this, at all.
  8. Where does the threshold come from? A cost ratio, or a tuning run against a metric with an implicit one.
  9. Was the evaluation honest? Chapter 19: leaky selection reported 87.3% accuracy on pure noise.
  10. What is the cost per case, in the units of the business? The only number that closes the loop.
    11. The metric chain, end to end

    A decision needs three things, and each is invisible to the metric above it. Ranking — does the model order cases correctly? ROC-AUC answers this and nothing else. Calibration — do the numbers mean what they say? Brier and reliability diagrams answer this; AUC is bit-identical across all four calibrations. Cost — what does acting on it produce? Only an explicit cost model answers this, and it moved by up to 34% across models AUC could not distinguish.

    Report all three. A single headline number will be one of them at best, and the chapter's tables show which two it silently drops.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. At 1% prevalence, always saying "no" scores 99.0% against the real model's 84.1%. Accuracy inverts.
    2. ROC-AUC is flat at 0.921 across a hundredfold change in base rate, because it measures separation, not usability.
    3. 0.921 is exactly Φ(d′/√2) for d′ = 2 — a property of the model alone.
    4. PR-AUC falls from 0.9213 to 0.1929 and precision at 80% recall from 86.6% to 3.3%.
    5. Report PR-AUC or precision at fixed recall, plus the majority-class baseline, always.
    6. ROC-AUC is exactly the Mann-Whitney statistic, which is why it sees only order.
    7. F1 encodes a cost ratio of one to one, which is a claim rather than a neutral default.
    8. Four models with bit-identical AUC differ by up to 34% in cost per case — 29%, 34% and 21%.
    9. AUC is identical to twelve decimal places under any monotone transform, so it cannot detect miscalibration.
    10. The Brier score and calibration error do move — 0.1062 to 0.1561 and 0.0115 to 0.2000.
    11. Compare mean predicted with observed: 15.4% against 15.4% honest, 35.4% against 15.4% biased. One line.
    12. Platt scaling and isotonic regression fix calibration on a held-out set and leave the ranking untouched.
    13. The optimal threshold is 1/C when acting costs 1 and a miss costs C.
    14. At C = 2 an overconfident model costs exactly the same, because scaling log-odds leaves p = 0.5 fixed.
    15. The penalty grows as the threshold moves away: 0.0%, 9.3%, 28.8%, 79.2%.
    16. Feature importances say what the model uses, never which levers to pull.
