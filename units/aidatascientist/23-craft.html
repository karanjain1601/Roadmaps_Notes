CHAPTER 23

The craft

Halving your largest source of error beats eliminating all four smaller ones — 34% off the total against 13%. And multiplying five factors each uncertain by a factor of two does not give you a thirty-two-fold range; it gives you 4.7, which is why a five-minute estimate is worth doing before a five-day analysis.

    BEFORE ANYTHING

    Start from the decision

    The most common failure in this job is a competent answer to a question nobody was going to act on.

  1. What decision does this change? If there is no answer, the analysis is entertainment. That is occasionally fine, and it should be labelled.
  2. What would you do at each possible answer? If the action is the same whatever comes back, stop. Chapter 1 measured this exactly: the value of information was £0 when you would ship either way, and peaked at £99k when the prior sat at 33%.
  3. How precise does it need to be? Distinguishing 2% from 20% is a different job from distinguishing 2% from 2.5%, and chapter 7 prices the difference at a hundredfold in sample size.
  4. When is it needed? An approximate answer on Tuesday usually beats a precise one next month, and the next section says how approximate you can afford to be.
    5. The question behind the question

    "Can you pull the conversion rate by channel?" is rarely the actual need. It is usually a proxy for "should we move budget", and those have different analyses — the second requires the incremental effect that chapter 21 spent a chapter on, and the first will be quietly used to answer it anyway.

    Asking what the number is for costs one message and routinely changes the work. It also surfaces the case where the decision has already been made, which is worth knowing before spending a week.

    WHERE TO SPEND EFFORT

    The error budget

    Every analysis has several sources of uncertainty. They are not equal, and the arithmetic of how they combine says where to work.

    34.0% against 13.3%. And eliminating the second largest source completely — a real week of work, a defensible-sounding improvement — buys 6.2%.

    Which is the argument against the instinct to fix the thing you noticed most recently, or the thing that is easiest to fix, or the thing a reviewer complained about. Effort follows the variance, not the count of steps, and the variance is usually concentrated in one place.

    Note also what this says about the finite sample. It carries 75.2% of the variance here, which means the honest recommendation is often "get more data or accept the range" rather than any amount of methodological polish.

    And it all depends on independence

    At ρ = 0 the total is 0.5766. At ρ = 1 it is the simple sum, 1.0500 — 82% larger. Perfectly correlated errors do not cancel at all.

    This is the practical reason one shared bad assumption is worse than five unrelated approximations. Five independent roundings partly offset each other; five estimates all made by the same person on the same optimistic afternoon do not, and the analysis inherits the full bias.

    BEFORE THE ANALYSIS

    Estimate first

    Multiply a handful of rough numbers and see what comes out. The result is far more reliable than it has any right to be, and there is a reason.

    Uncertainty grows as the square root of the number of factors

    Five factors, each uncertain by a factor of two: the fear is ×32 and the reality is ×4.71, nearly seven times narrower. Going from one factor to eight widens the range 3.55×, against the 128× that compounding would imply.

    The mechanism is the one this whole unit keeps returning to. Errors in the log of each factor are independent, so they add in quadrature: √5 × ln2, not 5 × ln2. Some factors are overestimated and some underestimated, and they partly cancel.

    63% of five-factor estimates land within a factor of four of the truth. That is not a guarantee, and it is enough to tell a £50k opportunity from a £5m one — which is usually the decision the estimate is for.

    Unless the errors share a cause, and then it collapses

    Five factors, correlation raised from 0 to 1: the observed range goes from ×12.88 to ×305.87 and the hit rate from 63% to 31%.

    A shared optimistic assumption running through every factor does not cancel with itself. So the discipline that pays is not more precision per factor — it is checking that the factors were not all estimated by the same person in the same mood, and deliberately sourcing at least one of them from somewhere that would disagree.

    How to use this in practice

    Before starting, write the answer as a product of four or five things you can guess, guess them, and multiply. You now know the order of magnitude and, more usefully, which factor the answer is most sensitive to — because that is the one worth actually measuring.

    Then the error budget from the previous section applies directly: the largest source dominates, so measure that one properly and leave the rest as guesses. That is often a day of work instead of a fortnight, and the difference in the final range is small enough to see in the table above.

    HOW TO WORK

    Making the answer survive contact

    Most of these exist because someone will ask "where did that number come from" three months from now, and it will be you.

    One script, top to bottom
    From raw source to final number, runnable from clean. A notebook executed out of order is not a record of anything, and the failure mode is discovering the headline came from a cell that no longer exists.
    Write the assertions as you go
    Row counts after each join, ranges on every rate, no duplicate keys. Chapter 10's leak was a silent 0.7% loss of users that produced a significant effect from a true zero 92% of the time. Assertions catch that class of thing and nothing else does.
    Check the total against something you did not compute
    A finance figure, a dashboard, a number someone quoted last quarter. Reconciling to an independent source catches whole categories of error that internal consistency never will.
    Version the data, not just the code
    Backfills happen. An analysis that cannot say which snapshot it ran against cannot be reproduced, and rerunning it later on silently different data is worse than not rerunning it.
    Keep the exploratory work, and label it
    Delete nothing, promote nothing without rerunning it in the clean script. Chapter 14's segment hunting fired 59% of the time on data with no heterogeneity at all, and the separation between "explored" and "concluded" is the only defence.
    The single highest-return habit

    Before you look at the answer, write down what you expect and what would surprise you. It takes two minutes and it does two things: it makes Twyman's law operational, because you now have a definition of "surprising" that predates the result, and it prevents the version of confirmation bias where the checking effort scales with how much you dislike the number.

    THE OUTPUT

    Communicating a result

    An analysis that does not change a decision has failed, however correct it is.

  5. Decision first, in the first sentence. Whoever reads it wants to know what happens next. Methodology at the top means the recommendation gets skimmed and misread.
  6. Give the range, not just the point. "+2.3%" invites a decision the data cannot support; "+2.3%, and we can rule out anything below +0.4%" gives the reader what they need.
  7. Say what the analysis could not resolve. This is the sentence that stops a null result being cited later as evidence of no effect, and it is the one most often left out.
  8. Put the number in the decision's units. A coefficient of 0.0031 per pound of spend is not a finding until it is £48,000 a year.
  9. Round to the precision you have. Reporting 2.3471% when the interval spans two points is a claim about precision that the analysis does not support, and readers correctly infer carelessness elsewhere.
  10. Name the assumption that would change the answer. One line. It is what separates a result someone can act on from one they have to take on trust.
    11. Two framings of the same finding

    Weak: "We ran a logistic regression on 90 days of data controlling for tenure, platform and channel. The coefficient on the new onboarding flow was 0.31 (p = 0.03), suggesting a positive effect on activation."

    Strong: "Ship it. The new flow raises activation by about 4 points, and we can rule out anything below 1 point — comfortably above the 2-point bar. This assumes the 14-day activation window still reflects long-run retention; if that has changed, the estimate is optimistic. Method and checks below."

    Same analysis. The second gives a decision, a range, a comparison to the bar, and the assumption that would overturn it — and it is shorter.

    Odds ratios and coefficients are not results

    Chapter 16 measured what happens when they are reported as such: an odds ratio of 2.0 is a 98% relative increase at a 1% baseline and a 5.3% increase at 90%, a factor of nineteen. Convert to predicted probabilities at realistic baselines before anyone reads them.

    The same applies to any coefficient from a model tuned for prediction. Chapter 19's prediction-optimal model reported b₁ = 0.208 against a true 1.50.

    THE HARD PART

    When the answer is not what people wanted

    It happens often, and how it is handled determines whether anyone believes the next one.

    Separate the finding from the recommendation
    "The experiment shows no detectable effect" is a finding. "Do not ship" is a recommendation that also depends on cost, strategy and things you may not know. Conflating them turns a factual disagreement into a political one.
    Do the extra checks, and do them for both directions
    Someone asking "are you sure?" is usually asking a reasonable question. Run the checks. The discipline is running the same checks when the result is welcome, which is chapter 14's point about applying Twyman's law symmetrically.
    Do not reanalyse until it agrees
    Chapter 8 priced continuing until significance: twenty looks took the false-positive rate from 5.0% to 25.1%. Trying analyses until one supports the preferred conclusion is the same mechanism with a longer feedback loop and no correction available.
    Offer what would settle it
    "This study could not distinguish a small win from nothing; a two-week test at the current traffic could, and CUPED on pre-period purchase rate would cut the required time by a third." That converts an argument into a plan, and chapter 12 measured the third.
    Be willing to say the data cannot answer it
    Sometimes the honest output is that no available data supports the claim anyone wants to make. Saying so is the job. Chapter 13's designs are the alternative when randomising is impossible, and their assumptions belong in the first paragraph rather than the appendix.
    Credibility is the actual asset

    The value of this work is that when you say a number, people act on it. That is built by being right, by flagging your own errors before anyone else finds them, and by being visibly as sceptical of results you like as of results you do not.

    It is spent by overclaiming once. A confident recommendation that turns out to rest on chapter 19's leaked feature or chapter 15's uncorrected clustering costs more than the analysis was worth, because it is retroactively applied to everything you said before it.
