CHAPTER 14

Reading an experiment

A feature with a true long-run effect of 1.00 reports 3.49 on day one. Plot the daily effect against the calendar and a staggered rollout hides the decay almost completely; plot it against each user's own exposure age and it is obvious. Then there are the segments: with no real heterogeneity at all, hunting through twenty of them produces a finding 59% of the time.

    THE FIRST REFLEX

    Twyman's law

    Any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong.

    It sounds cynical and it is the most useful heuristic in experimentation. Real effects in a mature product are small. A result that is much larger than anything you have seen before is far more likely to be a bug than a breakthrough, simply because bugs are more common than breakthroughs.

  1. Check the sample ratio first. Chapter 10 measured what a small leak does: an effect significant 92% of the time from a true effect of exactly zero. This is the single highest-yield check and it takes a minute.
  2. Check whether the metric is mechanically affected. A feature that surfaces more content raises page views by construction, and it will be significant every time.
  3. Check the analysis grain. Chapter 9: randomising by user and analysing by session understated the standard error 2.6×.
  4. Check whether anything else shipped. A concurrent release, a marketing push, an outage in one region.
  5. Check the power. Chapter 7: at 11% power the mean reported effect was 3.65× the truth, and 5.3% of significant results had the wrong sign. A surprisingly large effect from a small study is evidence of a small study.
    6. Apply it symmetrically

    The failure mode is applying Twyman's law only to results you dislike. A surprisingly good result gets shipped; a surprisingly bad one gets investigated until it goes away. That asymmetry is a bias-generating machine, and it operates entirely through decisions that each look like diligence.

    The fix is procedural: run the checks before you look at the direction, and write the ship rule down beforehand, which is what chapter 9's experiment plan is for.

    Novelty, primacy, and why the calendar lies

    Effects that change with exposure are common. Whether you can see them depends on which axis you plot.

    The diagnostic, and why the obvious version fails

    The standard advice is to plot the daily effect over time and look for a decay. With everyone entering at once that works: 3.492 on day 1, 1.669 on day 7, 1.058 on day 14, 1.000 on day 21. The shape is unmistakable.

    With a staggered rollout it fails. The calendar-day curve goes 3.501, 2.218, 1.985, 1.907 — and once the cohort mix settles it is nearly flat, varying by only 0.51 across the second half. Every calendar day now blends users on their first day with users on their tenth, so the average barely moves even though every individual user's effect is decaying steeply.

    Plot the same data against each user's own exposure age and it reappears: 3.557, 1.629, 1.257 — a range of 2.43, nearly five times what the calendar shows. The rule is simply: plot against days-since-exposure, not calendar date.

    Primacy runs the other way, and is more expensive

    Some changes make things worse before they make them better: users have to find the moved button, learn the new flow, rebuild a habit. The demo's primacy setting gives day 1 an effect of −0.808 and day 7 of 0.018, against a true long-run 1.00.

    An early read would have killed a feature that works. And this failure is harder to catch than novelty, because a bad early result feels like an answer — nobody argues for running longer when the number is negative.

    SLICING

    Segment hunting, and the test that actually works

    "It didn't work overall, but it worked for mobile users in Germany." Here is how often that sentence is available when nothing is happening.

    Segment hunting cannot distinguish signal from nothing

    Read the second column. With zero true heterogeneity, some segment looks significantly different from the overall 59% of the time. With real heterogeneity it is 100%. A procedure that fires most of the time whether or not the thing exists is not a test.

    The third column is what a test looks like: 5% → 39% → 100% → 100%. That is a single test for heterogeneity across all segments at once — the analogue of an F-test for an interaction — and it holds its false-positive rate when nothing is there while detecting real variation when it is.

    Note also the fourth column. With no heterogeneity at all, the biggest gap between two segments averages 0.26 on a true effect of 0.20. So the strongest possible version of the story — "it works twice as well for this group and not at all for that one" — is routinely available from pure noise.

    WHAT TO REPORT

    Shrinkage

    Once you know some segments genuinely differ, the individual estimates are still too spread out. There is a principled fix.

    Each segment estimate contains its true effect plus noise, so the observed spread is always larger than the true spread. Empirical-Bayes shrinkage pulls each estimate toward the overall mean by however much of the observed variation the noise can account for.

    When noise dominates

    True spread 0.00, apparent spread 0.0699. Shrinkage gives 0.0079 — it correctly concludes that essentially all the variation was noise.

    When signal dominates

    True spread 0.30, apparent spread 0.3050. Shrinkage gives 0.2881 — barely touched, because the noise can only explain a small part of a large spread.

    It does not erase real differences

    That is the property worth internalising, and it is why shrinkage is the right default rather than a way of dismissing inconvenient segments. It removes the portion of the observed spread that the noise accounts for, and leaves the rest.

    Practically: report the shrunk estimates, not the raw ones, whenever you show a per-segment breakdown. The raw table always looks more interesting, and that is precisely the problem — the most extreme segment in a raw table is the one most likely to be extreme by luck, which is chapter 7's winner's curse applied across segments instead of across studies.

    Pre-register the segments that matter
    Two or three, named in the plan, with a reason. New versus returning users is a real hypothesis; the cross-product of platform, country and tenure is a fishing expedition.
    Run the single heterogeneity test first
    If it does not fire, the per-segment table is a description of noise and should be labelled as such. If it does, then look at which segments and by how much.
    Treat exploratory segments as hypotheses
    "Mobile users in Germany responded more strongly" is a candidate for the next experiment, not a finding from this one. Saying so costs nothing and protects everyone.
    Remember the power
    A segment is a fraction of the sample, so its MDE is much larger. Twenty equal segments have chapter 7's MDE multiplied by √20 — about 4.5× — which is why segment estimates are so noisy in the first place.

    DECIDING

    When the answer is ambiguous

    Most of the time. Chapter 6 measured why: an effect sitting on the decision threshold is called conclusive 2.5% of the time, at every sample size.

    The four readings from chapter 6, and what to do with each
    The intervalReadingAction
    Clears the thresholdWorth itShip, and say what you expect in production
    Excludes zero, not the thresholdReal but maybe not worth itShip or not on cost, not on significance
    Tight around zeroRuled out what matteredDo not ship. This is a finding.
    Wide, spans everythingLearned nothingDo not pretend otherwise. Decide on other grounds or run it properly.
    The second row is where most experiments land

    "+0.8%, 95% CI [0.1%, 1.5%], and we needed 1% to justify the maintenance cost." That is a real effect that may not be worth having, and no further statistics will resolve it — the question has become one of cost.

    The right move is to make that explicit rather than rounding it to a yes or a no: the experiment has done its job, and the remaining decision is a product judgement. Presenting it as "significant, so ship" hides a choice someone else should be making.

    Two ways of dodging an ambiguous result

    Running it longer because it is nearly significant. Chapter 8 priced this: twenty looks at 1.96 takes the false-positive rate from 5.0% to 25.1%, and the reported effect from 0.0648 to 0.1122 against a truth of 0.0500.

    Finding a segment where it worked. Available 59% of the time when nothing is happening.

    Both feel like extra rigour. Both are ways of converting an ambiguous result into a confident one without acquiring any new information.

    THE WRITE-UP

    What a good result read looks like

    Short, and in an order that makes the caveats impossible to skip.

    results.md
    # Recommendation carousel — result

## Decision
DO NOT SHIP for now. Re-run in Q3 with the fix below.

## Headline
Repeat purchase +0.31 pts, 95% CI [-0.29, +0.91].
We needed +0.62 to clear the maintenance cost, and the interval
does not reach it. It also does not exclude zero. This study
could not distinguish "small win" from "nothing".

## Checks
- Sample ratio 50.02 / 49.98, p = 0.61. Clean.
- Analysed at the user grain, as randomised.
- No concurrent releases in the window.
- Guardrails: p95 latency +4ms (within tolerance), no change in
  unsubscribes or support contacts.

## Time
Effect plotted against days-since-exposure, not calendar date.
Day 1: +1.4 pts. Day 14: +0.3 pts. Consistent with novelty,
so the headline uses days 8-28 only. The full-window figure
(+0.55) is inflated and we are not using it.

## Segments
Pre-registered: new vs returning. Heterogeneity test p = 0.34 —
no evidence the effect differs by segment. The per-segment table
below is shown shrunk, and should be read as noise.

## What we would need
MDE at current traffic is 0.62 pts, exactly at our threshold —
chapter 7's borderline case, where a true effect on the line is
called conclusive ~2.5% of the time however long we run.
CUPED on pre-period purchase rate (rho = 0.63) would cut this to
about 0.48, which would make the question answerable.
    Three habits that do most of the work

    Decision first. Anyone reading a results doc wants to know what happens next. Burying it under methodology means it gets skimmed and misread.

    State what the study could not resolve. "This could not distinguish a small win from nothing" is more useful than a p-value, and it is the sentence that prevents the result being cited later as evidence of no effect.

    Say what would make it answerable. The last block turns a disappointing result into a plan, and it is the difference between a null result that wastes a fortnight and one that improves the next experiment.
