CHAPTER 12

Variance reduction: CUPED and stratification

Chapter 7's quadratic wall says halving the detectable effect costs four times the traffic. This chapter halves it using data you already have. A pre-period covariate correlated at 0.9 cuts the variance 79.3% — worth 4.82 times the traffic — and leaves the estimate exactly where it was. There is one rule, and breaking it turned a true effect of 0.1000 into 0.0400.

    WHY THIS IS WORTH SO MUCH

    The other half of the fraction

    The standard error is σ/√n. Everyone reaches for n, and σ is usually the cheaper lever.

    Buying precision with traffic

    Quadratic and slow. Chapter 7 measured it: halving the MDE takes four times the users, and going from a 32% detectable lift to 4% takes sixty-four times.

    You also cannot usually buy it — traffic is what it is, and the test can run for two weeks.

    Buying it by cutting σ

    Linear in the standard error, and free. The data is already in your warehouse: what these users did last month.

    Nothing about the experiment changes. Same assignment, same duration, same users. Only the arithmetic afterwards.

    The idea in one sentence

    Most of the variation between users is not caused by your feature. A user who spent £400 last month will spend a lot this month whether or not they see the new checkout, and that predictable variation is noise from the experiment's point of view.

    If you can predict part of each user's outcome from data that existed before the experiment started, you can subtract that part. What remains still contains the whole treatment effect — because the prediction knew nothing about the assignment — but has much less variance around it.

    THE TECHNIQUE

    CUPED

    Controlled experiments Using Pre-Experiment Data. One line of arithmetic, and the gain is entirely determined by one number.

    cuped.py
    import numpy as np

def cuped(y, x, arm):
    """
    y   : the experiment metric, one row per user
    x   : the SAME metric measured before the experiment started
    arm : boolean, treated or not
    """
    theta = np.cov(y, x, ddof=1)[0, 1] / x.var(ddof=1)   # the optimal coefficient
    y_adj = y - theta * (x - x.mean())                   # subtract what was predictable

    t, c = y_adj[arm], y_adj[~arm]
    est = t.mean() - c.mean()
    se  = np.sqrt(t.var(ddof=1) / len(t) + c.var(ddof=1) / len(c))
    return est, se

# theta is computed on the POOLED data, both arms together. That is standard,
# and it does not bias the estimate: theta is a single number applied identically
# to every user, so it cannot shift one arm relative to the other.

    Two columns to compare. The variance cut tracks ρ² almost exactly at every setting — 8.3 against 9.0, 23.2 against 25.0, 46.4 against 49.0, 79.3 against 81.0. That identity is the entire theory.

    Why ρ², and what it means practically

    Subtracting the best linear prediction of Y from X leaves the residual variance, which is σ²(1 − ρ²). So the fraction of variance removed is ρ² — the same quantity as R² in chapter 4, and the same geometry: you are projecting out the component of Y that lies along X.

    The practical translation matters more. Since the required sample size scales with variance, a cut of ρ² is worth 1/(1 − ρ²) times the traffic. At ρ = 0.7 that is 1.87×; at 0.9 it is 4.82×; at 0.95 it is 9.29×. Doubling your traffic is a quarter's work. This is an afternoon.

    And at ρ = 0 it changes nothing — 0.02623 either way. There is no downside to trying it, which is unusual enough to be worth stating: the method cannot make the estimate worse, only fail to improve it.

    The estimate stays unbiased at every correlation, which is the whole selling point: CUPED moves the variance, not the mean. And the reported standard error falls along with the true spread, so the gain shows up in the interval rather than being cosmetic.

    THE ONE HARD RULE

    The covariate must predate the assignment

    Everything else here is a preference. This is not.

    What happens when it does not

    Switch the demo's covariate to one measured after the treatment started, and the estimate becomes 0.0400 against a truth of 0.1000 — at every correlation, because the damage has nothing to do with correlation.

    A covariate touched by the treatment carries part of the effect. Subtracting it does not remove noise, it removes some of the thing you are measuring. The variance falls, the interval tightens, everything looks better, and the number is wrong.

    And it looks spectacular on the diagnostics: the spread collapses from 0.02559 to 0.00003. An almost infinitely tight interval, around the wrong number.

    This is the same error as chapter 10's triggered analysis and the same family as conditioning on a post-treatment variable in a regression. It is silent, it looks like an improvement, and no diagnostic in the pipeline will flag it.

  1. "Sessions during the experiment" is not a valid covariate even though it correlates beautifully with the outcome. The treatment may have changed it.
  2. "Sessions in the four weeks before assignment" is valid, because nothing you did could have affected it.
  3. Watch the boundary carefully. A user assigned on day three of a rolling rollout needs a covariate window ending before their assignment, not before the experiment's start date.
  4. New users have no pre-period, which is the practical limit of the whole technique — see below.

    Three things that table says

    Every method is unbiased — 0.1009, 0.1009, 0.1006, 0.1005, 0.0998 against a truth of 0.100. They differ only in precision, which is what makes the choice purely practical.

    Stratification and CUPED converge. Twenty bins gets 31.8% and CUPED gets 33.0% on the same covariate, because with enough bins post-stratification is nearly the same estimator as a linear adjustment. Four bins gets 27.4% — coarse binning throws away some of the signal, exactly as chapter 1's adjustment demo found.

    CUPAC wins by using more covariates. Replacing the single pre-period metric with a prediction built from all of them takes the cut from 33.0% to 50.7% — worth 2.03× the traffic against CUPED's 1.49×.

    Translated into what anyone actually cares about: CUPAC detects effects 29.8% smaller than the unadjusted analysis on identical data. Buying that with traffic instead would take 2.03 times as many users, and chapter 7's wall means there is often no way to get them.

    CUPED
    One covariate, usually the same metric measured before. Simple, explicable, and captures most of the gain when the metric is autocorrelated — which for revenue and engagement it strongly is.
    CUPAC
    Replace the single covariate with a model's prediction of the outcome from many pre-period features. The adjustment is identical; only the covariate is better. This is DoorDash's variant, and the gain over CUPED is exactly the extra predictive power the model has.
    Stratification
    Split into bins and pool the within-bin differences. Applied before assignment it also guarantees balance, which is chapter 10's legitimate route to balanced arms. Netflix's work on stratifying by pre-period viewing is the canonical example.
    Regression adjustment
    Regress the outcome on the treatment indicator and the covariates. Algebraically close to CUPED, and the natural framing if you already think in chapter 15's terms.

    IN PRACTICE

    Where it works and where it does not

    The technique is nearly free. The limits are about which users and which metrics.

  5. New users have no pre-period. The commonest limitation, and it bites hardest on acquisition experiments where the population is new users by definition. Options: use a covariate available at signup, or accept no reduction for that segment and take it where you can.
  6. Rare-event metrics have little to predict from. If a user converted zero times last month and zero times this month, the covariate carries almost no information. Variance reduction is weakest exactly where power is worst.
  7. Fit θ on the pooled data. A single number applied identically to every user cannot shift one arm relative to the other, so this does not bias the estimate — and using both arms makes θ better estimated.
  8. Check ρ once, in advance. The correlation between your metric now and the same metric a month ago is a fixed property of your product. Compute it, and you know what CUPED is worth before you build anything.
  9. It composes with everything else. Ratio metrics from chapter 11, sequential boundaries from chapter 8, clustered standard errors — CUPED sits underneath all of them, because it only changes the outcome column.
    10. Where to put it in the plan

    Compute the MDE twice in chapter 7's calculation: once plain, once with the variance reduction you expect from your measured ρ. Frequently the second number turns an infeasible experiment into a feasible one, and that conversation happens before anything is built rather than after a null result.

    And put the adjustment in the pipeline rather than in an analyst's notebook. A technique worth 2× the traffic should not depend on somebody remembering to apply it.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. The standard error is σ/√n, and σ is usually the cheaper lever. Traffic is quadratic; cutting σ is free.
    2. Most variation between users is not caused by your feature, and anything predictable from before the experiment is noise you can remove.
    3. CUPED subtracts θ(X − X̄) where θ = Cov(Y,X)/Var(X), and X predates assignment.
    4. The variance cut equals ρ² — measured 8.3, 23.2, 46.4 and 79.3% against a predicted 9.0, 25.0, 49.0 and 81.0%.
    5. That is worth 1/(1 − ρ²) times the traffic: 1.87× at ρ = 0.7, 4.82× at 0.9, 9.29× at 0.95.
    6. The estimate stays unbiased at every correlation. CUPED moves the variance, not the mean.
    7. At ρ = 0 it changes nothing, so it cannot make things worse — only fail to help.
    8. The covariate must predate assignment. A post-treatment covariate gave 0.0400 against a truth of 0.1000.
    9. That failure is silent and looks superb: the spread collapsed from 0.02559 to 0.00003 around a wrong answer.
    10. For a rolling rollout, the window must end before each user's own assignment, not the experiment's start date.
    11. Every method is unbiased: 0.1009, 0.1006, 0.1005 and 0.0998 against 0.100. They differ only in precision.
    12. Stratification converges to CUPED with enough bins — 31.8% at 20 bins against CUPED's 33.0%, and 27.4% at 4.
    13. CUPAC takes the cut to 50.7% by predicting from several covariates instead of one, worth 2.03× the traffic.
    14. That detects effects 29.8% smaller than the unadjusted analysis on identical data.
    15. New users have no pre-period, and rare-event metrics have little to predict from. Both are real limits.
    16. Compute the MDE twice in the plan — plain, and with the reduction you expect — before anything is built.
