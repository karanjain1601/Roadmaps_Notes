    Three things the table settles

    The first column reproduces chapter 7 exactly — 71.2, 31.9, 15.9, 7.1, 3.2 — which is worth stating because chapter 7 obtained them by simulation and this obtains them from the formula. And quadrupling the sample halves the MDE to floating-point precision.

    Correcting for twenty metrics costs 38.0% of MDE, because the critical value grows roughly like √log m. Chapter 8 priced the alternative: 65.5% family-wise error at twenty metrics, and 1.03 false winners per run. Correcting is cheap and not correcting is not.

    CUPED at ρ = 0.7 is worth 1.96× the traffic, matching chapter 12's 1/(1−ρ²). It is the only lever here that buys precision without buying users.

    0.35 is above the threshold and 0.18 is below it. Yet 0.18 is a real effect that is probably not worth having, and 0.35 establishes nothing at all — its interval runs from −0.73 to 1.43, which includes "harmful", "trivial" and "transformative".

    Ranking results by point estimate puts the fourth row first. Reading the interval puts it last. That single habit is most of what chapter 6 was for.

    An independent confirmation of chapter 7

    Chapter 7 measured a 3.65× exaggeration at 11% power by simulating twenty thousand studies. This table computes 3.64× at 10.2% power from a closed form — a completely different method arriving at the same number.

    The inflation falls monotonically with power and reaches 1.00× at the bottom. The estimator itself is unbiased at every row; it is the condition on significance that does the damage.

    And at 80% power the inflation is 12%. That is the practical argument for powering properly, and it is not the usual one: powering an experiment is not only about detecting the effect, it is about being able to believe the size you report when you do.

    The pattern across both tables

    Almost every row is a case where the number looks fine and is wrong. That is the unit's actual subject. Analyses rarely fail loudly; they fail by producing a plausible figure with an interval that is too narrow, a metric that cannot see the problem, or a comparison that answers a different question.

    Which is why the recurring recommendation is not more sophistication but more falsification: run the A/A test, check the density, difference the series, plot against exposure age, hold out 2% at random.

    WORKED PROBLEMS

    Six to try

    Each has a specific answer somewhere in the unit. Work out the diagnosis before checking.

    1. The retention model that stopped working
    A churn model held steady at 0.83 AUC for eight months, then fell to 0.71 over three weeks. The feature-drift dashboard shows nothing. What are the two candidate explanations, which monitor would separate them, and why did the dashboard stay quiet? — Chapter 22.
    2. The campaign that beat its control and lost money
    A retention offer targeted at the highest-risk decile showed a significant lift against holdout, and revenue fell. What is the most likely explanation, and what would you measure to confirm it? — Chapter 21.
    3. The pricing analysis with an implausible coefficient
    A regression of units sold on price gives a positive coefficient, robust across specifications. Name the missing variable and predict the direction of the bias before you look for it. — Chapter 15.
    4. The experiment that will not resolve
    Three weeks in, +0.4% against a 1% threshold, interval [−0.3%, +1.1%]. Leadership wants an answer on Friday. What do you say, what would make it answerable, and roughly how much would each option buy? — chapters 6, 7 and 12.
    5. The fraud model with 99.4% accuracy
    Fraud runs at 0.4% of transactions. The team is pleased. What is the trivial baseline, which metrics would you ask for, and what would you expect precision at 80% recall to look like? — chapters 2 and 20.
    6. The market launch with no control
    A feature launched in one country and revenue rose 8%. Which design would you use, what does it assume, and what test would you run to check the assumption? — Chapter 13.
    The habit these are training

    In all six, the productive first move is the same: state what would have to be true for the result to be real, then check that thing. Not "is the analysis correct" but "what is the specific mechanism by which this could be wrong, and does the data show it".

    That is what a placebo test, an A/A test, a density check and a differenced regression all are. Chapter 13's density ratio going from 1.01 to 11.68 is the model: the assumption left evidence, and someone looked.

    ONWARD

    Where to go next

    This unit covered the roadmap's statistics, experimentation, econometrics and prediction. Three directions from here.

    Deeper on the methods

    Machine Learning for the algorithms in depth, and Python for Data Analysis for the tooling this unit assumed.

    Wider on the role

    Data Analyst and BI Analyst for the reporting side, Data Engineer for the pipelines underneath.

    What to actually practise

    The demos in this unit were built so you can break them. Every one recomputes from real code, so changing a setting and predicting the result before you look is a genuine exercise — and the settings where your prediction was wrong are the ones worth rereading.

    Beyond that, the highest-return practice is running an A/A test on your own experimentation platform. Chapter 10 gives the expected results; anything that deviates from them is a real finding about your infrastructure, and it is the kind of thing nobody discovers until an important result turns out to be wrong.

    The one thing to carry

    Across twenty-four chapters, the same failure recurs in a dozen costumes: a number that looks fine, produced by a procedure that could not have detected the problem. Clustered rows counted as independent. A monitor that cannot see concept drift. A metric invariant to the thing that matters. A split that lets the model see the future.

    The defence is never more sophistication. It is asking, before you believe a number, what would this analysis have shown if the answer were wrong — and then going and checking that specific thing.
