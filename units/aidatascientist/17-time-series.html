CHAPTER 17

Time series: structure and stationarity

Two hundred daily observations with a lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.9 carry the information of about ten independent ones. And two completely independent random walks look significantly related 81% of the time — rising to 89% with more data, which is the opposite of every intuition about sample size.

    DECOMPOSITION

    What a series is made of

    Four components, and separating them is most of the work.

    Trend
    The long-run direction. The crucial distinction, and the one this chapter turns on, is between a deterministic trend (the series moves around a fixed line) and a stochastic one (the series wanders, with no line to return to). They look similar and need opposite treatments.
    Seasonality
    Fixed-period repetition: day of week, month of year, hour of day. Product metrics usually have several at once — weekly and annual, plus a payday cycle.
    Cycle
    Repetition without a fixed period: business cycles, adoption waves. Harder to model and easy to mistake for a trend over a short window.
    The rest
    What remains, which is where the modelling happens. It is almost never independent noise, and the next section is about why that matters.
    Why this chapter comes after regression rather than before

    Everything in chapter 15 assumed the rows were independent, and measured what happens when they are not: coverage fell to 37% and robust standard errors did nothing about it.

    A time series is the case where the rows are never independent. Today resembles yesterday, and that single fact reshapes the standard errors, the tests, and the meaning of "more data".

    This is the same result three chapters have now produced

    Chapter 3: 10,000 rows from 500 users at an ICC of 0.4 were worth 1,163. Chapter 9: randomising by user and analysing by row understated the standard error 2.6×. Chapter 15: clustered errors took coverage to 37%.

    Autocorrelation is the same phenomenon with time as the cluster. The unit of independent information is not the row, and counting rows overstates how much you know. Everything else in this chapter follows from that.

    The diagnostic is one line: the measured lag-1 autocorrelation recovers ρ directly (−0.009, 0.285, 0.481, 0.677, 0.874 against 0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9). And the full ACF decays geometrically as ρk — 0.7045, 0.4958, 0.3466 at lags 1, 2 and 3 against a predicted 0.7000, 0.4900, 0.3430 — which is the signature that identifies an AR(1) in the first place.

    THE CONDITION

    Stationarity

    A series is stationary if its statistical behaviour does not depend on when you look.

    Concretely: constant mean, constant variance, and an autocorrelation that depends only on the gap between two points rather than on their position. Almost every time-series method assumes it, and almost no raw business series has it.

    Deterministic trend

    yt = a + bt + noise. The series varies around a fixed line. Shocks are temporary — it returns.

    Made stationary by removing the trend: fit the line, keep the residuals.

    Stochastic trend (unit root)

    yt = yt−1 + noise. A random walk. There is no line — it wanders, and shocks are permanent.

    Made stationary by differencing: model the changes rather than the levels.

    Applying the wrong one does not help

    Detrending a random walk leaves it non-stationary, and the next demo measures exactly how little it helps: 72.2% false positives after detrending, against 80.8% before.

    Differencing an already-stationary series makes things worse in the other direction — over-differencing induces negative autocorrelation and takes the false-positive rate from 4.7% to 12.3%.

    Telling the two apart is what a unit-root test (ADF, KPSS) is for. Their null hypotheses run in opposite directions, which is why people run both: ADF's null is a unit root, KPSS's null is stationarity, and agreement between them is more convincing than either alone.

    More data makes it worse

    Read down the random-walk column: 65.0%, 74.3%, 80.8%, 88.5%. Every other failure in this unit gets better with sample size. This one gets steadily worse, because the t-statistic grows without bound when both series are non-stationary — the average |t| between two unrelated walks is 7.9, and the average R² is 0.235.

    With drift it is total: 100.0% at 200 observations. Two things that both go up will always look related, and most business metrics go up.

    This is why "our NPS and our revenue moved together over eighteen months" is close to no evidence at all. It is also chapter 14's Twyman's law with a specific mechanism: an R² of 0.9 between two monthly business series is a reason for suspicion, not celebration.

    REPAIRS

    Which fix works for which trend

    Two treatments, and each solves one problem while creating the other.

    False-positive rate after treatment, T = 200. Target is 5%
    SeriesRawDifferencedDetrended
    Stationary noise4.7%12.3%4.7%
    Random walk80.8%5.0%72.2%
    Walk with drift100.0%5.0%72.2%
    Read the table as a two-by-two of matched and mismatched fixes

    Differencing rescues both walks completely — 80.8% to 5.0%, and 100.0% to 5.0%. Model the changes and the spurious relationship disappears entirely.

    Detrending does not — 72.2% in both cases. A random walk has no trend to remove; it wanders, so subtracting a straight line leaves something that still wanders. Detrending is the correct fix for a deterministic trend and does nothing for a stochastic one.

    And differencing a series that was already fine makes it worse — 4.7% to 12.3%. Over-differencing induces negative autocorrelation, which is its own violation. "Difference everything" is not a safe default.

  1. Differencing does not throw away real signal. When two series share genuine shocks, the differenced regression still detects the relationship 100% of the time. The fix costs nothing when there is something to find.
  2. Cointegration is the exception worth knowing. Two series can each have a unit root while a combination of them is stationary — genuinely tied together, wandering as a pair. Differencing both loses that long-run relationship, and an error-correction model keeps it.
  3. Seasonal differencing is a separate lever (yt − yt−12) for annual patterns in monthly data, and can be combined with ordinary differencing.
  4. Logs before differencing when variation scales with level, which is most revenue series. Differenced logs are approximately growth rates, which is usually the quantity of interest anyway.

    5. JUDGEMENT

    Reading a time series claim

    Five questions, and the first two catch most of it.

  5. Are both series stationary? If not, the correlation is uninterpretable and the t-statistic is meaningless. This one question invalidates a large fraction of "these two metrics move together" analyses.
  6. How many independent observations are there really? Not how many rows. At ρ = 0.9, 200 rows are worth about ten.
  7. Was the relationship predicted in advance? Spurious relationships are abundant, so finding one is not informative. Specifying it beforehand is.
  8. Does it survive differencing? The cheapest robustness check available, and it takes one line.
  9. Is there a plausible mechanism at the right lag? A relationship at lag 0 between weekly series with a supposed causal delay of a month is a coincidence with a story attached.
    10. What to do with a suspicious correlation

    Difference both series and re-run it. If it survives, it is worth investigating; if it evaporates, you have learned that the apparent relationship was two trends coexisting. This takes a minute and settles most arguments.

    And be specific about which claim you are making. "These move together" is nearly free to satisfy and almost never what a decision needs. "A change in X is followed by a change in Y two weeks later, and here is what happened when X moved" is a claim with content.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. A series decomposes into trend, seasonality, cycle and the rest, and the rest is not independent noise.
    2. The crucial distinction is deterministic against stochastic trend — a fixed line to return to, or no line at all.
    3. Autocorrelation is clustering with time as the cluster, the same result as chapters 3, 9 and 15.
    4. At ρ = 0.9 the naive standard error is understated 4.40-fold.
    5. 200 observations then carry the information of about ten — a nineteen-fold reduction.
    6. The understatement tracks √((1+ρ)/(1−ρ)) at every level tested.
    7. The measured lag-1 recovers ρ directly, so the diagnostic costs one line.
    8. An AR(1)'s ACF decays geometrically: 0.7045, 0.4958, 0.3466 against 0.7000, 0.4900, 0.3430.
    9. Two independent random walks look related 80.8% of the time at 200 observations.
    10. And it gets WORSE with more data — 65.0%, 74.3%, 80.8%, 88.5%.
    11. With drift it is 100.0%. Two things that both go up will always look related.
    12. The average R² between unrelated walks is 0.235 and the average |t| is 7.9.
    13. Differencing rescues both walks completely: 80.8% and 100.0% both drop to 5.0%.
    14. Detrending does not — 72.2% in both cases. A random walk has no trend to remove.
    15. Over-differencing hurts: stationary noise goes from 4.7% to 12.3%.
    16. But differencing costs nothing when there is real signal — still detected 100% of the time.
