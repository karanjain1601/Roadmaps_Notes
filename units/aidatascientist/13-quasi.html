CHAPTER 13

When you cannot randomise

Comparing the two groups gives 10.00 and comparing the two periods gives 5.00, against a true effect of 2.00. Difference-in-differences removes both nuisances and lands on 2.00 exactly — provided the trends were parallel, and its bias turns out to be precisely the amount by which they were not. Regression discontinuity comes with something better: a direct test of its own key assumption.

    THE SITUATION

    When randomising is genuinely impossible

    Not "inconvenient". Impossible, or unethical, or already too late.

  1. The change is market-wide. A price change, a brand campaign, a regulatory deadline. There is no control arm because everyone gets it at once.
  2. The rule is a rule. Free shipping over £50, a discount for users above a loyalty tier. You cannot randomly deny it to half the qualifying users.
  3. It already happened. A competitor exited, an outage occurred, a policy shipped last quarter. Nobody planned an experiment and the question is real anyway.
  4. Randomising would be unethical or absurd. Most questions about harm, and most questions where the treatment is something a user chose.
    5. Where these sit on the ladder

    Chapter 1 measured the alternative: with a strongly confounded feature, comparing adopters with non-adopters reported 25.59 points against a true 2.00, and adjustment recovered it only when the confounder was both measured well and binned finely.

    The designs in this chapter do better than that, because each finds a source of variation that is almost as good as random. They are not as good as randomising, and the honest way to present one is always: here is the estimate, here is the assumption it rests on, and here is what I did to check it.

    THE WORKHORSE

    Difference-in-differences

    Two groups, two periods, and two nuisances that cancel.

    A feature launches in one market and not another. The treated market is bigger to begin with, and everything is growing over time. Neither fact has anything to do with the treatment, and each single comparison confuses one of them with it.

    The first row is the argument for the method. Comparing the two groups gives 9.997 — off by the level gap of 8. Comparing the two periods gives 4.996 — off by the time trend of 3. Each removes one nuisance; DiD removes both and lands on 2.000.

    And the bias is exactly the divergence

    Read down the last column: 0.000, +0.500, +1.500, +3.000, against divergences of 0.0, 0.5, 1.5 and 3.0. The DiD bias equals the amount by which the trends were not parallel. Not approximately — exactly, because that is what the estimator is.

    Which makes parallel trends not a technicality to mention in a footnote but the entire identifying assumption. And the uncomfortable part: it concerns the counterfactual post period, so it can never be verified. You can only build a case.

    BUILDING THE CASE

    What you can actually check

    Four things, in rough order of how much they buy you.

    The placebo test: run DiD on two pre-treatment periods
    Trend divergencePlacebo estimateVerdict
    0.00.003Clean
    0.50.503Flags a problem
    1.51.503Flags a problem
    3.03.003Flags a problem
    Run the placebo
    Apply the DiD estimator to two periods where nothing happened. It should return zero, and here it returns the divergence exactly — 0.503 when the trends diverge by 0.5. It cannot prove the trends would have stayed parallel afterwards, but it catches the groups that were already diverging, which is where most of the damage comes from.
    Plot many pre-periods
    Not two, all of them. Parallel lines before the intervention are the single most persuasive thing you can show, and a chart makes a slow divergence obvious in a way a single placebo number does not.
    Pick the control group deliberately
    A comparable market, a comparable cohort. "The rest of the world" is rarely a good control, and choosing the control group after seeing which one gives a nice answer is chapter 8's multiplicity in its most respectable disguise.
    Look for the other thing that happened
    The most common failure is not a subtle trend difference but a second event: the treated market also had a holiday, a competitor exit, a pricing change. Ask what else was different about that market at that moment.
    Two traps specific to DiD

    Parallel trends is not scale-free. Trends can be parallel in levels and divergent in logs, or the reverse. Choosing to analyse a ratio rather than a difference is choosing a different assumption, and it should be justified rather than defaulted into.

    Staggered adoption breaks the simple estimator. When different units are treated at different times, the standard two-way fixed-effects regression uses already-treated units as controls for later-treated ones — which can produce an estimate with the wrong sign even when every individual effect is positive. This is a well-documented failure with modern estimators to fix it, and it is worth knowing the phrase before running a staggered rollout analysis.

    THE SHARPEST DESIGN

    Regression discontinuity

    When a rule decides who gets treated, the people either side of the line are almost a randomised comparison.

    Free shipping over £100. A customer at £99 and a customer at £101 are near-identical in every way except that one got the treatment. That near-identity is the whole design.

    Comparing everyone above the threshold with everyone below gives 9.258 against a true jump of 4.00, because the outcome rises with the running variable anyway. RDD's contribution is to compare only near the cutoff.

    The bandwidth is a bias/variance dial, and you can read it off that table

    Wide is precise and biased: at 80 the spread is 0.177 and the bias +2.046, because a straight line fitted over a wide window cannot follow a curve. Narrow is unbiased and noisy: at 5 the bias is +0.059 and the spread 0.571, three times wider.

    This is the one genuinely difficult choice in RDD, and it is why serious implementations use a data-driven optimal bandwidth and then report the estimate across several. A single bandwidth with no sensitivity check is a warning sign, because moving from 80 to 10 changed the answer by two full points here.

    And the assumption RDD lets you test

    The design rests on units not being able to choose which side of the line they land on. Let them, and the estimate goes from 4.179 to 6.194 against a truth of 4.00 — because the people who nudge themselves over are not typical of those just below.

    But this failure leaves a fingerprint. Count the units just above the cutoff and just below: the ratio is 1.01 when nobody manipulates and 11.68 when most do. A pile-up on the treated side of a threshold is visible, testable, and it is the standard McCrary density check.

    That is the real advantage of RDD over difference-in-differences: its key assumption leaves observable evidence. Parallel trends does not.

    ONE TREATED UNIT

    Synthetic control

    What to do when the treated group is a single market and no other single market resembles it.

    You launched in Spain. No other country is a good comparison. Synthetic control builds one: a weighted combination of the untreated countries chosen so the weighted average tracks Spain's pre-treatment history closely, then uses that composite as the counterfactual.

    What it buys

    A control that matches on the pre-period trajectory, not just the level — which is a much stronger claim than picking the nearest-looking market by hand.

    And the fit is visible. Anyone can look at the pre-period chart and judge whether the synthetic control tracks.

    What it assumes

    That a combination matching the past would have matched the future. Same family as parallel trends, argued from a longer history.

    And that no donor market was affected by the treatment — chapter 9's interference, at the country level.

    How the inference works, which is unusual

    With one treated unit there is no sampling variation to appeal to, so significance comes from placebo permutation: run the same procedure pretending each untreated market was the treated one, and see where the real estimate falls in that distribution. If Spain's post-treatment gap is larger than 18 of 19 placebos, that is a p-value of roughly 0.05.

    It is the same logic as the DiD placebo above, generalised: the credibility comes from showing the method does not manufacture effects when applied where none exist.

    JUDGEMENT

    Reading a quasi-experimental claim

    Yours or someone else's. Four questions, and the last is the one people skip.

  5. What is the source of variation, and why is it as good as random? If there is no answer, this is chapter 1's rung-one comparison wearing a technical name.
  6. What is the assumption, stated plainly? "Parallel trends" is a phrase; "the two markets would have grown at the same rate had we not launched" is a claim someone can disagree with.
  7. What would make it false, and was that checked? A placebo, a density test, a pre-period plot. An analysis with no falsification attempt has not been tested.
  8. How much would the conclusion move if the assumption were mildly wrong? This is the one people skip, and the DiD table answers it directly: a divergence of 0.5 — barely visible on a chart — moved a true effect of 2.00 to 2.500, a 25% overstatement.
    9. The honest framing

    None of these designs is a substitute for randomising, and presenting one as though it were is the failure mode chapter 1 called the confident answer to an unanswerable question.

    What they are is a great deal better than the naive comparison, when their assumption holds — and the assumption is exactly the thing to put in the first paragraph rather than the appendix. "Estimated +2.5%, identified by difference-in-differences against the Nordic markets; parallel pre-trends hold over eighteen months but we cannot rule out a divergence coinciding with launch" is a sentence a decision can be built on.

    And when a randomised alternative exists, even a partial one — a staggered rollout, a holdout in one region, a switchback — it is almost always worth the delay. Chapter 9's designs beat everything here.
