CHAPTER 06

Confidence intervals, done honestly

Two error bars can overlap by 29% of their length and still differ significantly. Checking a chart for overlap is a test at p = 0.006, not 0.05, and it discards up to 31 points of power. And an effect sitting exactly on your decision threshold is called "clearly worth it" just 2.5% of the time — at every sample size, however much data you gather.

    THE CLAIM

    What an interval actually says

    A 95% interval does not say there is a 95% chance the truth is inside it. The difference matters less than people pretend, and more than nothing.

    The guarantee is about the procedure: if you repeated this study many times, 95% of the intervals produced this way would contain the true value. Your particular interval either does or does not — there is no probability left in it once the data is fixed.

    How much this matters in practice

    Less than the pedantry suggests, and here is the honest version. Treating an interval as a plausible range for the truth will not usually mislead you, and it is how essentially everyone reads one. The Bayesian object that does license that reading — a credible interval — frequently coincides with it numerically.

    What the distinction protects you from is one specific error: reading the endpoints as equally plausible as the middle. They are not. Values near the centre are far better supported than values at the edges, and an interval that just barely excludes zero is not evidence that the effect is at the far end.

    Wider is not worse, it is more honest
    A wide interval reports genuine uncertainty. A narrow one computed on clustered data, as in chapter 3, is confidently wrong. Narrowness is a property of the data, not a quality of the analysis.
    The level is a choice, not a fact
    95% is a convention. A 90% interval is narrower and a 99% one wider, on identical data. If someone reports 90% intervals without saying so, everything looks more precise than it is.
    An interval for a difference is what you want
    Not two intervals for two groups. This is the whole of the next section, and it is the most common way intervals are misread in practice.

    THE COMMONEST ERROR

    Overlapping error bars

    Everyone reads charts this way. It is a test, it is not the test you think, and it is much stricter than the one you meant to run.

    Two groups, each with its own 95% interval. The bars overlap slightly. The near-universal reading is "not significantly different" — and it is wrong, in a direction that is exactly computable.

    The arithmetic is worth doing once, because it settles the question permanently.

    Where 2.772 comes from

    Two estimates each with standard error SE. The bars fail to overlap only when the gap between the estimates exceeds 2 × 1.96 × SE = 3.920 SE.

    But the difference between two independent estimates has a standard error of √2 × SE, not SE. So significance at p < 0.05 requires a gap of only 1.96 × √2 = 2.772 SE.

    The non-overlap rule therefore demands 3.920 SE where the test demands 2.772. Expressed as a significance level, "do the bars overlap" is a test at α = 0.0056 — roughly one in 179, not one in twenty.

    1. Intervals may overlap by 1.148 SE — 29.3% of a bar's full length — and still be significantly different. That is not a marginal case; it is a substantial visible overlap.
    2. Under the null the overlap rule fires 0.5% of the time against the correct 4.9%. It is not a broken test, it is an unintentionally strict one.
    3. At a small real difference it finds 22.0% against the proper test's 52.8% — 30.8 points of power discarded by reading a chart instead of computing a difference.
    The asymmetry that makes this dangerous

    Non-overlapping bars do imply significance — the rule is conservative, so a positive reading is trustworthy. Overlapping bars imply nothing at all.

    So the error is one-directional: you will never wrongly declare a difference this way, and you will regularly miss real ones. Which is precisely the failure that does not announce itself, because a missed effect looks exactly like no effect.

    The fix is trivial: plot the interval for the difference, not two intervals for two groups. One bar, and whether it crosses zero is then the actual test.

    FOR A DECISION

    The four things an interval can tell you

    Significance is a two-way split. A decision needs four, because "different from zero" and "big enough to act on" are separate questions.

    Suppose an effect must exceed some threshold to be worth shipping. Position the interval against both zero and that threshold, and four genuinely different situations appear.

    The result that changes how you plan a study

    Look at the first three rows. When the true effect sits exactly on the decision threshold, the interval is called "clearly worth it" 2.4%, 2.5% and 2.6% of the time at n = 100, 400 and 2,000. Extending to n = 50,000 gives 2.2%. It is flat at 2.5% however much data you collect.

    That is exact, not empirical. The lower bound clears the threshold precisely when the estimate exceeds threshold + 1.96 SE, and the estimate is centred on the threshold with standard deviation SE — so the probability is P(z > 1.96) = 2.5%, at every sample size. More data shrinks the interval around the threshold rather than away from it.

    Proving an effect exceeds a threshold is a fundamentally harder problem than proving it is non-zero, and no amount of traffic solves it when the effect is genuinely borderline. What solves it is a real effect comfortably above the line: at twice the threshold, 98.9% at only n = 400.

    The last two rows are the pair from chapter 5, now quantified. Both report "not significant". At n = 4,000 the study ruled out anything that mattered 94.3% of the time — a genuine finding. At n = 50 it learned nothing 65.0% of the time — a failed study. Same words, opposite meanings, and only the interval width distinguishes them.

    CONTROLS

    What determines the width

    Three inputs, and only one is really under your control.

    The width of a 95% interval is roughly 2 × 1.96 × σ/√n
    InputEffectHow much control you have
    Sample sizeWidth falls as 1/√nReal, and quadratically expensive — chapter 7
    VariabilityWidth is proportional to σMore than people think — chapter 12
    Confidence level90% is 16% narrower than 95%Total, and almost always the wrong lever
    ClusteringMultiplies width by √(design effect)Structural — fix the analysis, not the width
    The underused one is variability

    Everyone reaches for sample size, which costs four times the traffic to halve the interval. Cutting σ is the other half of the same fraction and is frequently cheaper: trim outliers with a pre-agreed rule, use a ratio to a pre-period baseline, or condition on a covariate that predicts the outcome.

    That last one is CUPED, and chapter 12 shows it routinely delivering what would otherwise require several times the traffic — without touching the estimate.

    CONSTRUCTION

    Which interval to build

    Four situations, and one default that is wrong more often than it should be.

    A mean, moderate n: the t interval
    Estimate ± t × SE. Correct when the sampling distribution is roughly normal, which chapter 2 showed is a claim about the data rather than a given.
    A proportion: not the normal approximation
    The textbook p̂ ± 1.96√(p̂(1−p̂)/n) fails badly near 0 and 1 — at p̂ = 0 it produces an interval of zero width, which is a confident statement that the rate is exactly zero. Use Wilson or Agresti–Coull instead. Both are one line and neither has this failure.
    Anything without a formula: the bootstrap
    Medians, percentiles, ratios, differences in ratios. Resample the independent unit, and remember from chapter 3 that it fails structurally for extremes.
    A ratio metric: the delta method
    When the denominator is itself random — revenue per session, clicks per user — the naive standard error is simply wrong. This is common enough in experimentation to get its own chapter, chapter 11.
    The zero-width interval is worth recognising

    Zero conversions out of 200 gives a normal-approximation interval of [0.000, 0.000] — the arithmetic asserts the true rate is exactly zero on the basis of having seen nothing. The Wilson interval gives roughly [0.000, 0.019], which is the honest statement: the rate is low, and could be as high as about 2%.

    The same failure appears in chapter 2's rare-binary row, where a nominal 95% interval covered 9.7% at n = 5. When events are rare, the normal approximation is not slightly off; it is structurally broken.

    COMMUNICATING IT

    Reporting an interval

    The format does a surprising amount of the work.

    Weak

    "The difference was statistically significant (p = 0.03)."

    "Conversion improved by 2.1%."

    "No significant difference was found."

    Each discards the two things a decision needs: how big, and how sure.

    Better

    "+2.1 percentage points, 95% CI [0.3, 3.9]. Worth roughly £340k a year at the point estimate, £50k at the pessimistic end."

    "+0.4 points, 95% CI [−1.8, +2.6]. Consistent with a small harm or a moderate gain; this study could not distinguish them."

    Translate both ends into the decision's units

    An interval in percentage points is a statistical object. The same interval in pounds per year, or support tickets per week, is a decision object — and the lower end is usually the number that settles it, because that is the outcome you are committing to being no worse than.

    This also disciplines the analysis. If the lower end of your interval still justifies the project, ship it. If the upper end would not justify it, stop — and note that you could have worked that out from chapter 7's calculation before running anything.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. The 95% guarantee is about the procedure, not about your particular interval.
    2. The endpoints are not as plausible as the middle. That is the one misreading worth guarding against.
    3. Non-overlap requires a gap of 3.920 SE; significance requires 2.772 SE, because the difference has a standard error of √2 × SE.
    4. "Do the bars overlap" is therefore a test at α = 0.0056 — about one in 179, not one in twenty.
    5. Intervals can overlap by 29.3% of a bar's length and still differ significantly.
    6. The rule discarded 30.8 points of power at a small real difference: 22.0% against 52.8%.
    7. The error is one-directional. Non-overlap does imply significance; overlap implies nothing.
    8. Plot the interval for the difference, not two intervals for two groups.
    9. A decision needs four categories, not two: worth it, real but unclear, ruled out, learned nothing.
    10. An effect exactly at the threshold is called "worth it" 2.5% of the time at every n — 2.4%, 2.5%, 2.6%, 2.2% from n = 100 to 50,000.
    11. That is exact: the estimate is centred on the threshold, so clearing it needs a 1.96-SE fluke regardless of sample size.
    12. Proving an effect exceeds a threshold is harder than proving it is non-zero, and more data does not solve it.
    13. "Ruled out 94.3%" and "learned nothing 65.0%" both report as not significant. Only the width tells them apart.
    14. Width is driven by n, σ and the level. Cutting σ is the underused lever.
    15. Use Wilson for proportions. The normal approximation gives a zero-width interval at zero events.
    16. Report both ends in the decision's units. The lower end usually settles it.
