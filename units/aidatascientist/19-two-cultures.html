CHAPTER 19

From explanation to prediction

The ridge penalty that minimises prediction error is thirty-five times larger than the one that minimises coefficient error. At the prediction optimum the coefficient on a variable whose true value is 1.50 comes back as 0.208 — and the model predicts better for it. Two goals, two different answers, and no way to have both.

    THE SPLIT

    Two questions that look like one

    Both are answered by fitting a model to data. Almost nothing else about them is the same.

    What changes when the goal changes
    ExplanationPrediction
    The questionWhat would happen if we changed X?What will Y be for this case?
    What is estimatedA coefficientA function
    Success isAn unbiased effect with honest uncertaintyLow error on data you have never seen
    CollinearityA serious problemMostly harmless
    Adding a variableRequires a causal argumentRequires only that it helps
    BiasDisqualifyingOften worth buying
    The killer failureOmitted confoundersLeakage
    The row that surprises people is the bias row

    Everything in chapter 15 treated bias as the thing to avoid: a wrong functional form gave 3.800 against a true 2.000 and no standard error repaired it. That framing is correct for explanation and wrong for prediction, where deliberately biasing an estimate to reduce its variance is the central technique.

    Chapter 4 already produced half of this. With predictors correlated at anything from 0 to 0.999, the prediction error stayed at 0.118 throughout while the spread of a single coefficient grew twenty-two-fold. Collinearity destroyed attribution and did nothing at all to prediction — because the model only needs the sum of the collinear effects, which is perfectly well determined.

    THE TRADE MADE VISIBLE

    The penalty that predicts best is not the one that attributes best

    Ridge regression gives us a dial between the two goals. Turning it shows they have different optima.

    Eight predictors, three of which matter and five of which do not, with the first two correlated at 0.97. Forty training observations. The penalty λ shrinks every coefficient toward zero.

    The two columns bottom out in different places

    Coefficient error is lowest at λ = 1. Prediction error is lowest at λ = 35, thirty-five times further along the dial. Choose the prediction optimum and the coefficients get worse (0.6420 against 0.5125); choose the coefficient optimum and the predictions get worse (2.2382 against 2.1443).

    And look at what happens to b₁ along the way: 1.5321 at λ = 0, then 0.8623, 0.4872, and 0.2077 at the prediction optimum — against a true value of 1.50. A model tuned to predict as well as possible reports a coefficient seven times too small, and is right to.

    Plain OLS gives 1.5321, essentially unbiased, and predicts worst of all. That is the trade in a single row.

    Which is why "the model says feature X matters most" is usually a category error

    If the model was tuned for prediction — and any model selected by cross-validated error was — its coefficients have been deliberately distorted to reduce variance. Reading them as effect sizes reads the distortion as a finding.

    This applies with more force to tree ensembles and gradient boosting, where feature importances are affected by correlated features, cardinality and the fitting order, and have no interpretation as effects at all. Chapter 20 covers what they can and cannot support.

    WHY BIAS HELPS

    Where prediction error comes from

    Three parts, and only two of them are yours to manage.

    Irreducible noise

    The variation in Y that nothing could predict. In the demo above, σ = 2.0 — so a prediction error of 2.14 is already close to the floor.

    Worth estimating early: it tells you when to stop.

    Bias and variance

    Bias: the model is too simple to represent the truth. Variance: it responds too much to which particular sample it saw.

    Ridge trades the second for the first, and the demo shows the trade paying.

    The winner's curse, wearing different clothes

    Chapter 7 measured a study at 11% power reporting a mean effect 3.65× the truth among significant results. Chapter 14 found the biggest gap between twenty identical segments averaging 0.26 on a true effect of 0.20, and shrinkage collapsing an apparent spread of 0.0699 to 0.0079.

    Ridge is the same operation with the same justification: extreme estimates are extreme partly because of noise, so pulling them in gets closer to the truth. Empirical-Bayes shrinkage, James-Stein, ridge and regularisation in a neural network are one idea in four notations.

    And the same caveat carries over. Chapter 14's shrinkage left a true spread of 0.30 almost untouched at 0.2881. Regularisation removes what the noise can explain, and no more.

    THE PROJECT-ENDING FAILURE

    Leakage

    Omitted confounders ruin explanation. Leakage ruins prediction, and it is far easier to commit by accident.

    The demo below contains nothing. The features are pure noise, the target is a coin flip, and there is no relationship of any kind to find.

    87% accuracy on a coin flip

    Choosing the fifteen best features on the full dataset and then cross-validating reports 87.3%. There is nothing there. The cross-validation is real, the folds are honest, the code has no bug — and the number is meaningless, because the feature choice already saw every test fold.

    Choosing inside each fold reports 49.0%, which is correct.

    And the illusion grows with the number of features — 77.9%, 84.0%, 87.3%. The more you measure, the more convincing the nothing becomes, because with 4,000 noise features some fifteen of them will correlate with any 120-row target by chance. This is chapter 8's multiplicity, arriving through a door nobody watches.

    And the honest method still finds real signal

    Worth checking, or in-fold selection would just be a way of rejecting everything. Hide eight genuinely predictive features among the 4,000 and it scores 93.4%. The correct procedure is not conservative — it is correct.

    The rule generalises past feature selection to every decision made from data: scaling, imputation, outlier removal, encoding, threshold tuning, hyperparameter search. If it looked at the test rows, it leaked. In practice this means the entire pipeline goes inside the fold, which is exactly why the scikit-learn Pipeline object exists.

    Target leakage
    A feature that is a consequence of the outcome rather than a cause. num_cancellation_emails predicts cancellation superbly and is unavailable before it. The tell is an implausibly strong single feature; the test is whether it exists at the moment you need the prediction.
    Temporal leakage
    Using information from after the prediction point. Chapter 18 measured this: a shuffled split not only inflated the score but ranked two candidate models the wrong way round.
    Group leakage
    The same user, patient or device appearing in train and test. The model recognises the entity rather than learning the pattern, and chapter 3's rule applies unchanged: split at the level of the independent unit.
    Preprocessing leakage
    The demo above. Anything fitted on all the data before splitting, including the innocuous-looking steps.

    MODEL CLASS

    Choosing something to fit

    Less consequential than the two sections above, which is itself the point.

    What each is actually good at
    ModelStrong whenWeak when
    Regularised linearWide data, linear-ish signal, coefficients wantedInteractions matter and are not specified
    Gradient boostingTabular data, almost always the first thing to tryExtrapolating beyond the training range
    Random forestRobust default, few knobsUsually a little behind boosting
    Neural networksImages, text, audio, very large dataModest tabular data, where they rarely win
    k-nearest neighboursA baseline, and low dimensionsMany features — distance stops meaning anything
    The order that actually pays

    1. Get the evaluation right. A leaky test harness makes every subsequent decision meaningless, including which model class won — chapter 18's ranking flip.

    2. Get the features right. Nearly always worth more than the model choice on tabular data.

    3. Then try gradient boosting and a regularised linear baseline. If they are close, take the linear one.

    The common failure is doing these backwards: an elaborate model search on top of an evaluation that cannot tell the candidates apart.

    JUDGEMENT

    Reading a prediction claim

    Five questions, in the order that finds problems fastest.

  1. What is the baseline? Accuracy of 88% is meaningless if the majority class is 90%. Chapter 18 made the same point for forecasts, and the answer is the same: report it alongside, always.
  2. Was every fitted decision inside the fold? Selection, scaling, imputation, tuning. The 87.3% row is what this question is for.
  3. Would every feature exist at prediction time? Ask it feature by feature for anything suspiciously strong.
  4. Does the test set resemble deployment? Same population, same period, same entities absent from training.
  5. Is anyone reading the coefficients as effects? If the model was tuned for prediction, they are not effects — b₁ came back at 0.208 against a true 1.50.
    6. The honest way to hold both

    Most real projects want prediction and an explanation, and the temptation is to get one model to do both. It cannot: the demo above shows the two objectives optimised thirty-five-fold apart on a single dial.

    What works is being explicit about which you are doing at any moment. Fit the prediction model for prediction and evaluate it out of sample. If you also need an effect, estimate it separately — with the design of chapter 9 if you can randomise, the methods of chapter 13 if you cannot, and the controls chosen by causal argument rather than by cross-validated error.

    Two models, two purposes, both honest. One model asked to do both will quietly do neither.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Explanation and prediction differ on every row that matters, including whether bias is disqualifying.
    2. Collinearity destroys attribution and barely touches prediction — chapter 4's 0.118 at every correlation from 0 to 0.999.
    3. Coefficient error is lowest at λ = 1; prediction error is lowest at λ = 35.
    4. At the prediction optimum the coefficients are worse (0.6420 against 0.5125) and vice versa (2.2382 against 2.1443).
    5. b₁ goes 1.5321, 0.8623, 0.4872, 0.2077 as the penalty rises — against a true 1.50.
    6. A model tuned to predict best reports a coefficient seven times too small, and is right to.
    7. So "the model says X matters most" reads a deliberate distortion as a finding.
    8. Prediction error is noise plus bias plus variance, and only the last two are yours.
    9. Ridge is chapter 14's shrinkage and chapter 7's winner's curse in another notation.
    10. Choosing features on the whole dataset then cross-validating gave 87.3% accuracy on pure noise.
    11. Choosing inside each fold gave 49.0%, which is correct.
    12. The illusion grows with the feature count — 77.9%, 84.0%, 87.3%.
    13. And in-fold selection is not conservative: given eight real features among 4,000 it scores 93.4%.
    14. The rule covers every fitted decision — scaling, imputation, tuning — not just selection.
    15. Get evaluation right, then features, then the model class. The common failure is the reverse order.
    16. If you need a prediction and an effect, build two models. One asked to do both will do neither.
