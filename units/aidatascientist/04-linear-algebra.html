CHAPTER 04

Linear algebra for data

Least squares is not an optimisation that happens to have a formula. It is a projection, the residual is orthogonal to every column of X to machine precision, and R² is exactly the squared cosine of an angle. Reading it that way explains the chapter's other result: at a predictor correlation of 0.99 the coefficient spread grows sevenfold while the prediction error does not move at all.

    THE OBJECTS

    Data as vectors, two ways round

    The same table admits two readings, and switching between them is most of what this chapter is for.

    Rows as points

    Each customer is a point in a space with one axis per feature. Distance means similarity, clusters mean groups, and a model draws a surface through the space.

    This is the reading behind k-nearest neighbours, clustering and embeddings, and it is the one most people already have.

    Columns as vectors

    Each feature is a vector in a space with one axis per observation. A thousand customers means a thousand-dimensional space, and each column is one arrow in it.

    This is the reading that makes regression obvious, and almost nobody is taught it first. The rest of the chapter uses it.

    The dot product measures alignment
    For centred, scaled vectors it is the correlation. Zero means orthogonal, which for centred data means uncorrelated. Every "how related are these two columns" question is a dot product.
    The norm measures size
    For a centred column, the norm is the standard deviation times √n. So "how much does this feature vary" and "how long is this vector" are the same question.
    A span is everything you can build
    The set of all weighted combinations of your columns. This is the crucial one: a linear model can only produce predictions inside the span of its features, and everything it cannot reach becomes residual.

    THE CENTRAL IDEA

    Least squares is a projection

    Your outcome y is a vector. Your predictors span a subspace. The fit is the closest point in that subspace, and "closest" has a geometric meaning that is exactly true rather than approximately.

    y almost certainly does not lie in the span of X — if it did, the fit would be perfect. So the best you can do is find the point in the span nearest to y. That point is the projection, and the line from y to it is perpendicular to the subspace.

    The geometry of a fit 200 observations, two predictors, coefficients recovered by least squares
    RESIDUAL · EACH COLUMN0should be exactly zero
    R²0
    ANGLE BETWEEN y AND ITS FIT0°cos² of this is R²
    PYTHAGORAS HOLDS TO0total = explained + residual

    Three facts fall out, and all three are exact rather than approximate.

    1. The residual is orthogonal to every column of X, at around 10−13 — floating-point zero. This is not a property the fit happens to have; it is the definition. Setting the derivative of the squared error to zero is the statement that the residual is perpendicular to the span.
    2. The sums of squares form a right triangle. Total 807.307 = explained 604.156 + residual 203.150, exactly. That is Pythagoras, and it is why the quantity is called a sum of squares at all.
    3. R² is the squared cosine of the angle between y and its projection. Measured: R² of 0.7484 against cos²θ of 0.7484, identical. An R² of 0.75 is an angle of 30.1 degrees.
    What this buys you when reading a model

    Adding a predictor adds a dimension to the span, so the projection can only get closer to y and R² can only rise. That is a geometric certainty, not an empirical finding — which is why an unadjusted R² can never be evidence that a variable belongs in a model.

    It also explains why a model cannot extrapolate outside its span, why perfectly collinear predictors produce no unique answer — the projection is unique but its coordinates are not — and why residual plots are the right diagnostic: any structure left in the residual is a direction the span failed to reach.

    THE OPERATOR

    A matrix is a transformation

    The second reading, and the one that makes decomposition make sense.

    Multiplying by a matrix takes a vector and returns another. Any such transformation can be understood as some combination of rotation, scaling and flattening, and the useful vocabulary is about which it does.

    The properties worth being able to read off
    PropertyGeometricallyWhy you care
    RankThe dimension of the output spaceRank below the column count means redundant features
    DeterminantHow much volume is scaledZero means the transformation flattens — information is lost
    EigenvectorA direction that is only scaled, never rotatedThe natural axes of the data
    EigenvalueHow much that direction is scaledFor a covariance matrix, the variance along that axis
    Condition numberRatio of the largest to smallest stretchHigh means the inverse amplifies noise — the next section
    The one to internalise is the condition number

    Solving least squares means inverting XᵀX. If that matrix flattens some direction almost to nothing, its inverse stretches that direction enormously — and stretches the noise along with it.

    That is not an abstraction. It is precisely the mechanism behind the next demo, and behind every regression output where two sensible-looking predictors both come back with enormous standard errors and opposite signs.

    WHEN THE SPAN COLLAPSES

    Collinearity destroys attribution, not prediction

    The most practically useful result in the chapter, and it is routinely stated as if collinearity broke the model. It does not.

    Two predictors, each with a true coefficient of 1.0, and a correlation between them that we can dial. The demo fits four hundred replicate datasets at each setting and measures how much the estimates move.

    Read that table across and the finding is unmissable. The prediction error column never moves — 0.118 at every correlation, identical to three decimals — while the spread of an individual coefficient grows from 0.074 to 1.643, a factor of twenty-two.

    And the sum is better determined than either part

    At a correlation of 0.99, b1 has a spread of 0.520 and is unusable on its own. But b1 + b2 has a spread of 0.074 — tighter than either coefficient at zero correlation.

    That is the geometry saying exactly what it knows. The data pins down the combined effect of the two predictors precisely, because that is the direction the span actually covers. What it cannot do is split that effect between two nearly identical columns, because moving weight from one to the other barely changes the fit.

    The coefficients also remain unbiased throughout — the mean of b1 stays at 1.0. They are correct on average and hopeless individually, which is a specific and unusual failure mode.

    The growth tracks the theory precisely: the spread of b1 grew 7.03× from r = 0 to r = 0.99, and √VIF predicts 7.09×. So the variance inflation factor is not a rule of thumb — it is the exact multiplier, and 1/(1 − r²) is worth remembering.

    Which makes "should I drop a collinear predictor" the wrong question

    It depends entirely on what the model is for, and chapter 19 is about that distinction.

    If you need prediction, do nothing. The table says collinearity costs you nothing at all. Dropping a predictor to "fix" a VIF is throwing away information to improve a diagnostic you do not need.

    If you need attribution — which of these two levers to pull — then no amount of cleverness recovers it from this data, because the data does not contain it. The honest responses are to report the combined effect, to collect data where the two vary independently, or to run an experiment that moves one and not the other.

    FINDING THE NATURAL AXES

    PCA is a rotation, and SVD is the general case

    If correlated predictors are the problem, the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix are one answer: a new set of axes that are uncorrelated by construction.

    The covariance matrix describes an ellipse. Its eigenvectors are the axes of that ellipse and its eigenvalues are the variance along each. Principal components analysis is nothing more than rotating to those axes and keeping the longest ones.

    Eigenvalues of a two-by-two covariance, computed exactly
    Var(x)CovVar(y)EigenvaluesFirst component explains
    1011.0000, 1.000050.0%
    10.611.6000, 0.400080.0%
    10.9511.9500, 0.050097.5%
    41.514.6213, 0.378792.4%
  1. The eigenvalues sum to the trace — total variance is conserved by a rotation, exactly. Verified to 10−9 at every setting above.
  2. They multiply to the determinant, which is why a near-zero determinant and a near-zero smallest eigenvalue are the same statement about collinearity.
  3. Reconstruction error is the sum of the discarded eigenvalues. At a covariance of 0.95, dropping to one component costs 0.0500 out of a total of 2.00 — you keep 97.5% of the variance in half the dimensions.
  4. None of them is negative, because a covariance matrix cannot have negative variance in any direction. A negative eigenvalue means a numerical problem, not a finding.
    5. Two things PCA is regularly asked to do and cannot

    It is not feature selection. Every component is a mixture of all the original variables, so you have not dropped any inputs — you still need to collect all of them. And the components usually have no interpretation, which trades a collinearity problem for an explanation problem.

    It is not scale-invariant. PCA on unstandardised data finds whichever variable has the largest units. Revenue in pounds and age in years will hand the first component to revenue, entirely because of the units. Standardise first, or you are doing PCA on your unit choices.

    The generalisation is the singular value decomposition, which does the same thing for any matrix rather than a square symmetric one. It underlies low-rank recommenders, latent semantic analysis and the compression view of embeddings, and the identity above — error equals the discarded singular values — is the Eckart–Young theorem.

    THE ROW READING AGAIN

    Distance, similarity and embeddings

    Back to rows as points, where one choice does most of the work.

    Euclidean distance cares about magnitude
    Straight-line distance. Sensible when the axes are comparable and every unit means the same thing. Fails badly when they are not, which is why standardising before any distance-based method is not optional.
    Cosine similarity cares only about direction
    The angle between two vectors, ignoring length. This is the default for text and embeddings because a long document and a short one on the same topic point the same way and have very different norms.
    They coincide when vectors are normalised
    On unit-length vectors, ranking by cosine similarity and ranking by Euclidean distance give identical orderings. Which is why vector databases normalise on write and then use whichever is faster.
    In high dimensions, distances concentrate
    As dimensions grow, the ratio between the nearest and furthest point approaches one, and "nearest neighbour" stops meaning much. This is the curse of dimensionality, and it is why embeddings are compressed to a few hundred dimensions rather than left at the vocabulary size.
    An embedding is the projection idea again

    A high-dimensional object — a document, a user's history, an image — is mapped to a few hundred coordinates chosen so that the directions that matter are preserved and the rest is discarded. That is the same operation as keeping the top components above, with a learned basis instead of an eigenbasis.

    Which is why the whole chapter is one idea in several costumes: represent the data in a space, find the subspace that carries the signal, and treat what is left over as residual. Regression, PCA, embeddings and recommenders are all that operation.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Read a table two ways: rows as points in feature space, columns as vectors in observation space. The second makes regression obvious.
    2. A linear model can only predict inside the span of its features. Everything outside becomes residual.
    3. Least squares is a projection, and the residual is orthogonal to every column of X — measured at around 10−13, which is floating-point zero.
    4. The sums of squares form a right triangle: 807.307 total = 604.156 explained + 203.150 residual, exactly.
    5. R² is the squared cosine of the angle between y and its fit — 0.7484 both ways. An R² of 0.75 is an angle of 30.1 degrees.
    6. Adding a predictor can only raise R², as a geometric certainty, so unadjusted R² is never evidence a variable belongs.
    7. Collinearity leaves prediction error completely untouched — 0.118 at every correlation from 0 to 0.999.
    8. It destroys attribution: the spread of b1 grew from 0.074 to 1.643, a factor of twenty-two.
    9. The coefficients stay unbiased throughout. Correct on average, hopeless individually.
    10. The sum is better determined than either part: at r = 0.99, b1 has a spread of 0.520 and b1 + b2 has 0.074.
    11. √VIF is the exact multiplier: 7.03× observed against 7.09× predicted.
    12. So "drop the collinear predictor" depends on the goal. For prediction, do nothing. For attribution, the data does not contain the answer.
    13. PCA rotates to the eigenvectors of the covariance. Eigenvalues sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant, exactly.
    14. Reconstruction error is the sum of the discarded eigenvalues — 0.0500 of 2.00 at a covariance of 0.95.
    15. PCA is neither feature selection nor scale-invariant. Standardise first or you are analysing your unit choices.
    16. Cosine and Euclidean agree on normalised vectors, and in high dimensions all distances concentrate.
