CHAPTER 22

Shipping a model, and watching it drift

The drift monitor most teams run trips hard on the failure that does no damage and stays completely silent on the one that takes AUC from 0.81 to 0.24. And a model retrained on the cases its own decisions let it observe does not decay gently — its true accuracy oscillates between 70.8% and 47.3% while the accuracy on the dashboard correlates with reality at r = 0.03.

    THE HANDOVER

    What changes when a model ships

    Everything up to this point assumed a fixed dataset. Production has none of those guarantees.

    The four assumptions that stop holding
    OfflineIn productionCovered in
    The data distribution is fixedIt moves, in three distinguishable waysNext section
    Labels are availableThey arrive late, partially, or neverThe monitoring section
    The model does not affect the dataIts decisions determine what you observeThe feedback section
    Features are computed the same wayTraining and serving pipelines divergeBelow
    Training-serving skew deserves its own mention because it is so common

    A feature computed with a window function over a historical table and recomputed live from a streaming source will not match, and the mismatch is silent. The model receives inputs it was never trained on and degrades without any distribution having actually changed.

    The fix is structural rather than vigilant: compute features once, in one piece of code, used by both paths. That is what a feature store is for, and it is why the discipline exists at all.

    Covariate shift trips it at 1.605 standard deviations — a loud alarm. And the model is fine: AUC 0.8185 against a baseline 0.8122, calibration error 0.0064. Nothing is wrong, because P(y|x) has not changed and the model was never asked about anything else.

    Concept drift leaves it at 0.024 — completely silent, because the inputs look exactly as they always did. Meanwhile AUC has fallen to 0.2437 and accuracy to 34.1%. The relationship between inputs and outcome has inverted, and the input distribution cannot possibly show that.

    So the monitor most teams run fires on the harmless case and misses the fatal one. Of the two genuinely damaging cases here it catches zero.

    Label shift is chapter 20's split arriving as a production failure

    The base rate moves and the model predicts 43.2% against an observed 68.7%. Calibration error 0.2545, and the ranking survives untouched at 0.8150.

    That is exactly chapter 20's finding — four models with bit-identical AUC differing by up to 34% in cost — happening to one model over time instead of across four models at once. If the score feeds a threshold or an expected-value calculation, this is a serious failure that every ranking metric will report as fine.

    It is also the easiest of the three to fix: recalibrate on recent labelled data and the ranking you already have becomes usable again.

    WHAT TO WATCH

    Monitors, in order of what they buy

    Ranked by how much real failure each one catches per unit of effort.

    1. Operational health
    Error rates, latency, null rates per feature, request volume. Boring, and it catches the most common production failure by far: a broken upstream pipeline feeding nulls or stale values into a model that scores them without complaint.
    2. Calibration against outcomes, as they arrive
    Mean predicted against observed, on whatever labels have landed. One line of code, catches label shift immediately, and it is the monitor the table above shows earning its place. The lag is the problem, not the computation.
    3. Prediction distribution
    Available with no labels at all. It moves under covariate shift (0.3201 above) and, importantly, moves when an upstream pipeline breaks. Silent under concept drift, so it is a supplement rather than a safety net.
    4. Input distribution
    Popular, cheap, and the demo shows why it disappoints: it fires at 1.605 on the harmless case and 0.024 on the fatal one. Worth having as a diagnostic after something else has alarmed, and a poor primary alarm.
    5. The business metric
    The only thing that is definitely worth measuring, and the slowest and noisiest. Which is the argument for a permanent holdout: keep a small randomised slice unscored, and the difference is a continuous measurement of what the model is actually worth.
    Concept drift needs labels, and there is no way around it

    No label-free monitor can detect a change in P(y|x), because every label-free monitor only sees x. The demo makes this concrete: the input and prediction monitors both read exactly their no-drift values while the model collapses.

    So the practical answer is to shorten the label loop rather than to find a cleverer unsupervised statistic. Label a small random sample deliberately and quickly, even when the natural labels take months. A few hundred fast labels a week detects concept drift that no amount of distribution monitoring ever will.

    THE SELF-INFLICTED ONE

    When the model decides what you get to see

    Credit approvals, fraud review, content moderation, recommendations. If the model's decision determines which outcomes you observe, retraining on those outcomes has a problem.

    The demo below approves the top 20% by score, observes labels only for those, and retrains on recent data. The truth is mildly curved and the model is linear — which is the normal case, since no model is exactly right.

    It does not decay. It oscillates.

    True accuracy runs 70.8, 47.3, 69.2, 69.3, 55.2, 70.0, 69.8, 53.7 — a 24-point swing, crashing to near chance and recovering on a roughly three-generation cycle. I expected a slow decline and the model does something worse: it is unstable, so any single generation's reading tells you almost nothing about the next one.

    And the number the team actually sees is uncorrelated with all of it: apparent accuracy correlates with true accuracy at r = 0.03. Generation 3 shows the dashboard's best reading, 79.6%, while generation 2's true accuracy was 47.3%. The monitor is not lagging or noisy. It is measuring something else.

    But the mechanism is visible without any labels at all

    The spread of the training inputs tracks true accuracy at r = 0.933: 1.37, 1.04, 1.52, 1.51, 1.11, 1.38, 1.38, 1.11 against the accuracy series above. When the approved region narrows, a linear fit to a curved truth becomes locally excellent and globally wrong; when it happens to re-broaden, the model recovers.

    That is an actionable finding. Monitor the diversity of what you are training on, not just the distribution of what you are scoring — and treat a narrowing training set as the alarm it is.

    The fix is a few per cent of deliberate exploration

    Approve 18% by score and 2% at random and the instability disappears: true accuracy runs 70.5, 65.0, 70.3, 70.3, 67.3, 70.3, 70.4, 68.4 — a swing of 5.5 points against 24, and it never crashes.

    Those random approvals cost something real, and they are the only thing that keeps the training set representative of the population you actually face. They also give you an unbiased estimate of performance in the region the model rejects, which is the thing you otherwise have no way to learn.

    It is the same principle as chapter 9's holdout and chapter 10's A/A test: pay a small known cost for information you cannot otherwise obtain.

    MAINTENANCE

    Retraining

    Less obvious than it looks, and the demo above is the reason.

  1. Retrain on a trigger, not a schedule. Monthly retraining is a ritual; retraining when calibration drifts past a threshold is a response. And retraining fixes covariate and label shift while doing nothing for a broken pipeline.
  2. Validate every retrained model against the incumbent before it ships. The demo's generation-3 model looked best on the dashboard and the generation-2 model was the disaster. Offline comparison on a fresh, randomly sampled set is the only version that means anything.
  3. Watch the training set, not just the model. The input-spread correlation of 0.933 is the leading indicator here, and it needs no labels.
  4. Keep the exploration budget through every retrain. It is the first thing cut when someone optimises the approval rate, and its value only shows up as an absence of failures.
  5. Automate the rollback, not just the deploy. If the new model can go out automatically, it must be able to come back automatically.

    6. GOING LIVE

    Shipping it

    The rollout is an experiment, and Part 3 already covered how to run one.

    Shadow mode first
    Score live traffic, act on nothing, compare predictions with what happens. Catches training-serving skew and pipeline breakage before any user is affected, and costs nothing but compute.
    Then a randomised rollout
    Not a percentage ramp by itself — a randomised comparison against the current policy, measured on the business metric. Chapter 9 applies unchanged, including its warning about interference: a recommender changes what everyone sees, so user-level randomisation may understate or overstate the effect.
    Guardrail metrics, agreed in advance
    Latency, error rate, and the outcomes the model is not optimising. Chapter 14's multiplicity applies: with twenty guardrails, something will fire.
    Keep the holdout permanently
    Not just for the launch. A small unscored slice is the only continuous measurement of what the model is worth, and it is what tells you whether a year of retraining has helped or quietly cost you.
    The launch is the cheapest measurement you will ever get

    At launch there is a natural comparison, an obvious reason to randomise, and everyone's attention. Six months later, establishing what the model is worth requires turning it off for some users, which is a conversation nobody wants to have.

    So set up the permanent holdout at launch, when it is free, rather than deciding later that you need one.

    JUDGEMENT

    Reading a production model

    Five questions to ask about something already running.

  6. How would you know if it stopped working? If the answer is a feature-drift dashboard, the answer is that you would not — 0.024 while AUC fell to 0.2437.
  7. How long until a label arrives, and what happens in the meantime? This sets the maximum speed of every response.
  8. Does the model influence its own training data? If yes, ask what the exploration budget is. If there is none, the performance you see is not the performance you have.
  9. Is there a holdout? Without one, nobody knows what the model is worth, only what it predicts.
  10. Are training and serving features computed by the same code? If not, they have diverged; the only question is by how much.
