CHAPTER 09

Designing an experiment

Randomisation removes confounding, and it does not remove everything. When two arms compete for the same limited stock, a full rollout delivers 0.23 percentage points and the A/B test reports 4.42 — a 19-fold overstatement from a perfectly executed experiment. Randomise too finely instead and you measure 19.1% of a real effect while reporting a standard error 2.6 times too small.

    THE FIRST DECISION

    What gets randomised

    Everything else in the design follows from this, and it is usually decided by whatever the platform makes easy.

    The rule that generates the answer

    Randomise at the coarsest unit across which effects can leak, and no coarser.

    Too fine and the arms contaminate each other, so you measure a diluted version of the effect. Too coarse and you have very few units, so you cannot measure anything precisely. The two demos in this chapter are those two failures, and they pull in opposite directions.

    Note that the analysis unit is a separate decision from the randomisation unit, and getting that wrong is the more common error. If you randomise by user, you must analyse by user — the second demo measures what happens otherwise.

    WHEN UNITS ARE NOT INDEPENDENT

    Interference through a shared resource

    The assumption underneath every A/B test is that one user's assignment does not affect another user's outcome. In a marketplace it is simply false.

    A feature raises purchase intent by 25%. Stock is finite. Treatment users want more of it, and every unit they take is one a control user cannot have.

    The A/B test is measuring a transfer, not a gain

    Nothing is wrong with the randomisation. Assignment is perfectly random, the arms are balanced, the p-value is valid, and the estimate answers a real question — just not the one anyone asked.

    What it measures is treatment users outbidding control users for a fixed pool of stock. That is a genuine effect within the experiment. It vanishes the moment everyone is treated, because then there is nobody left to take the stock from.

    The same structure appears wherever supply is shared: ride-hailing drivers, ad inventory, marketplace listings, support agents, warehouse capacity. It is the reason marketplace teams distrust user-level A/B results, and they are right to.

  1. Positive spillover runs the other way. If treating a user makes their contacts more likely to convert, control is lifted rather than depressed, and the A/B understates. Same violation, opposite sign, and much harder to notice because the result looks conservative.
  2. Cluster randomisation is the standard fix — randomise whole markets, so competition happens within an arm rather than across arms. In the table it tracks the truth far better at every stock level.
  3. Switchback designs alternate the whole system between treatment and control over time. Common in dispatch and pricing, where you cannot split the market spatially.
  4. Budget-split designs give each arm its own share of the constrained resource, which removes the competition directly but changes what you are measuring.
    5. What cluster randomisation costs

    It is unbiased and it is expensive. In the demo, going from 100 markets to 10 raises the spread of the estimate 1.9 times — because markets genuinely differ from one another, and with ten of them you are effectively running an experiment with ten observations.

    That is the whole trade in one line: user-level randomisation here is precise and wrong; cluster randomisation is right and imprecise. Which you prefer depends on how badly the interference bites, and the only way to know that is to measure it — typically by running both and comparing, or by varying the treated fraction and watching the estimate move.

    THE OPPOSITE FAILURE

    Randomising too finely

    More units means more power, which is why session-level randomisation is tempting. Here is what it costs.

    Suppose the effect persists once a user has seen the feature — they learn where the button is, or the recommendation changes what they browse. Randomise by session and a user who saw it in session one carries the benefit into session two, whatever label that session gets.

    The two designs fail in opposite ways, and only one of them looks wrong

    Session-level randomisation is precise and diluted. At ten sessions per user it reports 0.057 against a truth of 0.300 — under a fifth of the real effect — and its standard error is honest. Everything about the output looks healthy; the number is simply too small.

    User-level randomisation is unbiased and easy to analyse wrongly. It reports 0.305, correct. But if you analyse it at the session grain — one row per session, as the event table arrives — you report a standard error of 0.0495 when the estimate actually varies by 0.1272. Understated 2.6 times.

    Aggregating to one row per user before analysing gives 0.1246 against a true 0.1272 — a ratio of 0.98. Exact. This is chapter 3's design effect and chapter 5's clustering failure arriving as a design decision, and the fix is the same one: analyse at the grain you randomised at.

    Note what happens at one session per user: both designs agree, at 0.3007 and 0.2832. The problem only exists because users return, which is to say it exists in essentially every product.

    WHAT YOU MEASURE

    Choosing the metric before you run

    Named in advance, singular, and matched to the decision. Three properties, each of which is routinely violated.

    It has to move for the reason you think
    A feature that surfaces more content raises page views by construction. That is a mechanical consequence, not evidence of value, and it will be significant every time.
    It has to be sensitive enough to detect
    Chapter 7's calculation, applied to the metric you will actually decide on. Revenue has far worse power than clicks, which is precisely why teams drift towards clicks and then find their revenue does not move.
    It has to be robust to the obvious gaming
    Ask what the laziest way to move this number would be. If a dark pattern would win the experiment, the metric is wrong — and someone will eventually find that out for you.
    It has to be a ratio you can compute a standard error for
    Revenue per session and clicks per user have a random denominator, and the naive standard error is simply wrong for them. That is common enough to get its own chapter, chapter 11.
    One primary, several guardrails, everything else exploratory

    Chapter 8 measured what happens without this discipline: twenty metrics at 0.05 hands you at least one false winner 65.5% of the time.

    Primary: one metric, named in the plan, on which the decision turns. Guardrails: latency, error rate, unsubscribes — checked for harm, not for wins, so no correction needed. Everything else: reported with intervals and explicitly labelled as hypothesis-generating.

    HOW LONG

    Duration is not just a sample-size question

    Power says how many users. Three other things say how many days.

    Run whole weeks

    Weekday and weekend users differ in almost every product. A test that runs Tuesday to Saturday over-samples one and under-samples the other, and the imbalance is not random.

    Whole weeks, always. It costs a couple of days and removes an entire class of argument.

    Wait out novelty

    A visible change draws attention that fades. Effects measured in the first days are frequently larger than the settled ones — chapter 14.

    The check is to plot the effect by day of exposure rather than by calendar day, which separates novelty from a genuine trend.

    Running longer is not the same as more users

    Extending a user-level test does not add users at the rate you might expect — it mostly adds more data per existing user, and chapter 3 showed what that is worth. At twenty rows per user with a modest correlation, ten thousand rows carried the information of 1,163.

    So a two-week test does not have twice the power of a one-week test. It has somewhat more, and how much depends on how many genuinely new users arrive in week two.

    And extending because the result is nearly significant is chapter 8's peeking with an extra step, which took the false-positive rate from 5.0% to 25.1%.

    WRITING IT DOWN

    The experiment plan

    One page, before anything is built. Most of its value is that it forces the decisions above to be made deliberately rather than by default.

    experiment-plan.md
    # Recommendation carousel on the home page

## Decision
Ship / do not ship, by 14 March. Owner: Priya.
If it ships, it replaces the existing "popular now" row.

## Hypothesis
Personalised recommendations raise repeat purchase, because current
browsing data shows users leaving after failing to find category X.

## Design
- Randomisation unit: USER (effects persist across sessions)
- Analysis unit: USER (aggregate sessions before analysing)
- Split: 50/50, all logged-in users in GB
- Interference: none expected — stock is not constrained in this category.
  If it were, this would need market-level randomisation.

## Metrics
- Primary: repeat purchase rate within 28 days
- Guardrails: p95 home-page latency, unsubscribe rate, support contacts
- Exploratory: category mix, session length  (NOT decision-grade)

## Power
- Baseline 11.4%, 62,000 users per arm over two whole weeks
- MDE at 80% power: 0.62 pts absolute, 5.4% relative
- We expect 4–8% relative. The MDE sits inside that range, so a null
  result will be genuinely ambiguous. Accepted, because the decision
  deadline is fixed.

## Analysis
- Welch's t on user-level rates, two-sided, alpha 0.05
- No interim looks. If we must look, O'Brien–Fleming boundaries.
- Pre-registered segments: new vs returning. Nothing else is confirmatory.

## What would change our minds
- Ship if the interval clears +0.6 pts
- Do not ship if it clears zero downward, or if latency regresses
- If the interval spans both, we do not ship and we say why
    The two lines that do most of the work

    "The MDE sits inside the range we expect, so a null result will be ambiguous." Writing that down before the test converts a future argument into a present decision. Chapter 6 measured the alternative: an effect sitting on the decision threshold is called conclusive 2.5% of the time, at every sample size.

    "What would change our minds." Stating the ship rule in advance is what stops the result being reinterpreted afterwards, and it is the cheapest protection against everything in chapter 14.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Randomise at the coarsest unit across which effects can leak, and no coarser.
    2. Too fine contaminates the arms; too coarse leaves too few units. The two failures pull in opposite directions.
    3. Interference breaks the assumption that one user's assignment does not affect another's outcome.
    4. With very scarce stock a full rollout delivers 0.23 points and the A/B reports 4.42 — a 19.3× overstatement from a correctly executed experiment.
    5. The A/B is measuring a transfer: treatment users outbidding controls for fixed stock, which vanishes at full rollout.
    6. Positive spillover runs the other way and is harder to notice, because it makes results look conservative.
    7. Cluster randomisation tracks the truth far better at every stock level in the table.
    8. And costs 1.9× the spread going from 100 markets to 10, because markets genuinely differ.
    9. Session-level randomisation measured 19.1% of a persistent effect at ten sessions per user, and 10.5% at twenty.
    10. It is diluted but honestly reported. Nothing in the output looks wrong.
    11. User-level randomisation analysed at the session grain understates the SE 2.6× — 0.0495 against a true 0.1272.
    12. Aggregating to one row per user gives 0.1246 against 0.1272. Exact. Analyse at the grain you randomised at.
    13. At one session per user both designs agree, so the problem exists only because users return.
    14. Name one primary metric, a few guardrails, and label the rest exploratory.
    15. Run whole weeks, and separate novelty by plotting against day of exposure rather than calendar day.
    16. Write down what would change your mind before the result arrives.
