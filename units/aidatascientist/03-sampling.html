CHAPTER 03

Sampling, estimation and the bootstrap

A sample of a thousand tells you almost exactly as much about sixty million people as about a hundred thousand — the fraction sampled is nearly irrelevant, and the count is everything. But twenty rows per user at a modest within-user correlation makes ten thousand rows worth 1,163, and the bootstrap covers the maximum 0% of the time at every sample size tested.

    THE CENTRAL IDEA

    The sampling distribution

    Everything in Part 2 rests on one thought experiment, and it is worth stating precisely because it is usually skipped.

    You have one sample and computed one number from it. If you had drawn a different sample you would have got a different number. The sampling distribution is the distribution of that number across all the samples you might have drawn — and its standard deviation is what we call the standard error.

    Two distributions that get confused, and confusing them is expensive

    The population distribution describes individual observations: how revenue varies across customers. It does not get narrower with more data — more customers does not make customers more alike.

    The sampling distribution describes an estimate: how the sample mean would vary across repeated samples. This does get narrower with more data, at a specific and unforgiving rate.

    "Our revenue per user is £42 ± £38" is almost certainly the first when the writer meant the second, and the difference is usually a factor of √n.

    Two properties matter for everything that follows. An estimator is unbiased if its sampling distribution is centred on the truth, and consistent if it converges to the truth as n grows. Those are different: an estimator can be unbiased and hopelessly imprecise, or slightly biased and much more useful — which is the entire argument for the shrinkage in chapter 14.

    THE COST OF PRECISION

    Root n, and what does not matter

    Precision costs quadratically

    The standard error is σ/√n, so halving it takes four times the data and a tenth of it takes a hundred times.

    Measured: n = 100 gives 0.1000, n = 400 gives exactly 0.0500, n = 10,000 gives exactly 0.0100. This is the single most important constraint on experiment design, and chapter 7 is about living within it.

    The population size does not

    A sample of 1,000 gives a standard error of 0.03146 from a population of 100,000 and 0.03162 from 60 million — a difference of half a percent.

    The correction only bites when you sample a large fraction: from a population of 2,000, a sample of 1,000 is 29% narrower, because you have surveyed half of everyone.

    Why the polling question has a surprising answer

    "How can 1,000 people represent 60 million?" is the most common objection to survey research, and the answer is that the fraction sampled is almost irrelevant — what matters is the count. A sample of 1,000 is as informative about a country as about a city.

    The finite population correction is √((N−n)/(N−1)), which is essentially 1 whenever n is small relative to N. At n = 1,000 out of 60 million it changes the standard error in the fifth decimal place.

    Which relocates the real problem. Polls go wrong because of who answers, not how many — and no sample size fixes a biased sampling frame.

    THE HIDDEN COST

    Clustered data and the design effect

    The row count in your table is not the sample size. Usually it is much larger.

    Ten thousand sessions from five hundred users are not ten thousand independent observations. Sessions from the same person resemble each other, so each additional session from a user already in the sample carries less information than a session from a new one.

    The design effect is 1 + (m − 1) × ICC, where m is the cluster size and ICC is the within-cluster correlation. At twenty rows per user with a correlation of 0.4, ten thousand rows are worth 1,163, and treating them as independent understates the standard error by a factor of 2.9.

    What that factor does to a p-value

    An estimate that is 2.9 standard errors from zero using the naive calculation is 1.0 standard errors from zero using the correct one. The first is significant at any conventional threshold; the second is indistinguishable from noise.

    And this is the default failure in analytics data, because event tables are naturally at the event grain while the thing you are measuring lives at the user grain. Nothing warns you: the row count is large, the estimate is stable-looking, and the interval is simply wrong.

    The fixes are all forms of respecting the grain: aggregate to one row per user before analysing, cluster the standard errors by user, or randomise and analyse at the cluster level — which is chapter 9's argument for choosing the randomisation unit deliberately.

    THE GENERAL TOOL

    What the bootstrap does

    A way to get an interval for almost any statistic without deriving anything.

    The sampling distribution is the distribution of your estimate across samples you did not draw. The bootstrap approximates it by resampling your own data with replacement, recomputing the statistic each time, and reading the spread of the results.

    bootstrap.py
    import numpy as np

def boot_ci(x, stat=np.mean, reps=10_000, level=0.95, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)          # per-analysis, not global
    n = len(x)
    ests = np.array([stat(rng.choice(x, size=n, replace=True)) for _ in range(reps)])
    lo, hi = np.quantile(ests, [(1 - level) / 2, 1 - (1 - level) / 2])
    return lo, hi

# works for statistics with no convenient formula
boot_ci(revenue, stat=np.median)
boot_ci(revenue, stat=lambda s: np.quantile(s, 0.9))
boot_ci(pairs,   stat=lambda s: np.corrcoef(s[:, 0], s[:, 1])[0, 1])

# clustered data: resample USERS, not rows
users = df["user_id"].unique()
picked = rng.choice(users, size=len(users), replace=True)
resample = pd.concat([df[df.user_id == u] for u in picked])

    That last block matters more than the rest. The bootstrap must resample whatever your independent unit is. Resampling rows from clustered data reproduces the design-effect error exactly, and produces a confidently narrow interval.

    THE LIMITS

    Where the bootstrap fails

    It is close to universal and it is not universal, and the failure is silent.

    Zero, and it does not improve with n

    The reason is structural rather than statistical. A resample can never contain a value larger than the largest value in your sample, so every bootstrap estimate of the maximum is at or below the sample maximum — which is itself always below the true maximum. The interval sits entirely beneath the answer, every time.

    More data does not help, because the mechanism is not noise. At n = 500 the coverage is still 0.0%. Compare the mean, which is fine everywhere: the bootstrap works when the statistic depends on the bulk of the distribution and fails when it depends on the edge.

  1. Extremes: maxima, minima, the range. The demo's case. Use extreme value theory instead, which is built for exactly this.
  2. Very small samples. With n = 8 there are only so many distinct resamples, and the bootstrap distribution is visibly lumpy. The median is worse than the mean here, because it can only ever take one of a few observed values.
  3. Clustered or dependent data resampled at the wrong grain. Not a limitation of the method but the most common way it is misused, and it produces intervals that are too narrow rather than obviously broken.
  4. Heavy tails, where it inherits the problem rather than solving it. Better than the normal approximation of chapter 2, and still under-covering, because a resample can only contain whales you happened to observe.

    5. IN PRACTICE

    Where samples go wrong before any of this

    The arithmetic above assumes a random sample. That assumption fails far more often than the arithmetic does.

    The frame excludes people
    You sampled from logged-in users, or from people who completed onboarding, or from rows that have a value in a nullable column. Each is a population, and none is the one you meant. This is the most common error in the whole chapter and it happens in the WHERE clause.
    Non-response is not random
    Survey respondents differ from non-respondents in exactly the ways you are asking about. A 20% response rate is not a random 20%, and increasing the sample size makes the estimate more precisely wrong — the bias does not shrink with n while the interval does.
    Survivors are a filtered sample
    Analysing current customers to find out why customers leave. Analysing successful campaigns to find what makes campaigns succeed. The observations that would have answered the question are the ones missing from the table.
    The data was collected for something else
    Nearly all analytical data is exhaust from a system built for a different purpose, so its inclusion rules encode decisions nobody documented. "Which rows are in this table, and why" is worth ten minutes before any of the calculations here.
    Bias and variance do not shrink together

    Everything in this chapter reduces variance: more data, narrower intervals, better estimates of the standard error. None of it touches bias. A biased sample of ten million gives a very precise estimate of the wrong quantity, and the confidence interval will be reassuringly tight around it.

    So the order of operations is: establish what population the rows represent, then worry about sample size. Reversing those is how organisations spend a quarter improving the precision of a number that was measuring the wrong people.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. The population distribution describes observations; the sampling distribution describes an estimate. Only the second narrows with more data.
    2. The standard error is σ/√n, so halving it takes four times the data and a tenth takes a hundred times.
    3. The population size barely matters: a sample of 1,000 gives 0.03146 from 100,000 people and 0.03162 from 60 million.
    4. The correction only bites when you sample a large fraction — 1,000 from 2,000 is 29% narrower.
    5. Polls go wrong through who answers, not how many. No sample size fixes a biased frame.
    6. The design effect is 1 + (m − 1) × ICC. Twenty rows per user at 0.4 makes 10,000 rows worth 1,163.
    7. That understates the standard error by 2.9×, which turns a clearly significant result into indistinguishable-from-noise.
    8. Clustering is the default in event data, because tables are at the event grain and the question is at the user grain.
    9. The bootstrap resamples your data to approximate the sampling distribution, for statistics with no convenient formula.
    10. It must resample the independent unit. Resampling rows from clustered data reproduces the design-effect error exactly.
    11. Bootstrap coverage of the maximum was 0.0% at every n tested, because a resample can never exceed the sample maximum.
    12. That does not improve with data, since the mechanism is structural rather than noise. It works on the bulk and fails at the edge.
    13. Check the frame before the arithmetic: exclusions, non-response, survivors, and data collected for another purpose.
    14. More data shrinks variance and never bias. A biased sample of ten million is precisely wrong.
