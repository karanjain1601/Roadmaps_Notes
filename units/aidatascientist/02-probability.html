CHAPTER 02

Probability you actually use

"A sample over thirty is enough for the central limit theorem" is right for two of the five distributions tested here, and another needs a sample of five thousand. On a heavy-tailed variable, a nominal 95% interval covers 78.9% at n = 30 — and the misses are one-sided, so the error is a systematic understatement rather than noise.

    RECOGNITION

    Distributions worth recognising on sight

    Not for the formulae. For knowing what kind of thing you are holding, which determines what is safe to do with it.

    The ones that come up, and what they imply
    DistributionGenerated byTypical ofThe implication
    NormalMany small additive effectsMeasurement error, heights, sumsMean and sd describe it fully
    LognormalMany small multiplicative effectsIncome, revenue, session length, file sizeWork on a log scale; the mean is not typical
    PoissonRare events at a constant rateArrivals, defects, clicks per hourVariance equals the mean — check whether it does
    BinomialFixed trials, constant probabilityConversions out of visitsTrials must be independent, and often are not
    ExponentialWaiting between Poisson eventsTime to next order, time to failureMemoryless — a strong and testable claim
    Power lawPreferential attachmentCity sizes, sales by product, degreeThe mean may not converge at all
    The most useful single fact in that table

    Sums of many small effects go normal; products of many small effects go lognormal. Almost every business quantity is generated multiplicatively — a conversion rate times a basket size times a frequency — which is why revenue, session length and order value are right-skewed with monotonous reliability, and why np.log1p appears so often in this kind of work.

    That also predicts the rest of this chapter: if your data is lognormal rather than normal, the standard results about sample means need far more data than the usual advice suggests.

    THE THEOREM, AND ITS SMALL PRINT

    Where the central limit theorem needs more data than you think

    The honest test is not whether a histogram of sample means looks bell-shaped. It is whether a nominal 95% interval actually contains the truth 95% of the time.

    Four things follow, and the last is the one that changes how you read a result.

    1. "n over 30" is right for two of these five and badly wrong for two others. The smallest tested sample reaching honest coverage ranges from 5 to 5,000 — a factor of a thousand — and it is a property of the distribution rather than a universal constant.
    2. A heavy lognormal still under-covers at n = 1,000, at 92.3%. Revenue per customer looks exactly like this, and a thousand customers is not an unusual sample.
    3. A rare binary event fails worst of all at small n — 9.7% coverage at n = 5. With a 2% rate and five observations you almost always see zero events, the estimated standard deviation is zero, and the interval has no width at all.
    4. The misses are one-sided. On the lognormal at n = 30, 21.1% of intervals fall entirely below the true mean and 0.0% fall entirely above. That is not noise — it is a systematic understatement, in a known direction.
    Two separate problems, and the t correction only fixes one

    Switch the critical value to 1.96 and a perfectly normal population covers 88.1% at n = 5, against 94.8% with t. That undercoverage has nothing to do with the CLT — it is using the wrong critical value, and the t distribution fixes it exactly.

    Now try the lognormal at n = 30: 77.9% with 1.96 and 78.9% with t. The t correction moves it by one point. Skew and the critical value are independent problems, and only one of them has a standard fix.

    What actually helps with skew: work on a log scale and report the geometric mean or the median, use a bootstrap interval (chapter 3, with its own limits), or accept that you need far more data than the rule of thumb suggests.

    THE TAIL

    When the mean is the wrong summary

    Heavy-tailed data breaks more than the interval. It breaks the idea that the average describes anything.

    The mean is not typical
    For a lognormal, the mean sits well above the median and describes almost nobody. Reporting "average revenue per user" from a distribution where 1% of users are 60% of revenue tells the reader something arithmetically true and practically misleading.
    The sample mean is unstable
    Add one whale and it jumps. That instability is exactly what the coverage table measures: the estimate moves more than its own standard error suggests, so the interval is too narrow.
    For a power law with a small exponent, the mean may not exist
    If the exponent is at or below 2, the population variance is infinite and the sample variance grows with n rather than converging. At or below 1, the mean itself does not converge. This is not a pathology invented for textbooks — it shows up in file sizes, network traffic and insurance claims.
    The maximum grows with the sample
    For heavy tails, the largest observation you have is mostly a statement about how long you have been looking. A "record high" in such a series is the expected outcome of collecting more data, not an event.
    What to do instead

    Report the median and a couple of percentiles alongside the mean, and let the gap between them be the finding. Work on a log scale where the arithmetic behaves and the distribution is roughly symmetric. And if the total matters, say so explicitly — sometimes you genuinely care about the sum, and then the mean is the right object even though it describes no individual.

    The distinction is between "what does a typical user do" and "what does the business receive", and heavy-tailed data makes those two questions have very different answers.

    CONDITIONING

    Base rates, in production

    A model with excellent stated accuracy can produce alerts that are wrong nineteen times in twenty, and this is arithmetic rather than a modelling failure.

    A fraud model runs on a million transactions a day. It catches 90% of real fraud and has a 5% false-positive rate — both respectable. Fraud occurs in 0.3% of transactions.

    The measured result is worth sitting with. At a 0.3% base rate, the 90/95 model gives 5.1% precision — 49,850 false alerts a day against 2,700 real ones, or eighteen false alerts for every genuine case. Nothing is wrong with the model.

    Specificity dominates, and it is not the number people optimise

    Raising sensitivity from 90% to 99% moves precision from 5.1% to 5.6%. Raising specificity from 95% to 99.9% moves it from 5.1% to 73.0%.

    The reason is the arithmetic of the base rate: false positives are drawn from the 99.7% of transactions that are clean, so a one-point change in the false-positive rate is worth vastly more than a one-point change in recall. Yet sensitivity is the number that gets reported, because it sounds like the model's job.

    And the requirement is steep. For half the alerts to be real at this base rate, the false-positive rate must fall to 0.271% — from 5%. That is not a threshold adjustment; it is a different model.

    The general form: when the event is rare, your false positives are drawn from a much larger pool than your true positives, so the pool size dominates the rates. This is the same arithmetic as medical screening, and the same as chapter 8's false discovery rate, and it is why "the model is 95% accurate" is close to meaningless without the base rate beside it.

    READING IT CORRECTLY

    Conditional probability, in words that work

    Most confusion here dissolves when the same fact is restated as counts.

    The form people get wrong

    "The test is 95% accurate and you tested positive, so there is a 95% chance you have it."

    This confuses P(positive | disease) with P(disease | positive). They are different quantities and, when the condition is rare, they differ by an enormous factor — 90% against 5.1% in the demo above.

    The form that works

    "Out of 1,000,000 transactions, 3,000 are fraud and the model catches 2,700. Of the 997,000 clean ones it flags 49,850. So of 52,550 alerts, 2,700 are real."

    Same arithmetic, and almost nobody gets it wrong stated this way. Natural frequencies are read correctly by specialists and non-specialists alike, and percentages are not.

    The habit worth building

    Whenever you write a conditional probability, write the counts underneath it. Not for the reader — for yourself. It is the cheapest available check on whether the quantity you computed is the one you meant, and it catches the reversed conditional every time.

    The same trick makes Bayes' theorem unnecessary for most practical purposes: build the two-by-two table of counts and read the answer off it.

    THE ASSUMPTION UNDERNEATH

    Independence, and where it quietly fails

    Nearly every standard error assumes it. In behavioural data it is usually false, and the consequence is always in the same direction.

  1. Repeated measurements from the same person. Ten sessions from one user are not ten independent observations. Chapter 3 measures what this does: at twenty rows per user with a modest within-user correlation, ten thousand rows are worth about 1,163.
  2. Users within an account, a household or a team. The same problem one level up, and the usual reason an experiment randomised at the user level gives results that do not replicate.
  3. Observations close in time. Today's traffic resembles yesterday's. This is autocorrelation, it inflates apparent significance in any time series, and chapter 17 is about it.
  4. Anything with network effects. If treating one user affects another, the arms are not independent of each other and the estimate is biased rather than merely imprecise — chapter 9.
    5. The failure is always in the same direction

    Dependence between observations means each new row carries less information than an independent one would. So the true standard error is larger than the formula says, the interval is too narrow, and the p-value is too small.

    Which makes this a systematic bias towards finding effects, not a wash. Every one of the four cases above makes results look more significant than they are, and none of them produces an error message.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Sums of small effects go normal; products go lognormal. Most business quantities are generated multiplicatively, which is why they are reliably right-skewed.
    2. Test the CLT by coverage, not by the shape of a histogram. Does a nominal 95% interval actually contain the truth 95% of the time?
    3. "n over 30" was right for two of five distributions and wrong by up to a factor of a thousand. The smallest tested n reaching 94% ranged from 5 to 5,000.
    4. A heavy lognormal still covers only 92.3% at n = 1,000. Revenue per customer looks like this.
    5. A rare binary event covered 9.7% at n = 5, because you usually observe zero events and the interval has no width.
    6. The misses are one-sided: 21.1% of intervals fell entirely below the true mean and 0.0% above. A systematic understatement, not noise.
    7. The t correction and the skew problem are independent. With 1.96 a normal population covers 88.1% at n = 5; with t, 94.8%. On the lognormal, t moves coverage by one point.
    8. For heavy tails the mean is not typical, the sample mean is unstable, and for a power law with a small exponent it may not converge at all.
    9. At a 0.3% base rate, a 90/95 model gives 5.1% precision — 18 false alerts for every real case, and 49,850 a day on a million transactions.
    10. Specificity dominates. Sensitivity 90→99% moves precision to 5.6%; specificity 95→99.9% moves it to 73.0%.
    11. Half the alerts being real needs a false-positive rate of 0.271%, down from 5%. That is a different model, not a threshold change.
    12. Restate conditional probabilities as counts. Almost nobody reverses the conditional when it is written as natural frequencies.
    13. Dependence always biases in the same direction: intervals too narrow, p-values too small, results that look more significant than they are.
