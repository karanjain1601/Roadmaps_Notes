CHAPTER 15

Regression, read properly

Six things can go wrong with a regression and they do not go wrong in the same way. Heteroskedasticity, heavy tails and correlated errors leave the estimate exactly where it was and wreck the standard error — correlated errors took coverage to 37%. A wrong functional form and an omitted variable damage the estimate itself, which no correction repairs. And the omitted-variable bias matches its textbook formula to four decimal places.

    THE OBJECT

    What a coefficient actually means

    Three readings of the same number, in increasing order of how much they assume.

    The arithmetic reading
    The average difference in Y between observations that differ by one unit in X and are identical on every other included variable. This is always true, requires no assumptions, and is what chapter 4's projection computes.
    The descriptive reading
    "Holding the other variables constant, X and Y are associated by this much." Also fine, provided you say which other variables. The phrase "controlling for confounders" is meaningless without a list.
    The causal reading
    "Changing X by one unit would change Y by this much." This is a claim about a world you did not observe, and it needs every relevant confounder to be included, measured well and specified correctly. Chapter 1 measured what happens otherwise: 25.59 against a true 2.00.
    The word doing the work is "identical"

    "Holding everything else constant" is a statement about the columns in your table, not about the world. If two customers differ in X, they differ in a hundred other ways, and the regression only holds constant the handful you gave it.

    Which is why the same coefficient can be a fact, a description or a fantasy depending on nothing but what else is in the model — and why the next section is the most important arithmetic in this chapter.

    THE CENTRAL RESULT

    Omitted variable bias

    Leave out a variable that matters and the coefficients you keep absorb part of it. The amount is not a mystery — it has a formula, and the formula is exact.

    Compare the last two columns. The bias equals b₂ × δ to four decimal places at every correlation, where δ is the coefficient from regressing the omitted variable on the included one.

    Which makes the direction predictable without any data

    Two signs determine everything. If the omitted variable raises Y and is positively correlated with X, your coefficient is too big. Flip either sign and it is too small; flip both and it is too big again.

    That is a genuinely useful thing to be able to do in a meeting. "We left out customer income, which raises spending and correlates positively with tenure, so the tenure coefficient is overstated" is an argument you can make before running anything.

    And it vanishes at zero correlation — 1.9944 against a true 2.0000. An omitted variable only biases you if it correlates with something you kept. A variable that matters enormously for Y but is unrelated to X costs you precision and no accuracy.

    THE MATRIX

    Which violation damages which claim

    Textbooks list the assumptions as a block. They are not equivalent, and knowing which is which decides whether you need a different model or just a different standard error.

    Read the bias column and the coverage column together, and the six rows fall into two groups.

    Damage the standard error only

    Heteroskedasticity, heavy tails and correlated errors give slopes of 1.998, 1.996 and 2.010 against a true 2.000. The estimate is fine.

    What breaks is the interval around it — 84.5% and 37.0% coverage. These are fixable, and the next section fixes them.

    Damage the estimate

    A wrong functional form gives 3.800 and an omitted variable 3.055, both against a true 2.000. Coverage is 0.0% in each case, and it would be 0.0% however wide you made the interval.

    No standard-error correction repairs a biased estimate. These need a different model.

    Correlated errors are the worst, and only in a specific circumstance

    The classical standard error reports 0.0880 when the estimate actually varies by 0.3559 — understated four-fold, with 37.0% coverage.

    But this only happens when the regressor is also correlated within cluster. A shared shock on Y alone inflates the residual variance uniformly, which the classical formula handles correctly. The failure needs both: users in the same market share a shock and resemble each other on X.

    Which is exactly the situation in chapter 9's cluster-randomised experiment, and it is why treatment assigned at group level is the classic case where naive standard errors collapse.

    REPAIRS

    Robust and clustered standard errors

    Two corrections, and applying the wrong one buys nothing at all.

    Coverage of a nominal 95% interval, by standard error
    What is wrongClassicalRobust (HC)Clustered
    Heteroskedasticity84.5%91.3%90.5%
    Heavy-tailed errors95.3%95.8%93.0%
    Correlated errors37.0%36.5%90.8%
    The bottom row is the lesson

    Under correlated errors, robust standard errors give 36.5% coverage against the classical 37.0%. They do nothing, because HC corrections handle non-constant variance across observations and this problem is correlation between observations. Clustering takes it to 90.8%.

    "We used robust standard errors" is therefore not a general reassurance. It is a specific fix for a specific problem, and reaching for it under clustering is a fix that looks like diligence and changes nothing. The same fix as chapters 3, 5 and 9, arriving in regression clothing: cluster at the level the correlation lives at.

  1. Robust standard errors cost almost nothing when unneeded — 95.8% against 95.3% on heavy tails, 91.3% against 84.5% where they help. Reasonable as a default.
  2. Clustered standard errors need enough clusters. With fewer than about 40 they become unreliable in the other direction, which is chapter 9's cost of cluster randomisation.
  3. Neither fixes a biased estimate. Both scale the interval; neither moves the point.
  4. The bootstrap over clusters agrees with the clustered formula and needs no derivation — chapter 3, resampling the independent unit.

    5. Prediction is a third question again. A wrong functional form costs 1.6× the prediction error, and an omitted variable 1.5×, while heteroskedasticity leaves the slope untouched and still raises the error — a model can predict well and attribute wrongly, or attribute correctly and predict badly. Chapter 19 is about that separation.

    WHICH CONTROLS

    The same variable, three different roles

    "Control for everything you can" is wrong, and the demonstration is unambiguous.

    The same regression, run with and without one control variable
    Role of CWhat you wantWithout controlWith controlCorrect
    Confounder — C causes both X and Y2.0003.0522.000WITH
    Collider — X and Y both cause C2.0002.0010.830WITHOUT
    Mediator — X causes C causes Y1.720 total1.7230.600 directDepends
    The collider row is the one that surprises people

    Without the control the estimate is 2.001 — correct. Add the control and it becomes 0.830. Controlling for a variable that X and Y both cause creates a spurious association where none existed.

    The intuition: among customers with the same support-ticket count, one who bought more must have had fewer problems per purchase. Conditioning on a common consequence induces a relationship between its causes.

    And nothing in the output flags it. The R² rises, the control is significant, the model looks better by every diagnostic. The same statistical operation is required, forbidden and optional depending on the causal role of the variable — and the causal role is not in the data.

    Do not control for anything downstream of the treatment
    Mediators, colliders and anything measured after X. This is the same rule as chapter 12's covariate rule, which turned a true 0.1000 into 0.0400, and the same as chapter 10's triggered analysis.
    Do control for causes of both X and Y
    These are confounders, and omitting them is the previous section's bias. Note that this requires you to have measured them well — chapter 1's adjustment demo showed a noisy confounder leaves most of the bias intact.
    For a mediator, decide which effect you want
    Total effect (do not control) or direct effect (control). Both are legitimate; reporting the direct effect and calling it "the effect" is not.
    Draw the diagram before choosing
    Five minutes with boxes and arrows settles it, and there is no way to settle it from the data. Two models with identical fit can imply opposite policies.

    IN PRACTICE

    Reading a regression table

    Six questions, in the order that finds problems fastest.

  5. What is the unit of observation, and are the units independent? The 37% coverage row. Ask this first because it is the most common and the most damaging.
  6. What is missing that causes both X and Y? And which direction would it bias things? You can answer that from the two signs alone.
  7. Is anything in the model downstream of X? Colliders and mediators, which look like good controls and are not.
  8. Does the functional form fit? Plot residuals against fitted values and against each predictor. Structure there means the 3.800 row, and no standard error fixes it.
  9. Are the coefficients stable across specifications? If dropping one control moves the coefficient of interest by half, the result is a specification choice rather than a finding.
  10. What does the coefficient mean in the decision's units? A coefficient of 0.0031 per pound of spend may be highly significant and worth nothing.
    11. The two-line summary of the whole chapter

    If the estimate is unbiased, fix the standard error. Robust for heteroskedasticity, clustered for correlation between observations, and check you have picked the right one — the wrong correction bought 36.5% against 37.0%.

    If the estimate is biased, no standard error saves you. Fix the functional form, find the missing confounder, or accept that the coefficient is a description rather than an effect and say so.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. A coefficient has three readings — arithmetic, descriptive, causal — in increasing order of what they assume.
    2. "Holding everything else constant" means the columns in your table, not the world.
    3. Omitted variable bias equals b₂ × δ — matched to four decimal places at every correlation tested.
    4. Two signs give the direction: an omitted variable that raises Y and correlates positively with X inflates your coefficient.
    5. It vanishes at zero correlation — 1.9944 against a true 2.0000. A variable can matter enormously and cost you no accuracy.
    6. Heteroskedasticity, heavy tails and correlated errors leave the estimate alone: 1.998, 1.996, 2.010 against 2.000.
    7. A wrong functional form gave 3.800 and an omitted variable 3.055, both with 0.0% coverage.
    8. Correlated errors are the worst SE failure: reported 0.0880 against a true spread of 0.3559, covering 37.0%.
    9. But only when the regressor is also correlated within cluster. A shared shock on Y alone is handled correctly.
    10. Robust standard errors do nothing for clustering: 36.5% against the classical 37.0%. Clustered takes it to 90.8%.
    11. So "we used robust standard errors" is a specific fix, not a general reassurance.
    12. Neither correction moves a biased point estimate. Both only scale the interval.
    13. Controlling for a confounder took 3.052 to 2.000. Required.
    14. Controlling for a collider took 2.001 to 0.830. Forbidden — and R² rises while it happens.
    15. For a mediator, 1.723 is the total effect and 0.600 the direct one. Both legitimate; calling the second "the effect" is not.
    16. If the estimate is unbiased, fix the standard error. If it is biased, nothing else will do.
