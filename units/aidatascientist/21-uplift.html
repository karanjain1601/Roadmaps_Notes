CHAPTER 21

Uplift: predicting the effect, not the outcome

A perfect conversion model — one that knows every user's true probability — delivers 23.0 incremental conversions per thousand treated, against random targeting's 33.4 and uplift targeting's 249.8. A better outcome model does not fix that, because it is answering a different question from the one the campaign asks.

    THE MISMATCH

    Targeting the likely instead of the persuadable

    The standard pipeline builds a churn model, ranks by risk, and sends a retention offer to the top decile. Here is what that costs.

    A perfect outcome model does worse than random

    At a 25% budget: 23.0 against random's 33.4. Ranking by baseline rate is worse still, and at a 50% budget it goes negative — the campaign destroys value.

    The reason is that the people most likely to convert are largely people who would have converted anyway. Targeting them buys the offer's cost and almost no incremental conversion, while the users who would have moved sit outside the top quartile.

    This is not an accuracy problem. The model here has no error at all. Making it better cannot help, because the quantity it estimates is not the quantity the decision needs.

    And treating fewer people produces more

    Treating the best 25% by uplift yields 62.4 total incremental conversions per 1,000 users. Treating everyone yields 33.0 — less than half, at four times the cost.

    That is not a rounding effect. It happens because some users are made worse by the treatment, and at full coverage their negative contribution cancels much of the gain from everyone else.

    Ranking by conversion probability orders these sure things, persuadables, sleeping dogs, lost causes — the highest-value group second and the harmful group third. Ranking by uplift orders them persuadables, sure things, lost causes, sleeping dogs, which is the order a budget should be spent in.

    Which shows up in the marginal curve

    Take the additional users each ranking brings in, band by band. Uplift ranking adds 39.0, then −6.7, then −150.3 per thousand — falling monotonically, harmful group last. Outcome ranking adds 212.3, then −112.9, then +9.6 — not ordered at all.

    So the uplift curve tells you where to stop and the outcome curve cannot. A campaign ranked by conversion probability has a band of users it is actively harming, and the aggregate hides it: the total stays positive while a quarter of the audience is pushed the wrong way.

    Sleeping dogs are real, not a textbook device

    A retention call that reminds a quiet customer they are still paying. A discount email that teaches people to wait for discounts. A survey that surfaces a grievance. A "we miss you" notification to someone who had forgotten they had the app.

    They are hard to see because they are invisible in an aggregate A/B test that shows a positive average, and they are the reason a targeted campaign can beat a universal one on total effect rather than just on cost.

    THE HARD PART

    Why subtracting two good models gives a bad one

    At 600 rows both models are near-perfect and their difference is nearly useless

    0.9837 and 0.9823 against what each is predicting; 0.3272 for the difference against true uplift. Neither model is at fault — the baseline is a strong, shared signal that cancels when you subtract, leaving mostly the two models' estimation errors behind.

    The uplift signal is small relative to the baseline, so what survives the subtraction is a small true difference plus two noise terms that do not cancel.

    It is a variance problem, not a bias one: 0.3272, 0.4852, 0.7248, 0.9329 as the data grows. But note what that costs. The outcome models reach 0.98 at 600 rows; the difference has still not reached that at 40,000. Uplift modelling needs roughly two orders of magnitude more data than outcome modelling of comparable quality.

    T-learner (two models)
    The approach above. Simple, uses any model class, and suffers exactly the cancellation just measured. Reasonable when the treated and control groups are both large and the uplift is not tiny.
    S-learner (one model, treatment as a feature)
    Fit one model with treatment as an input and difference its predictions. Shares the baseline fit, so it avoids some cancellation — and regularisation may shrink the treatment feature toward zero, biasing uplift toward nothing.
    Transformed outcome
    With randomised assignment at probability e, the quantity Y·(T−e)/(e(1−e)) has expectation equal to the uplift, so any regression on it estimates uplift directly. Unbiased and high-variance, but it targets the right thing rather than a difference of two other things.
    Causal forests and X-learners
    Purpose-built. Causal forests split on treatment-effect heterogeneity rather than outcome, and give honest confidence intervals. The X-learner is designed for very unequal group sizes. Both are the right default when uplift is the goal and data allows.
    All of them require randomised assignment

    Every method here estimates a causal quantity, and none of them removes confounding. Fitting an uplift model on observational data where treatment was targeted reproduces the targeting rule rather than the effect — chapter 1's 25.59 against a true 2.00, with extra machinery.

    If assignment was not random, you need chapter 13's designs first, and the honest answer is often that the data cannot support an uplift model at all.

    MEASUREMENT

    Evaluating an uplift model

    You cannot score it per user, because no user has both outcomes.

    This is the fundamental problem of causal inference in its most practical form: for each person you observe either the treated outcome or the untreated one, never the difference. So chapter 20's per-case metrics do not apply, and the evaluation has to work in groups.

    The uplift curve
    Rank the held-out users by predicted uplift, then for each prefix compute the observed difference in outcome between treated and control users within that prefix. A good model produces a curve that rises steeply then flattens; the demo's marginal bands are this curve in coarse form.
    Qini coefficient
    The area between that curve and the diagonal a random ranking would produce — an AUC analogue for uplift. Useful for comparing models, and it inherits chapter 20's caveat: it summarises ranking, not the size of the gain.
    Report the optimal cut-off
    The uplift curve's peak is where the marginal user's effect crosses zero, and it is the number the campaign actually needs. Treating the top 25% beat treating everyone by 62.4 to 33.0 in the demo, and that is the kind of statement to put in the summary.
    Validate the whole thing with an experiment
    Randomise between the model's targeting and the current policy, and measure the difference. This is the only evaluation that cannot be fooled, and chapter 9 covers running it properly.
    The noisy model is still worth having

    Even at 600 rows, with a difference correlating only 0.3272 with true uplift, the uplift ranking gained 0.0533 against outcome ranking's −0.0331 on the top 30%. A poor estimate of the right quantity beat a perfect estimate of the wrong one at every sample size tested.

    And note that the outcome ranking's figure is negative at all four sample sizes: it reliably selects a group whose average uplift is below zero, because the strongest baseline driver enters the uplift with the opposite sign. That is the demo's version of sleeping dogs, and it is the normal case rather than a contrivance.

    IN PRACTICE

    When it is worth doing

    Uplift modelling is expensive in data and in complexity. Three conditions make it pay.

  1. The treatment is costly or has a downside. If sending the email is free and harmless, treat everyone and skip all of this. The value of uplift modelling scales with what you save by not treating someone.
  2. The effect genuinely varies between people. Chapter 14 is the prerequisite: with no real heterogeneity, hunting through twenty segments produced a "finding" 59% of the time. Run the single heterogeneity test before building anything.
  3. You have randomised data at scale. Both conditions above plus the two-orders-of-magnitude data requirement measured earlier.
    4. The cheap version that is often enough

    Before building an uplift model, run the experiment and look at the effect within two or three pre-registered segments. If new users respond and returning users do not, targeting new users captures most of the available value with none of the machinery.

    Chapter 14's shrinkage applies to those segment estimates, and its warning applies to the temptation to keep slicing: the apparent spread across twenty identical segments was 0.0699 against a true zero.

    JUDGEMENT

    Reading an uplift claim

    Five questions, and the first is the one that usually ends it.

  4. Was the training data randomised? If treatment was targeted, the model has learned the targeting rule.
  5. Is the reported metric about uplift or about outcome? An AUC for predicting conversion says nothing about a model's uplift ranking.
  6. What does the uplift curve look like, and where does it peak? If nobody computed a cut-off, the model is not being used for what it is for.
  7. Are there sleeping dogs? Ask whether the bottom of the ranking has a negative estimated effect and whether those users are currently being treated.
  8. Has it been validated against the current policy in an experiment? Offline uplift metrics are noisy; a head-to-head test is not.
    9. Part 5 in one thread

    Chapter 19: a model tuned to predict reports coefficients that are not effects — b₁ at 0.208 against a true 1.50. Chapter 20: a metric can be blind to everything a decision depends on, with four models at bit-identical AUC differing by up to 34% in cost. This chapter: a perfect prediction can point at the wrong people.

    The through-line is that prediction quality and decision quality are different things, and the gap between them is where most of the value is lost. Each chapter measures a different way of losing it, and each fix is the same shape: work out what quantity the decision needs, then estimate that.
