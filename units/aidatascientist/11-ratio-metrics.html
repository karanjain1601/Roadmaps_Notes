CHAPTER 11

Ratio metrics and the delta method

Clicks per session, revenue per user, items per order. When the denominator is itself random, the standard error every tool computes by default is wrong — a nominal 95% interval covered the truth 68.8% of the time here. The delta method fixes it in one line, and its covariance term is not optional: dropping it made the standard error 67% too large.

    RECOGNITION

    Most of your metrics are ratios

    And the ones that cause trouble have a specific signature: the denominator counts something the user controls.

    The test that sorts them

    Ask: is the denominator determined by the randomisation, or by the user's behaviour?

    If you randomise by user and count users, the denominator is fixed the moment you assign — and every standard formula applies, because each user is one independent observation contributing one number.

    If the denominator is sessions, orders or impressions, the user decides how much of it to produce. Then two things are random and correlated, the unit of analysis is still the user, and the rows in your table are not independent observations. This is chapter 9's grain problem with an extra complication: the number of rows per user is itself part of the metric.

    The treacherous part is that the metric is often more sensible than the per-user version. Clicks per session controls for how often people visit, which is usually what you want. The fix is not to avoid the metric — it is to compute its standard error correctly.

    WHAT GOES WRONG

    The naive standard error

    One row per session, compute the variance, divide by the number of sessions. It is what every tool does by default, and here is what it costs.

    At high session variation the naive standard error is 0.00557 when the estimate actually varies by 0.01041 — understated by 1.87 times. The delta method gives 0.01019, which is right.

    Coverage is the honest way to read that

    A nominal 95% interval built on the naive standard error covers the true value:

    • 89.4% when session counts barely vary
    • 81.8% at moderate variation
    • 68.8% at high variation

    The delta method covers 94.6%, 93.8% and 94.6% across the same three settings. A "95% interval" that contains the truth barely seven times in ten is not a calibration nicety — it is a p-value of 0.01 that should have been 0.15.

    And notice the failure gets worse exactly as the metric gets more useful. Clicks per session is worth computing because users visit at different rates; the more that is true, the more wrong the default standard error becomes.

    THE FIX

    The delta method

    A first-order Taylor expansion of a ratio, which reduces to one line of arithmetic on per-user sums.

    Aggregate to one row per user: Xi is that user's total clicks, Yi their total sessions. The metric is R = X̄ / Ȳ, and its variance is:

    delta.py
    import numpy as np

def ratio_se(x, y):
    """
    SE of R = sum(x)/sum(y), where x and y are PER-USER sums
    and users are the independent unit.

        Var(R) ~= [ Var(X) - 2R*Cov(X,Y) + R^2*Var(Y) ] / (n * mean(Y)^2)
    """
    n = len(x)
    mx, my = x.mean(), y.mean()
    R = mx / my
    v = x.var(ddof=1) - 2 * R * np.cov(x, y, ddof=1)[0, 1] + R**2 * y.var(ddof=1)
    return np.sqrt(v / (n * my**2))

# aggregate FIRST, then apply. The row count that matters is users, not sessions.
per_user = df.groupby("user_id").agg(clicks=("click", "sum"),
                                     sessions=("session_id", "nunique"))
R  = per_user.clicks.sum() / per_user.sessions.sum()
se = ratio_se(per_user.clicks.values, per_user.sessions.values)

# for a two-arm experiment, compute R and se in each arm, then:
#   diff = R_t - R_c ; se_diff = sqrt(se_t**2 + se_c**2)
    The covariance term is doing real work

    Drop it and the standard error becomes 0.01701 against the correct 0.01019 — 67% too large, and this time conservative rather than dangerous.

    The reason is intuitive. Heavy users produce both more sessions and more clicks, so the numerator and denominator move together. Part of their variability cancels in the ratio, and the −2R Cov(X, Y) term is what accounts for that cancellation. Treating numerator and denominator as independent throws away the fact that they are the same users.

    So the error runs in both directions depending on which shortcut you take: ignoring the clustering understates by 1.87×, ignoring the correlation overstates by 1.67×. Neither is safe, and the full expression is one line.

    Everything here scales as 1/√(users), not 1/√(sessions). Going from 2,000 to 8,000 users takes the true spread from 0.01041 to 0.00539 and the delta standard error from 0.01019 to 0.00513 — both essentially halved, as chapter 3's root-n law says they must when the independent unit is the user.

  1. The bootstrap works too, provided it resamples users — chapter 3. Slower, and it needs no formula, which makes it a good cross-check on a delta implementation.
  2. Clustered standard errors at the user level give the same answer by a different route, and are what a regression package will do if you tell it the cluster.
  3. The linearisation trick: define Ŷi = Xi − R·Yi per user, then run an ordinary t-test on Ŷ. It is algebraically the delta method and it drops straight into existing pipelines.
  4. All three agree, so pick whichever fits your stack. What you cannot do is nothing.

    5. A DIFFERENT PROBLEM ENTIRELY

    Which ratio did you mean?

    The standard error was an arithmetic question. This one is about what you are estimating, and no amount of statistics resolves it.

    "Clicks per session" has two defensible definitions, and they are not the same number.

    Ratio of sums

    total clicks / total sessions

    Every session counts equally, so users are weighted by how active they are. A user with forty sessions counts twenty times as much as one with two.

    Mean of per-user ratios

    average over users of (their clicks / their sessions)

    Every user counts equally, whatever their activity. A user with two sessions counts as much as one with forty.

    The last row is the one that ends meetings

    A change that slightly hurts light users and helps heavy ones moves the ratio of sums up 0.0145 and the mean of per-user ratios down 0.0150. Same experiment, same data, opposite conclusions.

    Both numbers are correct. Light users are 90% of users but only 31% of sessions, so weighting by session hands the answer to the heavy minority and weighting by user hands it to the light majority.

    They disagree even before any treatment: the baseline is 0.3034 by ratio of sums and 0.2150 by mean of per-user ratios. That gap is not an error, it is the two weightings describing different populations.

    DECIDING

    Which one the business means

    This is a product question wearing statistical clothing, and it should be settled in the experiment plan.

    Ratio of sums, when the total is what you receive
    Revenue, ad impressions served, infrastructure cost. If the business banks the sum, the sum is the right object and heavy users genuinely do matter more. This is also usually the metric that ties to a financial forecast.
    Mean of per-user ratios, when you care about typical experience
    "Did this make the product better for a user?" is a question about users, and letting the heaviest 10% dominate answers a different one. Fairness and satisfaction questions almost always want this weighting.
    Report both when they disagree
    Divergence is a finding, not a problem to resolve by picking the favourable one. It says the effect is heterogeneous across activity levels, which is worth knowing on its own — and chapter 14 is about reading that properly.
    Whichever you choose, choose before running
    Otherwise the last row of that table becomes a decision made after seeing which direction each metric moved, which is chapter 8's multiplicity in its most persuasive form.
    The denominator can be affected by the treatment

    One trap the demo holds fixed. If the feature changes how many sessions people have, the denominator is itself an outcome — and then "clicks per session" can fall while total clicks rise, simply because sessions rose faster.

    That is not a paradox and it is not necessarily bad, but it means the ratio no longer isolates what you think. The diagnostic is cheap: always report the numerator and denominator separately alongside the ratio. If the denominator moved, the ratio needs a different reading — and conditioning on a post-treatment quantity is exactly the error chapter 10 flagged in triggered analysis.

    IN PRACTICE

    A working checklist

    Five things, in order.

  5. Identify the independent unit — whatever you randomised. Everything downstream aggregates to it.
  6. Aggregate to one row per unit before computing anything. Numerator sum, denominator sum, two columns.
  7. Apply the delta method, or bootstrap the units, or cluster the standard errors. All three agree; pick one and put it in the pipeline rather than in a notebook.
  8. State which ratio you mean in the plan, and report the numerator and denominator separately.
  9. Validate with A/A tests — chapter 10. If your ratio metric's false-positive rate is 15% rather than 5%, this chapter is why, and the A/A programme is what tells you.
    10. Why this chapter is worth the trouble

    A standard error understated by 1.87× is not a subtle problem. It turns a 15% chance of being wrong into a reported 1%, and it does so on the metrics teams care most about — engagement rates, conversion per visit, revenue per order.

    It is also entirely fixable with one line of arithmetic on an aggregated table, which makes it one of the highest-return corrections in experimentation. The next chapter is the other one: CUPED and stratification can cut the standard error itself, which is worth several times the traffic.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Ask whether the denominator is set by the randomisation or by the user. If the user produces it, it is random.
    2. Per-user metrics are fine. Per-session, per-order and per-impression metrics are not, by default.
    3. The naive SE was 0.00557 when the estimate varied by 0.01041 — understated 1.87×.
    4. A nominal 95% interval covered 89.4%, 81.8% and 68.8% as session variation rose.
    5. The delta method covered 94.6%, 93.8% and 94.6% across the same settings.
    6. The failure worsens exactly as the metric becomes more useful, because both depend on users visiting at different rates.
    7. Var(R) ≈ [Var(X) − 2R·Cov(X,Y) + R²·Var(Y)] / (n·Ȳ²) on per-user sums.
    8. The covariance term is not optional: dropping it gave 0.01701 against 0.01019, 67% too large.
    9. So both shortcuts fail, in opposite directions — ignoring clustering understates, ignoring correlation overstates.
    10. Everything scales as 1/√(users), not 1/√(sessions). 2,000 to 8,000 users halved both.
    11. Bootstrap-by-user, clustered SEs and the linearisation trick all agree with the delta method.
    12. Ratio of sums weights by activity; mean of per-user ratios weights users equally.
    13. They disagreed at baseline before any treatment: 0.3034 against 0.2150.
    14. And they moved in opposite directions — +0.0145 against −0.0150 — when a change hurt light users and helped heavy ones.
    15. Light users were 90% of users and 31% of sessions. That gap is the whole disagreement.
    16. Choose the ratio before running, and report numerator and denominator separately.
