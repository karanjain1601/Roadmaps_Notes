CHAPTER 10

Randomisation, balance and A/A tests

A correctly randomised experiment fails at least one balance check 64% of the time, and the interval still covers the truth 95% of the time — chance imbalance is not a defect the standard error missed, it is what the standard error is. The check that does matter is the arm counts, and even there a small leak can make a spurious result significant 92% of the time while the mismatch is caught only 13%.

    WHAT IT ACTUALLY PROMISES

    What randomisation guarantees, and what it does not

    A precise statement, because the imprecise version is what causes the confusion in the next section.

    What it guarantees

    That the two arms are drawn from the same distribution of users. In expectation, every characteristic — measured, unmeasured, unimagined — is balanced.

    And that the difference between arms has a known sampling distribution, which is what makes the standard error meaningful.

    What it does not guarantee

    That any particular experiment has balanced arms. Randomisation is a statement about the procedure, exactly as a confidence interval is.

    Your specific split will differ on plenty of characteristics, and that is not a malfunction — it is the sampling variation the arithmetic already accounts for.

    Which reframes what a balance check is for

    It is not there to verify that the arms are similar — they will not be, on some dimension, every time. It is there to detect a broken assignment mechanism: a bug, a leak, a filter applied to one arm and not the other.

    Those are two very different jobs, and conflating them produces the behaviour the next demo measures: teams discarding perfectly good experiments because a covariate came out imbalanced.

    This is chapter 8's multiplicity arithmetic wearing a different hat. Twenty covariates at 0.05 means a flawless randomisation looks broken 63.9% of the time.

    And the imbalance does not break anything

    The demo tracks a covariate that genuinely predicts the outcome, so chance imbalance on it induces real bias in any single experiment. At 2,000 per arm the mean imbalance is 0.0255 and the mean absolute bias 0.0153.

    Yet the interval covers the true effect 95.1% of the time. Chance imbalance is not a defect the standard error failed to account for. It is what the standard error accounts for — the variability of the difference between two random halves is precisely the thing being estimated.

    And it shrinks as 1/√n: 0.0522 at 500 per arm, 0.0255 at 2,000, 0.0117 at 10,000. Randomisation works at scale because of that rate, not because anyone inspected the result.

    Discarding "imbalanced" runs is worse than useless

    Coverage among the runs that passed every balance check is 95.2%, against 95.1% overall. Filtering achieved nothing except discarding data.

    Worse, re-randomising until the arms look balanced changes the sampling distribution, so the standard error no longer describes the procedure you actually ran — and the resulting intervals are wrong in a direction nobody computes. If you want balance by construction, stratify or block before assigning, which is a legitimate design and is the subject of chapter 12. Do not achieve it by rejection.

    The one legitimate use of a balance check: a covariate imbalanced by far more than chance allows — say six standard errors — is not bad luck. That is a bug, and the next section is about the version of it that actually happens.

    THE CHECK THAT MATTERS

    Sample ratio mismatch

    Not whether the arms look similar, but whether they are the size they should be. It is the highest-value diagnostic in experimentation and it costs one line.

    You asked for 50/50 and got 49.3/50.7. That sounds like rounding. With large arms it is not — and it means some users were filtered out of one arm, which means the arms are no longer comparable at all.

    The row to worry about

    A small leak at 200,000 users manufactures a significant effect 92% of the time, and the strict SRM check catches the mismatch 13% of the time. That is a confident, publishable, entirely spurious result with a clean-looking diagnostic beside it.

    The arm share is 49.76% — a quarter of a percentage point off. Nobody reading a dashboard notices that, and at p < 0.001 the test does not either.

    The obvious hope is that a bigger experiment rescues you. It does not, because both signals grow at the same rate.

    The spurious result outruns the diagnostic

    Both the SRM statistic and the fake effect's significance scale as √n, so growing the experiment strengthens them together. But look at the order in which they arrive: the spurious effect is significant 92% of the time at 200,000, where SRM is still catching only 13%. There is a real band in which you get a confident wrong answer and a clean diagnostic.

    Which makes the threshold choice matter more than it looks. On the same 200,000-user leak:

    • p < 0.001 catches 13%, with 0.0% false alarms on a clean split
    • p < 0.01 catches 36%, with 0.4% false alarms
    • p < 0.05 catches 57%, with 4.0% false alarms

    Tightening the SRM threshold is exactly the wrong instinct. It saves a handful of investigations and blinds you in the band where the spurious result is most confident. Use a loose threshold and accept the false alarms — an hour spent confirming a split was fine costs vastly less than shipping a leak.

  1. Bot filtering applied after assignment — if the filter behaves differently by arm, it creates exactly this.
  2. Redirects and load failures — the treatment page is slower, so more users abandon before logging. The abandoners are systematically different.
  3. Client-side assignment with a slow SDK — users who leave quickly never get bucketed, and they are not a random sample.
  4. A join that drops rows — an inner join to a table that only has records for one arm's users.
  5. Triggered analysis done wrong — filtering to users who saw the feature means filtering the treatment arm on a post-treatment variable, which is the same failure wearing a respectable name.

    6. TESTING THE TEST

    What an A/A test actually measures

    Split the traffic, change nothing, and see what the pipeline reports. Valuable, and routinely misread.

    A single A/A test that comes back significant is not evidence of a problem: it should do so 5% of the time by construction. The value is in running many and checking the distribution.

    The p-values should be uniform
    Across many A/A tests, p-values should be spread evenly between 0 and 1. Too many small ones means the standard errors are too small — usually chapter 9's clustering problem, which understated the SE 2.6×. Too few means they are too large, and you are losing power.
    The false-positive rate should be 5%
    Not 0%, not 20%. This is the single number an A/A programme exists to produce, and it validates the entire analysis pipeline at once: assignment, logging, joins, metric definitions and standard errors.
    The arm sizes should match
    The SRM check above, run without a treatment to blame it on. This is the fastest way to find a bucketing bug.
    It cannot detect interference
    There is no treatment, so nothing leaks between arms. An A/A test will look perfect in exactly the marketplace where chapter 9's A/B test overstated the effect nineteenfold.
    Run them continuously, not once

    A one-off A/A test before launching an experimentation programme is a reasonable smoke test and expires immediately. The version that pays is a permanent A/A pair alongside real experiments, monitored for its false-positive rate.

    That catches regressions in the pipeline — a changed metric definition, a new bot filter, a logging migration — which is where these problems actually come from. Nobody ships a broken randomiser; they ship a change to something adjacent to it.

    MECHANICS

    How assignment should work

    Boring, and the source of most of the failures above.

    assign.py
    import hashlib

def variant(user_id: str, experiment: str, n_arms: int = 2) -> int:
    """Deterministic, independent across experiments, stable over time."""
    key = f"{experiment}:{user_id}".encode()          # SALT with the experiment id
    digest = hashlib.sha256(key).digest()
    return int.from_bytes(digest[:8], "big") % n_arms

# Deterministic:  same user, same experiment -> same arm, forever.
#                 No assignment table to keep in sync, no state to lose.
# Salted:         a user unlucky in experiment A is not unlucky in B.
#                 Without the salt, the same users share an arm every time.
# Well-mixed:     sha256 spreads sequential ids evenly. A cheap hash does not,
#                 and user ids are very often sequential.
  6. Salt with the experiment id. Without it, arms correlate across experiments and one unlucky cohort skews everything simultaneously.
  7. Use a real hash. Modulo on a sequential id, or a weak hash with poor low bits, produces arms that differ by signup date.
  8. Assign before any filtering. Every SRM cause above is a filter applied after the split. Bucket first, filter both arms identically, and record the filtering.
  9. Log the assignment, not just the exposure. You need to know who was assigned, including users who never saw the feature, or you cannot compute the intention-to-treat estimate — and you cannot detect SRM at all.
  10. Hold assignment stable across sessions and devices where you can. A user who flips arms is chapter 9's contamination, which measured 19.1% of a real effect.

    11. WHEN IT IS BROKEN

    What to do about an SRM

    Short section, because there is essentially one answer.

    Do not analyse the experiment

    An SRM means users were removed from one arm non-randomly. The two arms are therefore no longer comparable on anything, measured or not, and no adjustment fixes it because you cannot condition on a variable you never observed for the users who vanished.

    The demo shows why this is not excessive caution: the leaked experiment produced a mean "effect" of 0.0155, significant 94% of the time, from a true effect of exactly zero. It is not a small distortion of a real result. It is a fabricated result.

    Find the stage where the counts diverge
    Compare arm sizes at assignment, at exposure, at the first metric join, and after filtering. The step where the ratio breaks is the bug, and this usually takes ten minutes.
    Check whether it is one segment
    An SRM confined to a single platform, browser or country points at something concrete — a redirect, an SDK version, a regional CDN — rather than a general problem.
    Fix and re-run
    Not "fix and re-analyse the existing data". The users lost from one arm are gone and cannot be recovered.
    Then add it to the automated checks
    SRM should block a result from being shown, not appear in a footnote. It is the one diagnostic worth being aggressive about, and chapter 14's Twyman's law applies: a surprisingly large effect should send you here first.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Randomisation guarantees the arms come from the same distribution, not that any particular split is balanced.
    2. A balance check is for detecting a broken mechanism, not for verifying that the arms look similar.
    3. A correct randomisation flags at least one of 20 covariates 63.9% of the time, matching the 64.2% predicted by multiplicity.
    4. The interval still covers 95.1%. Chance imbalance is what the standard error accounts for, not a defect it missed.
    5. Imbalance shrinks as 1/√n — 0.0522, 0.0255, 0.0117 at 500, 2,000 and 10,000 per arm.
    6. Coverage among runs that passed every check is 95.2% against 95.1% overall. Filtering achieved nothing.
    7. Re-randomising until balanced invalidates the standard error. Stratify by design instead.
    8. Sample ratio mismatch is the check that matters: not whether the arms look alike, but whether they are the right size.
    9. A small leak at 200,000 users manufactures a significant effect 92% of the time from a true effect of exactly zero.
    10. And a strict SRM check catches it 13% of the time. The arm share is 49.76%.
    11. Growing the experiment does not rescue you. The spurious effect hits 92% at 200,000, where SRM catches 13%.
    12. Loosen the threshold: p < 0.05 catches 57% against p < 0.001's 13%, for 4.0% false alarms.
    13. Every common SRM cause is a filter applied after assignment. Bucket first, filter both arms identically.
    14. A single significant A/A test is expected; the value is in the distribution of p-values across many.
    15. A/A tests cannot detect interference, because there is no treatment to leak.
    16. An SRM means do not analyse the experiment. No adjustment fixes a non-random deletion.
