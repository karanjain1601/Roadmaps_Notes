CHAPTER 07

Power and the minimum detectable effect

With five thousand users an arm on a 3% conversion rate, the smallest lift you can detect is 32% relative. Almost no real feature moves conversion that much, so the test is guaranteed to be inconclusive before it starts — and that is knowable in thirty seconds. Worse, an underpowered test that does reach significance overstates the effect by 3.65× and points the wrong way 5% of the time.

    THE QUESTION

    What power answers

    Four quantities, locked together. Fix any three and the fourth is determined.

    The four corners of a power calculation
    QuantityMeaningUsually
    αFalse-positive rate you will tolerateFixed at 0.05 by convention
    PowerChance of detecting a real effect of a given sizeSet at 0.80, which is lower than people realise
    nObservations per armConstrained by traffic and patience
    MDESmallest effect the study can reliably detectThe output, and the one to look at
    Solve for the MDE, not for n

    The textbook framing asks "how many users do I need?" — which assumes you can get them. In a real product you usually cannot: traffic is what it is, and the test can run for two weeks.

    So invert it. Take the traffic you actually have, the duration you can actually tolerate, and compute the smallest effect this study could detect. Then ask whether an effect that large is plausible. If it is not, you have learned in half a minute that the experiment cannot answer its question, and you have learned it before spending two weeks.

    This is chapter 1's value-of-information calculation in operational form: an underpowered study was worth £20,000 against a £30,000 cost, which is worse than not running it.

    THE CALCULATION

    Working out what you can detect

    One formula, and the consequence that governs every experimentation programme.

    mde.py
    from scipy.stats import norm
import numpy as np

def mde(n, sd, alpha=0.05, power=0.80):
    """Smallest detectable difference, two equal arms."""
    z_a = norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)      # 1.96 at alpha = 0.05
    z_b = norm.ppf(power)              # 0.84 at power = 0.80
    return (z_a + z_b) * sd * np.sqrt(2 / n)

def mde_rate(n, baseline, **kw):
    """For a conversion rate, sd comes from the rate itself."""
    return mde(n, np.sqrt(baseline * (1 - baseline)), **kw)

# the number that decides whether to run it
abs_mde = mde_rate(5_000, 0.03)
print(f"{abs_mde:.4f} absolute, {abs_mde / 0.03:.1%} relative")

    The quadratic wall

    The MDE falls as 1/√n, so halving it takes exactly four times the traffic — verified to floating-point precision in the demo. Going from a 32% detectable lift to a 4% one is not four times the users, it is sixty-four times.

    This single relationship explains most of how experimentation programmes actually behave: why large companies can test small changes and small ones cannot, why "let it run another week" rarely rescues an underpowered test, and why chapter 12's variance reduction is valuable out of all proportion to how technical it sounds. Cutting σ by 30% is worth doubling your traffic.

    THE FEASIBILITY TEST

    Reading the MDE against reality

    One comparison, and it should happen before anything is built.

    You have a number: the smallest lift this test could detect. Now put a second number beside it: the largest lift you actually believe is possible. Three outcomes.

    MDE well below the plausible effect

    Run it. The study can distinguish the outcomes you care about, and both a positive and a negative result will be informative.

    MDE above any plausible effect

    Do not run it. You already know the result: not significant, with an interval so wide it excludes nothing. You will have spent two weeks to learn what you knew at the start.

    The uncomfortable middle

    MDE roughly equal to the effect you expect is the common case, and it is worse than it looks. Chapter 6 measured exactly this: when the true effect sits on the threshold you care about, the interval is called "clearly worth it" 2.5% of the time at every sample size.

    So a borderline-powered test does not give you a borderline answer. It gives you "real, but we cannot tell whether it is worth anything" — 97% of the time in that simulation, at any n.

    The honest responses are to find more traffic, cut variance, pick a more sensitive metric, accept a lower power target and say so, or decide without the experiment. Running it anyway and hoping is the option that wastes a fortnight.

  1. Write the MDE into the experiment plan, next to the effect you expect. That one line prevents most wasted tests.
  2. Compute it for the metric you will actually decide on, not the one that is easiest. A revenue metric usually has far worse power than a click metric, which is why teams drift towards clicks.
  3. Account for clustering. Chapter 3's design effect divides your effective n, and at twenty rows per user it divided 10,000 by 8.6.
  4. Account for multiple metrics. Five metrics at α = 0.05 is not α = 0.05 — chapter 8.

    5. THE CONSEQUENCE NOBODY PLANS FOR

    The winner's curse

    Low power does not just mean missing real effects. It corrupts the ones you find.

    Everyone knows an underpowered study often misses a real effect. The part that is rarely stated: when it does find one, the effect it reports is systematically too large — and it is the significance filter that causes this, not any flaw in the estimator.

    Read the third column before the fourth

    The mean of all estimates is 0.196 to 0.200 in every row. The estimator is perfectly unbiased at every sample size. Nothing is wrong with the arithmetic.

    The damage is done by conditioning on significance. At 11% power, an estimate only clears the bar when it happens to be a large fluke — so the studies that get reported are exactly the ones that overstate. The average published effect is 0.730 against a truth of 0.200.

    And it does not stop at magnitude. At 11% power, 5.3% of significant results point the wrong way entirely: a real positive effect, reported as a statistically significant negative one. At 80% power that falls to zero.

    Three practical consequences

    Effects shrink on replication, and this is expected. A team ships a feature measured at +8% in a small test and sees +2% in production. That is not a measurement error or a novelty effect — it is the winner's curse, and it was predictable from the power.

    Do not build the business case on the point estimate of an underpowered test. Use the lower end of the interval, which is far closer to the truth in exactly these situations.

    A surprisingly large effect from a small study is evidence of a small study, not of a large effect. The reflex should be to check the power before getting excited.

    A COMMON REQUEST TO REFUSE

    Post-hoc power is not a thing

    Asked for after every null result, and it cannot answer the question being asked.

    "The test was not significant — what power did we have?" Computing power from the observed effect is circular: observed power is a one-to-one function of the p-value, so it contains no information the p-value did not already carry. A non-significant result always yields low observed power, by construction.

    What people want to know

    "Could this study have detected an effect worth caring about?"

    What answers it

    The confidence interval. If it excludes everything that mattered, the study was informative. If it includes both a meaningful harm and a meaningful gain, it was not.

    Or the MDE, computed from the design rather than the result — which is the same number you should have had before starting.

    The one-line answer

    "Observed power adds nothing to the p-value. But the interval was [−1.8%, +2.6%], and we needed +2% to justify shipping — so this study genuinely could not tell us. Here is what it would take to run it properly."

    That is the same conversation the person wanted, conducted with a quantity that means something.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. α, power, n and the MDE are locked together. Fix three, the fourth follows.
    2. Solve for the MDE, not for n. Traffic is a constraint, not a variable.
    3. At 5,000 users an arm on a 3% baseline, the MDE is a 31.9% relative lift. Almost no feature does that.
    4. At 500,000 an arm it falls to 3.2%, which is where ordinary wins become visible.
    5. The MDE falls as 1/√n, so halving it takes exactly four times the traffic — and going from 32% to 4% takes sixty-four times.
    6. Which is why variance reduction is worth so much: cutting σ by 30% is worth doubling traffic.
    7. Compare the MDE with the largest effect you find plausible. If the MDE is bigger, do not run the test.
    8. A borderline-powered test gives "real but we cannot tell if it matters", not a borderline answer — 97% of the time, at any n.
    9. Compute the MDE for the metric you will decide on, and account for clustering and multiple metrics.
    10. The estimator is unbiased at every sample size — 0.196 to 0.200 against a true 0.200.
    11. Conditioning on significance is what corrupts it. At 11% power the mean reported effect is 0.730, a 3.65× exaggeration.
    12. At 11% power, 5.3% of significant results have the wrong sign. At 80% power, zero.
    13. Effects shrinking on replication is expected, not a mystery or a novelty effect.
    14. Build the business case on the lower end of the interval, not the point estimate of an underpowered test.
    15. A surprisingly large effect from a small study is evidence of a small study.
    16. Post-hoc power is a function of the p-value and adds nothing. Report the interval instead.
