CHAPTER 08

Peeking, sequential testing and multiplicity

Checking a running experiment twenty times instead of once takes the false-positive rate from 5.0% to 25.1%. On identical data with the same total sample, it also raises the reported effect from 0.0648 to 0.1122 against a truth of 0.0500 — so peeking corrupts the estimate as well as the p-value. Both are fixable, and one of the two standard fixes costs a fifth of your power while the other costs almost none.

    THE DASHBOARD PROBLEM

    What checking a running experiment costs

    Every experimentation platform shows a live p-value. Every stakeholder refreshes it. The test was never designed for that.

    An experiment with no real effect, checked repeatedly 4,000 simulated experiments, 4,000 users per arm in total
    FALSE-POSITIVE RATE0%

    No real effect. A correct procedure fires 5% of the time
    Looks125102050
    Fires5.0%7.6%14.0%19.3%25.1%32.4%

    Nothing is wrong with the test. A single look at 1.96 gives exactly 5.0%. What breaks it is looking again.

    Why it climbs, and why it never comes back down

    The running difference between two arms is a random walk. Early on it is noisy and wanders widely; as data accumulates it settles. Plot it and it looks like a river meandering towards zero.

    A fixed significance line at ±1.96 is a bank on either side. Each look is a fresh opportunity for the walk to be outside it — and crucially, the decision is absorbing: once you have declared a winner you stop, ship, and never observe that the walk was about to come back. You accumulate chances to be wrong and no chances to be corrected.

    THE SECOND DAMAGE

    Peeking also inflates the effect

    Less discussed than the false-positive problem, and it survives even when the effect is real.

    Set the demo's true effect to 0.05 and hold the total sample fixed at 4,000 per arm. The data is identical; only the number of interim checks changes.

    Identical total sample and a real effect of 0.0500 throughout
    LooksFiresMean estimate when it firesExaggeration
    160.6%0.06481.30×
    569.8%0.08301.66×
    2076.4%0.10002.00×
    5079.3%0.11222.24×
    Same data, twice the reported effect

    Splitting the same 4,000 users into fifty checks instead of one raises the hit rate from 60.6% to 79.3% and raises the reported effect from 0.0648 to 0.1122 — more than double the truth.

    The mechanism is selection, exactly as in chapter 7's winner's curse. Stopping at the first crossing means stopping at the moment the noise was most favourable. More checks means more chances to catch a favourable moment, so the estimate you freeze is drawn from further out in the tail.

    Which explains a familiar pattern: a test stopped early at +8% ships and delivers +3%. Nobody cheated, and the number was never real.

    DOING IT PROPERLY

    You can look often. You cannot look often at 1.96

    Two standard boundary families, and the difference between them is worth a quarter of your power.

    The false-positive rate is restored by raising the bar. The demo finds, by simulation, the constant boundary that holds the overall rate at 5% — and it reproduces the published group-sequential values.

    Those are the Pocock boundaries, arrived at independently. But raising the bar everywhere is expensive, and the alternative is to raise it unevenly.

    Why O'Brien–Fleming is the practical default

    Its boundary is a function of how far through you are: in this simulation it runs from |z| > 9.46 at the first look down to 2.12 at the last. Early on it is effectively unreachable, so almost no α is spent; by the end it is barely stricter than 1.96.

    The result is that it keeps 99% of the single-look power against Pocock's 81%, while still allowing you to stop early — you just need an enormous effect to justify stopping in week one, which is exactly the right incentive.

    Pocock's constant bar is easier to explain and better when you genuinely want an even chance of stopping at any point. In product experimentation you rarely do.

    The two corrected rows sit near 5% rather than exactly on it — both boundaries are found by simulation, so they carry a little noise. The fourth row is not a third option at all: its extra "power" is bought entirely with the 25% false-positive rate.

  1. α-spending functions generalise both: you choose how much of your 5% to spend at each look, and the boundary follows. This is what a serious platform implements.
  2. Always-valid inference — mixture sequential probability ratio tests, confidence sequences — lets you look continuously with an interval that is valid at every moment. The cost is a wider interval; the benefit is that peeking becomes harmless by construction.
  3. Bayesian monitoring is not automatically immune. A posterior is valid at any stopping time, but "ship when P(effect > 0) > 0.95" is still a stopping rule with operating characteristics you should simulate rather than assume.
  4. The cheapest fix of all: fix the sample size and do not look. Unglamorous, free, and it is what chapter 7's calculation is for.

    5. THE OTHER MULTIPLICITY

    Many metrics, many variants, many segments

    The same arithmetic as peeking, spread across space rather than time.

    Looking twenty times is twenty chances to be wrong. So is testing twenty metrics, or four variants, or slicing one result by five segments.

    TWO DIFFERENT GUARANTEES

    Bonferroni against Benjamini–Hochberg

    They control different things, and choosing between them is a question about what a false winner costs you.

    Twenty metrics, five of them genuinely real, n = 400
    MethodPower on the real onesAny false winnerFalse discovery rate
    No correction94.3%53.5%13.7%
    Bonferroni69.9%4.2%1.2%
    Benjamini–Hochberg84.0%19.1%4.9%

    Bonferroni controls the family-wise rate

    Test each at α/m. The guarantee: at most a 5% chance of any false winner — measured at 4.2%.

    The cost is severe: power falls from 94.3% to 69.9%, so it discards a quarter of the real effects to avoid one false one.

    BH controls the false discovery rate

    Sort the p-values, compare the i-th against 0.05 × i/m, reject up to the largest that passes. The guarantee: of the winners you declare, at most 5% are false — measured at 4.9%.

    Power recovers to 84.0%, so it buys back 14 of Bonferroni's 24 lost points.

    Which guarantee do you actually want?

    Ask what a false winner costs. If shipping one wrong thing is a disaster — a safety claim, a regulatory filing, a single go/no-go — you want the family-wise guarantee, and Bonferroni's lost power is the price.

    If you are screening — twenty metrics on a dashboard, a hundred segments, a scan for anything worth following up — then "of the things I flag, most should be real" is the guarantee that matters, and Bonferroni is needlessly punitive. This is the usual case in product analytics, and BH is the usual right answer.

    Note that BH does permit more family-wise errors: 19.1% against Bonferroni's 4.2%. That is not a failure, it is the trade being made explicit.

    IN PRACTICE

    What to actually do

    Most of the fix is structural rather than statistical.

    Name one primary metric before the test runs
    One metric, one decision, no correction needed. Everything else is a secondary metric, reported with its interval and explicitly labelled as not decision-grade. This single convention removes most multiplicity problems, and it costs nothing.
    Add guardrail metrics, but decide their role in advance
    Latency, error rate, unsubscribes. These are not there to be won, they are there to catch harm — so a one-sided check at a looser threshold is appropriate, and no correction is needed because you are not hunting for wins.
    Segment analysis is exploratory unless pre-registered
    "It worked for mobile users in Germany" from a null overall result is chapter 14's territory, and the honest framing is a hypothesis for the next test rather than a finding from this one.
    Fix the duration, and let the dashboard show an interval
    If people will look — and they will — show a confidence sequence or a group-sequential boundary rather than a naive p-value. A platform that displays a raw running p-value is inviting the 25% error rate.
    The subtlest version: the experiment that keeps getting extended

    "It is nearly significant, let us run another week." That is peeking with an extra step, and it is worse than the dashboard version because it feels like diligence rather than a decision rule.

    It also has a distinctive signature: extensions only ever happen when the result is close, so the additional data is being collected conditional on the current estimate. If you must extend, decide the new sample size and the new boundary before looking again, and record that you did.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Checking twenty times takes the false-positive rate from 5.0% to 25.1%, and fifty times to 32.4%.
    2. The test is fine; looking again is what breaks it. A single look gives exactly 5.0%.
    3. The running difference is a random walk and the decision is absorbing — you accumulate chances to be wrong and none to be corrected.
    4. Peeking also inflates the estimate. Same data, same total sample: 0.0648 at one look, 0.1122 at fifty, against a truth of 0.0500.
    5. That is selection, not bias. Stopping at the first crossing means stopping when the noise was most favourable.
    6. Which explains tests that ship at +8% and deliver +3%. Nobody cheated.
    7. Simulating the boundary that holds 5% reproduces the published Pocock values — 1.973, 2.202, 2.408, 2.555 against 1.960, 2.178, 2.413, 2.555.
    8. A constant raised bar costs 19% of the power: 49.2% against 60.6% for a single final look.
    9. O'Brien–Fleming keeps 99%, running from |z| > 9.46 at the first look to 2.12 at the last.
    10. Twenty looks at 1.96 is not a third option. Its extra power is the 25% false-positive rate.
    11. Twenty null metrics give at least one winner 65.5% of the time, averaging one false winner per experiment.
    12. Bonferroni controls the chance of any false winner (4.2%) and costs 24 points of power.
    13. BH controls the proportion of winners that are false (4.9%) and recovers 14 of those points.
    14. Choose by what a false winner costs. Go/no-go decisions want family-wise; screening wants FDR.
    15. One named primary metric removes most multiplicity problems and costs nothing.
    16. "Let us run another week" is peeking that feels like diligence.
