CHAPTER 01

What a data scientist actually does

A feature with a true effect of two percentage points shows up as twenty-five in the obvious comparison. Adjusting for the confounder helps, and only if you have measured it well and binned it finely — get either wrong and most of the bias survives. Randomisation needs neither, which is why so much of this unit is about experiments.

    THE ROLE

    Three adjacent jobs

    The titles overlap and the work does not. What distinguishes them is which question they are accountable for.

    What each role is answerable for
    The questionThe outputJudged on
    Data analystWhat happened?A number, a dashboard, a reportWhether it is correct and timely
    Data scientistWhat would happen if we changed something?An estimate, with uncertainty and a recommendationWhether the decision it supported was right
    ML engineerHow do we serve this reliably at scale?A running systemLatency, uptime, cost, and the model staying fresh
    The distinguishing word is would

    "Customers who use the mobile app churn less" is a description, and an analyst can produce it correctly. "Getting customers onto the app would reduce churn" is a causal claim, and everything difficult about this role lives in the gap between those two sentences.

    That gap is why the roadmap for this role is dominated by experimentation and inference rather than by modelling. A model that predicts churn well tells you nothing about what to do — it will happily learn that people who cancelled their subscription last week are likely to churn.

    In practice most people do parts of all three, and the useful thing is not the title but knowing which question you are currently answering. Producing a descriptive number and presenting it as a causal one is the single most common failure in the job, and it usually happens without anyone noticing the switch.

    WHY THE OBVIOUS COMPARISON FAILS

    Adopters against non-adopters

    You have the data already. You can compare the people who used the feature with those who did not. The demo shows what that measures.

    Suppose a feature genuinely raises conversion by two percentage points. Engaged users are both more likely to try it and more likely to convert anyway. Compare adopters with non-adopters and you measure the engagement, not the feature.

    Three results, and the middle one is the reason this chapter exists.

    1. The naive comparison reports 25.59 points against a truth of 2.00 — nearly thirteen times the real effect. With the confounding switched off, it agrees with the randomised estimate, which confirms the problem is the confounding rather than the arithmetic.
    2. Randomisation recovers 1.96 points without needing to know that engagement exists. That is the whole appeal: it removes every confounder simultaneously, including the ones you have not thought of and cannot measure.
    3. Adjustment needs two things to go right and usually gets one. Even with engagement measured perfectly, eight strata still report 3.48 points, because engagement varies within each bucket. And at heavy measurement noise, eighty strata still report 23.89 — barely better than not adjusting at all.

    Read that table by column as well as by row

    Down the first column, finer strata help a great deal — 3.48 to 2.12 — which is the residual confounding inside each bucket disappearing. Across the bottom row, they barely help at all: no amount of stratification rescues a confounder you have measured badly, because the buckets themselves are then wrong.

    So "we controlled for it" is not one claim but two: that you measured the confounder well, and that you conditioned on it finely enough. Both fail quietly, and neither is visible in the output. Randomisation requires neither, which is the entire argument for running an experiment when you can.

    One more thing the demo cannot show, and it matters more than anything it can: this simulation has exactly one confounder, and you know what it is. Real observational comparisons have an unknown number of them, and the ones you have not thought of contribute bias you cannot bound. Chapter 13 is about the designs that make progress anyway, and none of them is as good as randomising.

    EVIDENCE

    What each design can support

    Not a ranking of rigour for its own sake. A statement of which claims you are entitled to make.

    Designs, and the strongest honest claim each supports
    DesignSupportsMain threat
    A cross-sectional comparison"These groups differ"Everything above
    A before-and-after look"It changed when we changed it"Anything else that changed at the same time
    Adjusted observational"It differs, holding the things we measured constant"Unmeasured confounders; the demo's two failure modes
    Difference-in-differences"It changed more than a comparable group"The two groups' trends diverging anyway
    Regression discontinuity"It causes this, near the threshold"Only local; people gaming the threshold
    Randomised experiment"It causes this, in this population"Interference, novelty, wrong metric — chapter 14
    The useful discipline is not always climbing the ladder

    Randomising is often impossible: you cannot randomise a pricing change across a market, a regulatory deadline, or a brand campaign. The discipline is saying which rung you are on, and what would have to be true for the claim to hold.

    "Adjusted for tenure and plan; unmeasured differences in customer intent would bias this upward" is an honest sentence that a decision can be built on. "Customers who use the app churn 3 points less" presented as a reason to promote the app is the same analysis with the caveat removed, and it is how organisations spend money on nothing.

    BEFORE ANY OF IT

    Is the answer worth getting?

    An experiment costs traffic, time and delay. Its value is not the quality of the evidence but the difference it makes to the decision.

    Consider shipping a feature: worth £400,000 if it works, costing £200,000 if it does not. You believe there is some chance it works. What is a test worth?

    WORTH RUNNING?—

    Three findings, and the first is the one to apply before designing anything.

    1. If the result would not change what you do, the test is worth exactly zero. With a small enough downside you ship whatever it says, and the computed value comes out at £0 — not approximately, exactly.
    2. Information is worth most near indifference. £99,000 at a 33% prior, falling to nothing by 80%. Verified across the whole prior range, the value is never negative — information cannot hurt a decision — but it is frequently zero.
    3. An underpowered test is not a cheap version of a good one. At 20% power the information is worth £20,000 against £119,000 at 95%, and net of the £30,000 cost it is negative. Running it is worse than not running it.
    The two-minute version

    Before designing an experiment, ask: which result would lead to a different action? If both answers lead to the same next step, stop — you are about to spend weeks confirming something. And if the honest answer is "we would ship it anyway, but the number would be useful for the roadmap discussion", that is a real reason and it is worth much less than a decision, so it should buy a much cheaper study.

    Chapter 7 turns this into the calculation that decides feasibility, and the two questions run together: what is the smallest effect worth acting on, and can this experiment detect it?

    SCOPING

    Turning a request into a question

    Requests arrive underspecified. Four questions convert one into something that can actually be answered.

    What decision does this feed?
    And who makes it, and when. An analysis with no deadline and no decision-maker is a hobby. This also tells you the required precision: a go/no-go needs far less than a price change.
    What is the population, exactly?
    Which users, over what period, with which exclusions. "Customers" is not a population. Getting this wrong is the most common reason two people produce different numbers for the same question, and it is settled in one sentence.
    What is the outcome, in one metric?
    Named before the analysis runs. Chapter 14 shows what happens when it is chosen afterwards, and chapter 11 shows that even naming it leaves a decision about the denominator that changes the standard error.
    What would change your mind?
    Ask the requester. If no result would alter their view, you have found that out cheaply. If a specific result would, you now know what the analysis has to be able to detect.
    Write the conclusion first

    Draft the sentence you expect to be able to write: "Feature X raised conversion by about N%, from A to B, worth roughly £C a year." Then check whether the study you are planning could actually produce it, at the precision the decision needs.

    Half the time it cannot, and finding that out in ten minutes is the highest-return activity in the job. The other half, the draft becomes the analysis plan.

    THE WORK

    What the job is actually made of

    A rough division of the time, which surprises people arriving from a course.

    Where the time goes

    • Scoping and definitions — what are we measuring, on whom
    • Getting and checking data — and finding it does not mean what the column name says
    • Design — the randomisation unit, the metric, the duration
    • Analysis — frequently the shortest step
    • Communicating and defending it

    What determines whether it lands

    • Whether the question was the real one
    • Whether the metric matched the decision
    • Whether the uncertainty was stated in a form people could use
    • Whether it arrived before the decision was made

    Note that none of those is about the method.

    The two failure modes to recognise in yourself

    The rigorous answer to the wrong question. A beautifully executed analysis of something nobody asked, delivered after the decision. Prevented by the scoping questions above, all of which take minutes.

    The confident answer to an unanswerable question. An observational comparison presented causally, or an underpowered test read as evidence of no effect. Prevented by the ladder in this chapter and the calculation in chapter 7.

    The first wastes your time. The second wastes the organisation's money, and it is much harder to notice, because the output looks the same either way.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. The distinguishing word is would. Description is an analyst's job; the causal claim is this one.
    2. A feature with a true effect of +2.00 points measured +25.59 in the naive comparison — nearly thirteen times the truth.
    3. With the confounding removed, the naive comparison agrees with the randomised one, which shows the problem is confounding rather than method.
    4. Randomisation recovered 1.96 points without needing to know engagement exists.
    5. "We controlled for it" is two claims: that the confounder was measured well, and that it was conditioned on finely enough.
    6. Perfectly measured but coarsely binned: 3.48 points. Finer strata took it to 2.12.
    7. Badly measured: 23.89 even at 80 strata. No amount of stratification rescues a confounder you cannot measure.
    8. The simulation has one known confounder; real data has an unknown number, contributing bias you cannot bound.
    9. Say which rung of the ladder you are on, and what would have to be true for the claim to hold.
    10. If the result would not change the action, the test is worth exactly £0.
    11. Information is worth most near indifference — £99,000 at a 33% prior, nothing by 80% — and never negative.
    12. An underpowered test can be worth less than its cost. At 20% power, £20,000 of information for £30,000.
    13. Four scoping questions: which decision, which population, which single metric, and what would change your mind.
    14. Write the conclusion first and check whether the study could produce it.
    15. The two failure modes are a rigorous answer to the wrong question, and a confident answer to an unanswerable one. The second is harder to see.
