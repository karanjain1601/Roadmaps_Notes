CHAPTER 18

Forecasting and backtesting

On a random walk, using yesterday's value is optimal and nothing beats it. On three other series it is the worst choice available. And the way most people split a time series for testing does not merely flatter the model — on one series here it reverses the verdict against the baseline, and on another it ranks the two candidate models the wrong way round.

    Four different methods win on four series. There is no universally good forecaster, and choosing a baseline is therefore a claim about what kind of series you have.

    The random-walk row is the one that matters most

    Nothing beats using the last value: 5.50 against the average's 14.40, drift's 5.77 and exponential smoothing's 5.77. This is not a limitation of the methods tried — on a random walk the last value is provably the optimal forecast, because every future change is unpredictable by construction.

    And chapter 17 established that many business series behave close to random walks. So the honest starting position for a lot of forecasting work is: the naive forecast may be the right answer, and a model that fails to beat it has told you something true rather than failing.

    The same forecaster is the worst available on the other three rows — 6.29 against 2.83 on the seasonal series, 3.07 against 2.31 on the mean-reverting one. Which baseline to use is not a formality.

    THE LIMIT

    Error grows with horizon, and you cannot model your way out

    This is a property of the series, not of the model

    A four-week-ahead forecast is inherently √28 ≈ 5.3× less certain than a one-day-ahead one, and no amount of modelling changes that. It follows from the variance of a sum of independent shocks.

    Two consequences worth stating out loud. A single accuracy number is meaningless without a horizon — "our forecast is 94% accurate" is not a claim until you know whether that is tomorrow or next quarter. And comparing two forecasts at different horizons is comparing nothing, which is a common way for a new model to look better than the one it replaces.

    It also means forecast intervals must widen with horizon. A constant-width band is wrong at every horizon but one.

    THE MODELS

    Exponential smoothing and ARIMA

    Two families that cover most univariate forecasting, and they overlap more than their notation suggests.

    Exponential smoothing

    A weighted average of the past with geometrically decaying weights. Simple exponential smoothing tracks a level; Holt adds a trend; Holt-Winters adds seasonality.

    Easy to explain, hard to beat, and the natural home for "recent data matters more".

    ARIMA(p, d, q)

    d differences to reach stationarity — chapter 17's fix. p autoregressive terms (the series depends on its own past). q moving-average terms (it depends on past errors).

    More flexible, more to get wrong, and ARIMA(0,1,0) is exactly the naive forecast.

    How to choose p, d and q
    d from a unit-root test — the smallest number of differences that makes the series stationary, remembering that over-differencing has its own cost. p and q by fitting a small grid and comparing on AIC, or by reading the ACF and PACF. Automatic selection is standard and reasonable.
    Seasonal ARIMA
    SARIMA adds a second set of terms at the seasonal lag. Necessary for the weekly and annual patterns in most product data, and the point where manual specification stops being worth the effort.
    When to reach past these
    When you have external predictors (price, marketing spend, weather), many related series to pool across, or known future events. That is where gradient boosting and the modern libraries earn their place — and where chapter 19's framing applies.
    What they all assume
    That the future resembles the past. Every method in this chapter fails at a regime change, and none of them will warn you.

    THE MISTAKE

    Shuffling flatters the model in every one of the six rows, and most where interpolation is easiest — 1.7× on the smooth cycle. Each held-out point has its own neighbours sitting in the training set, so the model interpolates between times it has already seen rather than forecasting.

    Three failures, in increasing order of seriousness

    1. The score is inflated. Everywhere, by 1.2× to 1.7×. Annoying, and the least of it.

    2. The verdict against the baseline reverses. On the slow cycle, the shuffled split says the model beats naive 1.320 to 1.620. Tested honestly it loses, 2.280 to 1.620. Same model, same data, opposite conclusion.

    3. The wrong model gets picked. On the mean-reverting series the shuffled split rates local linear as the better of the two, 2.022 against 2.038. Under rolling origin it is clearly the worse, 2.886 against 2.361 — and both lose to naive. The ranking flips outright.

    That third one is why this matters beyond honesty in reporting. A shuffled split does not just inflate your score, it destroys your ability to choose between candidates, which is the thing evaluation exists to do.

    And rolling origin is not merely pessimistic

    It is worth checking that the honest method still finds real skill, or it would just be a way of rejecting everything. On the slow cycle it separates the two models correctly: local linear at 1.602 genuinely beats the naive baseline's 1.620, while local average at 2.280 loses badly. The shuffled split rates them 1.164 and 1.320 — nearly identical, and gives you no basis for the decision.

    Rolling origin — also called walk-forward validation — trains on everything up to a point, forecasts forward, advances, repeats. It is more computation and it is the only scheme that answers the question you actually have.

  1. Forecast at the horizon you actually use. If decisions are made four weeks out, backtest at four weeks. One-step-ahead accuracy is a different and much easier problem.
  2. Keep a gap if your features have lag. If a feature is only available with a week's delay in production, the backtest must respect that delay or it is measuring a model you cannot deploy.
  3. Refit the model at each origin, not once at the start. Refitting once uses information from after the forecast date to choose parameters.
  4. Report the naive baseline alongside every result. It costs nothing, and it is the only number that makes an RMSE interpretable.

    5. SCORING

    Which error metric

    They disagree, and the disagreement is about what kind of mistake you can afford.

    The four in common use
    MetricPenalisesGood forFails when
    RMSELarge errors quadraticallyWhen a big miss is much worse than two small onesOutliers dominate the score
    MAEAll errors equallyRobust default; optimises toward the medianYou genuinely fear large misses
    MAPEPercentage errorComparing across series of different scaleValues near zero, and it punishes over-forecasts more than under
    MASEError relative to naiveComparing across series; 1.0 means "no better than naive"Less familiar to stakeholders
    MAPE's asymmetry is not a footnote

    Forecast 100 when the truth is 50 and MAPE records 100%. Forecast 50 when the truth is 100 and it records 50%. The same absolute error, scored twice as harshly in one direction — so optimising MAPE systematically biases forecasts downward.

    It is also undefined at zero and enormous near it, which matters for any count series with quiet periods. MASE avoids all of this and has the useful property that 1.0 means exactly as good as the naive forecast, which is the comparison this chapter keeps insisting on.

    JUDGEMENT

    Reading a forecast claim

    Five questions. The first two disqualify most claims on their own.

  5. Compared with what baseline? An RMSE with no baseline is a number with no meaning. If the answer is "we didn't compare", the model may well be worse than using yesterday's value.
  6. Tested how? Rolling origin, or a shuffled split. The table above is why this question comes second.
  7. At what horizon? Accuracy without a horizon is not a claim, and error grows as √h on a random walk.
  8. Where is the uncertainty? A point forecast with no interval is a guess presented as a fact, and the interval must widen with horizon.
  9. What would break it? Every method here assumes the future resembles the past. Ask what regime change would invalidate it and whether anyone would notice.
