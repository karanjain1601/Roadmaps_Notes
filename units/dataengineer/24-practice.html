    How to read a low score

    Nobody scores highly on all of these, and a platform does not need to. Several items are only worth their cost above a particular scale, which chapter 3 and chapter 1's maturity table both make explicit.

    The signal worth acting on is not the total but the shape. A platform strong on storage and moving data and weak on serving is building carefully for consumers it is not serving well. One strong everywhere except governance has an audit coming.

    WHEN A NUMBER LOOKS WRONG

    A debugging routine

    The order matters, because each step eliminates a class of cause and the cheap checks come first.

    1. Is it actually wrong?
    Reproduce it. A surprising number of reports are a filter difference, a timezone, or two people looking at different date ranges — and chapter 17 measured a 31% spread arising from nothing but different defensible definitions. Establish what the number should be and how you know, before touching a pipeline.
    2. Did it run, and when?
    Freshness first, because "the pipeline did not run" is the most common cause and the fastest to check. Then look at whether an upstream stage ran late enough to have been read while incomplete — chapter 16's staggered-cron failure, which produces a complete-looking wrong answer with no error anywhere.
    3. How far back does it go?
    This is the highest-value question and the one usually skipped. Recompute the same figure for the last thirty days. A single bad day is a run failure; a step change points at a deployment; a slow drift points at a source system changing behaviour or a lookback window that stopped covering the tail.
    4. Is the row count right?
    Count against the source, and count distinct at the declared grain. Volume catches torn writes and duplicate retries; a distinct count catches the fan-out from chapter 9, where a measure inflates while every individual row is correct.
    5. Walk upstream, not down.
    Take the lineage graph and check each parent for the same symptom. Chapter 21 measured this as 5 inspections against 11 without the graph — but the larger point is that a single root usually explains every alert you received, so stop treating them as separate problems.
    6. Compare against something independent.
    If structure checks pass and the values are still wrong, only an external comparison will find it. Chapter 20's matrix showed why: reconciliation and recompute-and-compare were the two highest-value checks precisely because everything else verifies structure rather than content.
    The shortcut worth remembering

    If the row count is right, the schema is right, the freshness is right and the values are wrong, you are looking at one of a small family: a CDC handover that left cold rows stale (chapter 5), a type-1 dimension that overwrote history (chapter 9), late data that was never reprocessed (chapter 12), or a stage that read stale input (chapter 16).

    All four share the property of being internally consistent. Recomputing an old period and comparing distinguishes between them faster than reading code.

    Adjacent roles

    MLOps picks up where chapter 18 stops. Data Analyst and BI Analyst are the consumers of chapter 17, and reading them makes the serving decisions land differently. Software Architect covers the reversibility reasoning of chapter 3 as a general discipline.

    The five results worth carrying

    If the unit compresses to anything, it is these.

    Loud failures are cheap; silent ones are expensive
    A dropped column costs an hour. A new enum value bucketed as "other" costs every decision made since. Almost every defensive technique in this unit is aimed at the second category, and almost every default protects only against the first.
    Structure checks verify structure; only comparison verifies content
    Right row count, right schema, right freshness, wrong values is a whole family of failure, and it is the family that survives a typical test suite. Reconciliation and recompute-and-compare were the two highest-value checks for exactly this reason.
    Make the effect idempotent and delivery stops mattering
    The same answer arrived in chapter 5, 12, 13, 14 and 16. Exactly-once delivery is a claim bounded by one system; an idempotent write is a property you own.
    Layout decisions are made once and paid for forever
    Partitioning, file size, sort order and grain set the cost of every backfill, every query and every erasure request that follows. Chapter 2 measured a 2,781× range on the same correction, and chapter 23 found the same decisions setting the price of deleting one person.
    Advice in this field is scale-dependent
    The maturity table in chapter 1 is the most useful thing to carry into an argument. Before accepting a recommendation, work out which of the four organisational stages it assumes — most disagreement in this field is two people who are both right about different rows.
    A closing note on the demos

    Every figure in this unit came from a model that runs in the page you were reading, and several of them contradicted what I expected before running them — sorting made a file 33% larger, leveled compaction turned out not to beat a B-tree on write amplification, and a partition-scan model had to be corrected because it did not credit contiguity.

    That is the habit worth taking away more than any individual number: build the model before writing the prose, and when they disagree, fix the prose. It applies to a pipeline design document exactly as it applies to a chapter.

    The unit, in twelve lines

    1. The role exists to solve a load problem and a logic problem, and the second is harder.
    2. Freshness is a sum of waits and runs, and the waits dominate unless something triggers on completion.
    3. Source systems discard history and change without notice. Contracts and CDC are the two defences.
    4. Object storage is immutable and high-latency. Read big contiguous chunks in parallel; everything about layout follows.
    5. Compression and pruning are properties of your data, not of the format. Measure rather than assume.
    6. Declare the grain, and never join two of them.
    7. Make the effect idempotent and delivery guarantees stop mattering.
    8. Key skew caps throughput and no partition count fixes it, in a broker or in a cluster.
    9. One definition of each metric, executed rather than documented.
    10. Point-in-time correctness or the model is measuring the future.
    11. Structure checks verify structure; only comparison verifies content.
    12. Layout decisions are made once and paid for forever — in backfills, in queries, and in erasure requests.
