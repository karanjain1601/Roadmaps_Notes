CHAPTER 12

Batch ingestion, ETL and ELT

Batch is unfashionable and it is how most data still moves, because a schedule is dramatically easier to reason about than a continuous stream. Two properties decide whether a batch pipeline is operable: whether re-running it is safe, and whether it can tell you what it has and has not yet seen. Almost everything else is detail.

    THE ANATOMY

    What a batch pipeline is made of

    Five stages, and each one has a decision attached that will matter later.

    Extract
    Read from the source. Chapter 4 established the options and the trap: full snapshot, incremental with a lookback, or log-based capture, and never a bare timestamp watermark.
    Land
    Write the extracted bytes somewhere durable, unmodified. The step people skip, and the one that makes everything after it recoverable. Covered in the final section.
    Transform
    Parse, clean, conform, join, aggregate. Where the modelling of chapter 9 gets applied.
    Load
    Write into the target table. The choice of write strategy here is what determines whether a retry is safe, and it is the subject of the first demo.
    Verify
    Check the result before anyone reads it: row counts, freshness, referential integrity, distribution. Frequently absent, which is why chapter 20 exists.

    Around these sits the scheduling question. A pipeline runs on a clock ("every day at 02:00"), on completion of an upstream task, or on data arrival ("when the file lands"). Chapter 2 measured why this matters: clock scheduling contributes a full wait interval to end-to-end freshness at every hop, and 89% of the worst-case staleness in that model was waiting rather than computing. Completion-triggered chains remove most of it, which is chapter 16's business.

    THE LETTERS

    ETL, ELT, and what actually changed

    The reordering of two letters describes a genuine architectural shift, driven by economics rather than by insight.

    ETL transforms data in a dedicated processing tier before loading it into the warehouse. ELT loads raw data into the warehouse first and transforms it there with SQL.

    ETL was not a design preference; it was a necessity. Warehouse capacity was bought in advance, expensive and fixed, so you did not spend it on parsing JSON. A separate transformation tier — Informatica, Talend, hand-written Java — was cheaper than warehouse compute.

    Two changes inverted that. Warehouse compute became elastic and billed by the second, so using it for transformation stopped being wasteful. And storage became cheap enough that keeping the raw data as well as the transformed data was no longer a real cost.

    The row that decides it

    Reprocessing. Under ETL, discovering that your revenue logic has been wrong for three months means re-extracting three months from a source system that may no longer hold it — and chapter 4 showed source systems discard history. Under ELT the raw data is still in your warehouse, so the fix is re-running SQL, which chapter 2 priced at seconds rather than hours given a sensible layout.

    That is why ELT won, and it is the same argument as the raw layer in chapter 11: keep the input, because your transformation logic is more likely to be wrong than your extraction.

    Two honest qualifications. ELT lands raw data including personal data, so masking and access control have to be enforced inside the warehouse rather than before it — that is chapter 23, and it is a real obligation rather than a footnote. And "anyone who writes SQL can change the logic" is simultaneously ELT's greatest strength and the mechanism by which a warehouse accumulates four thousand undocumented models; the discipline in chapter 16 is what keeps that from happening.

    THE FIRST PROPERTY

    Idempotency, and what readers see while you retry

    Every scheduled job fails eventually and gets re-run. Whether that is safe depends entirely on the write strategy, and the row count is only half the question.

    A pipeline is idempotent if running it twice for the same input produces the same result as running it once. Without that property, every retry is a judgement call and every backfill is dangerous — which in practice means nobody re-runs anything and errors go uncorrected.

    The demo reloads a partition that already holds a thousand rows from a previous run, fails partway, and retries. It tracks two things: the final state, and what a concurrent reader would see at each moment during the process.

    The four results separate along two independent axes, which is the point of tracking both.

    1. Append is not idempotent and never can be. The 600 rows written before the failure remain, the retry writes 1,000 more, and the partition ends with 2,600 rows against a correct 1,000. Every retry makes it worse.
    2. Delete-then-insert ends correct and empties the table twice on the way. The final count is right, so a check after the run passes. But the delete commits on its own, so any reader arriving in that window sees zero rows — and a dashboard that happens to refresh then reports no revenue. Worse, if the retry never happens, the data is simply gone.
    3. Merge is idempotent and still shows a mixed state. Upserting on a key means the row count never changes — it stays at exactly 1,000 throughout — so count-based checks see nothing at all. But mid-run 600 keys hold new values and 400 hold old ones, which is a combination that was never correct. Any aggregate computed then is wrong in a way no row count reveals.
    4. Only the atomic overwrite is both idempotent and never observably torn. A failed attempt commits nothing, so readers see the old thousand rows throughout and then the new thousand. This is exactly the pointer-swap mechanism measured in chapter 11, which is why a table format is an operational feature rather than a storage detail.
    The practical rule

    Make the unit of work a partition, and make the write an atomic replacement of that partition. Then re-running any day for any reason is safe, a backfill is a loop over days, and a failure leaves the previous good data in place. Every table format supports this directly; on a warehouse it is INSERT OVERWRITE for the partition inside a transaction, or a swap of a staging table.

    Where atomic partition replacement genuinely is not available, merge on a stable key is the fallback: idempotent, though not transactional. Reach for delete-then-insert only if neither exists, and know that you have an outage window on every run.

    THE SECOND PROPERTY

    Late data and the lookback window

    A batch job for "yesterday" runs once and declares yesterday finished. Data does not agree, and the gap is much larger than most people expect.

    Two timestamps matter and they are frequently conflated. Event time is when the thing happened. Arrival time is when it reached you. They differ because of mobile devices going offline, retries, queue backlogs, third-party batch feeds and timezone handling.

    So a job that runs at 02:00 to process yesterday's events by event time will miss every event from yesterday that arrives after 02:00. If it never revisits that day, those events are permanently absent — and unlike chapter 4's extraction loss, the data is sitting right there in your landing zone, unprocessed.

    The numbers are worse than intuition suggests. With a mean lateness of 24 hours and no lookback, a naive daily job captures about 36% of each day's events. That is not a rounding error; it is a majority of the data missing, in a pipeline that runs green every morning.

    A one-day lookback takes it to about 76%, two days to 90%, three days to 96%, seven days to 98%. The curve is the tail of the lateness distribution, and it flattens — which is the useful part, because it means there is a defensible stopping point rather than an argument.

    Why the daily figure looks fine

    Set the lookback to zero and note that the job never errors. It processes the events it can see, writes a partition, and reports success. Row counts are plausible because they are a consistent fraction of the truth, and comparing today against yesterday shows nothing because both are equally incomplete.

    The failure only becomes visible if you compare a recent day against the same day recomputed a week later — which is exactly the check almost nobody runs, and which chapter 21 argues should be automatic.

    Two implementation notes on lookback windows. They require the idempotency from the previous section: reprocessing the last three days every night is only safe if re-running a day replaces it rather than appending to it, which is the atomic-partition pattern. And the window should be derived from measurement, not chosen: instrument the distribution of arrival minus event time, pick the percentile you are willing to lose, and set the window from that. Then re-measure, because a new mobile client or a changed third-party feed moves the distribution without telling you.

    THE OPERATION EVERYONE FEARS

    Backfills

    Reprocessing history is routine if the design allows it and an incident if it does not. Chapter 2 priced the layout; this is the procedure.

    A backfill runs when transformation logic changes, when a bug is found, when a new column is added, or when an upstream source is corrected. The scale is set by chapter 2's arithmetic: the same fourteen-day correction was 8.8 seconds on a partitioned columnar table and 6.8 hours on an unpartitioned row-oriented one.

  1. Backfill into a separate target first. Write to a shadow table, compare it against production for a period that should be unchanged, and only then swap. This catches the case where your fix breaks something else, which is common.
  2. Go newest-first if consumers care about recency. Backfilling 2019 forward means the useful data arrives last. Backfilling backwards from today means each completed chunk is immediately valuable and you can stop early if the older history turns out not to matter.
  3. Rate-limit deliberately. A backfill is an unbounded amount of work submitted at once, and it will consume every slot in a shared warehouse. Cap concurrency, and prefer a lower priority queue — a backfill that finishes in six hours instead of two but does not delay the daily load is the better outcome.
  4. Never let a backfill share a schedule with production. Same code, different concurrency, different priority, different alerting. Otherwise a stalled backfill pages whoever is on call for the nightly run.
  5. Record what you did. Which days, which code version, when, and why. Six months later somebody will ask why March 2026 disagrees with the numbers in a board deck, and the answer is in that log or nowhere.
    6. The specific failure to plan for

    A backfill of two years of daily partitions rewrites every one of them, which under a table format creates two years of new file versions and a very large amount of metadata churn. On merge-on-read tables it can leave the table slower than it started until compaction catches up. Budget for the compaction, and check the table's file count before and after — chapter 7's request arithmetic is why.

    THE STEP PEOPLE SKIP

    The landing zone

    Write the bytes down before you touch them. Almost every recovery in this chapter depends on having done so.

    A landing zone is immutable storage holding source data exactly as extracted, partitioned by ingestion time, retained for a defined period. Not parsed, not cleaned, not deduplicated — as it arrived, including the malformed records.

    The reason is that it makes your pipeline replayable. Every recovery scenario in this chapter reduces to re-running transformation over retained input:

    What a landing zone buys, per failure
    SituationWith a landing zoneWithout
    Transformation bug foundRe-run transforms over retained rawRe-extract from source, if it still has the data
    Late data arrivedReprocess the affected days from rawThe events are lost with the extract
    New column needed retroactivelyIt is already in the raw payloadHistory starts today
    Consumer disputes a numberShow them the input that produced itArgue from memory
    Source system loses dataYou are the backupBoth copies gone

    Three practical points. Partition by ingestion time, not event time, because ingestion time is knowable at write time and never changes, whereas event time can arrive late and would force you to rewrite an existing partition — the exact thing you are trying to avoid. Chapter 8 also measured that ingestion order gives you clustering for free.

    Land in a format that suits writing, and compact to one that suits reading. Avro or JSON on arrival, since records come one at a time; Parquet after compaction. That was chapter 8's row-versus-columnar conclusion applied to the ingest path.

    Set retention deliberately and write it down. Raw data is cheap but not free, and it contains personal data subject to the deletion obligations in chapter 23. "Keep everything forever" is not a policy, it is the absence of one.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. ELT won on the reprocessing argument. Keeping raw data means a logic error is a re-run rather than a re-extraction from a source that has discarded its history.
    2. ELT's cost is that raw personal data lands in your warehouse, so masking and access control move inside it rather than in front of it.
    3. Append is never idempotent. A failure at 60% then a retry leaves 2,600 rows where 1,000 is correct, and every further retry is worse.
    4. Delete-then-insert ends correct and empties the table twice on the way, so a post-run check passes while readers during the window saw zero rows.
    5. Merge is idempotent and still shows a state that was never true. The row count stays at exactly 1,000 throughout, so count-based checks detect nothing while 600 keys hold new values and 400 hold old ones.
    6. Only atomic partition replacement is both idempotent and never observably torn. Make the partition the unit of work and re-running any day becomes routine.
    7. Event time and arrival time are different, and the gap is large. At 24 hours mean lateness a naive daily job captures about 36% of each day.
    8. The completeness curve flattens: roughly 36%, 76%, 90%, 96%, 98% at zero to seven days of lookback, so there is a defensible stopping point.
    9. A zero-lookback job never errors, and counts look plausible because they are a consistent fraction of the truth. Only comparing a day against itself recomputed later reveals it.
    10. Derive the lookback from a measured lateness distribution, and re-measure, because a new client or feed moves it silently.
    11. Backfill into a shadow target, newest-first, rate-limited, on a separate schedule, and log it.
    12. Land the raw bytes, partitioned by ingestion time. Every recovery in this chapter reduces to re-running transforms over retained input.
