CHAPTER 01

What data engineering is

Every definition of this job that fits on a slide is wrong in the same way: it describes moving data, which is the easy part. The distinguishing feature of the work is that you take on production responsibility for systems you did not build and cannot change, on behalf of people who will not know the difference between your bug and their own.

    WHERE IT STARTS

    Two problems that create the job

    Nobody sets out to hire a data engineer. The role gets created when a company runs into two specific failures, usually within a month of each other.

    Picture a company eighteen months old. There is one application database — Postgres, say — holding users, orders and payments. The engineers ship features against it. Somebody in finance wants to know last month's revenue by region, so an analyst is given a read-only credential and writes the query themselves. It works. For a while this arrangement is not just adequate, it is correct: the data is perfectly fresh, there is exactly one copy, and no engineering time was spent on it.

    Then two things go wrong.

    The load problem

    The analyst's queries are nothing like the application's queries. The app fetches one order by primary key, thousands of times a second, and each fetch touches a handful of pages already in memory. The analyst's query scans every order in the last ninety days, joins it to users, and aggregates. It reads gigabytes. Run once at 9am it is invisible. But now there are six analysts, each with a dashboard that auto-refreshes, and a scheduled report that recomputes the whole year every hour.

    The failure mode here is not "the database gets slower". It is that the database gets slower non-linearly, and it gets slower for the application rather than for the analyst. Checkout starts timing out. Nobody connects the incident to the dashboard, because the dashboard is fine. You will model this exactly in the second demo on this page, because the shape of that curve is the single most useful thing to understand about why analytical and transactional workloads get separated.

    The logic problem

    The second failure is quieter and more corrosive. Two people are asked for revenue and give different numbers.

    Neither is lying and neither is incompetent. One included refunds, the other did not. One used the order's created timestamp, the other used the payment's settled timestamp, and those fall on different sides of a month boundary for about 2% of orders. One excluded internal test accounts because they remembered the convention; the other did not know there was a convention. Each wrote a defensible query. There were four such decisions, each with two reasonable answers, so there were sixteen possible revenue figures, and the organisation had no mechanism for choosing one.

    The load problem is technical, the logic problem is organisational

    Almost everyone hired to solve the first one discovers the second is the harder half of the job. You can fix contention by buying a bigger machine. You cannot fix disagreement about what revenue means by buying anything, and the fix — one definition, in one place, that everyone's tools read from — is a piece of engineering that has to be maintained forever.

    Both problems have the same shape of solution: stop reading from the source, and put something in between. That something is a data platform, and building and running it is data engineering.

    A WORKING DEFINITION

    What the job is

    Long enough to be useful, short enough to remember.

    Data engineering is the design and operation of systems that take data from where it is produced to where it is consumed, at a reliability, freshness and cost the consumers can plan around.

    Four words in that sentence are doing the real work.

    Systems, not scripts
    A script that produces the right answer once is an analysis. A system produces the right answer every day at 6am, tells you when it did not, and can be re-run for last March without producing a different answer. The gap between those two things is most of the discipline.
    Produced, not owned
    Data originates in systems built by other teams for other purposes. The orders table exists to serve checkout, not to serve you. Its schema will change without notice, because to its owners that change is internal. Everything difficult about the job descends from this asymmetry, and chapters 4 to 6 are entirely about managing it.
    Consumed
    There is always a consumer, and they are the arbiter of whether the work succeeded. Sometimes it is an analyst, sometimes a machine learning model, sometimes a customer-facing feature, sometimes a regulator. Different consumers want incompatible things, which is why serving gets two full chapters rather than one.
    Plan around
    Not "as fast as possible" — predictable. A table that is reliably four hours stale is more useful than one that is usually current and occasionally three days behind, because the first can be designed around and the second cannot. Predictability is the actual deliverable.

    Notice what is absent from that definition: any particular technology. Not Spark, not Kafka, not a warehouse. A company processing ten thousand rows a day with a cron job and Postgres is doing data engineering, and doing it correctly if those are the right tools for ten thousand rows. Reaching for a distributed system before you need one is a recognised failure mode with a real cost, and chapter 3 gives you the arithmetic to argue against it.

    The one-sentence version

    If somebody asks at a party: you make sure the company's data is somewhere useful, in a shape people can use, and that it is right. The word doing the most work is right.

    THE DEFINING PROPERTY

    Blast radius

    Every table you build is a dependency somebody else takes on without telling you. This is what makes the job structurally different from application engineering.

    When an application engineer changes a function, the compiler or the test suite tells them what broke. The dependency graph is in the repository, it is machine-readable, and it is bounded by the codebase.

    When someone renames a column, the dependency graph includes: every pipeline that selects it, every dashboard built on those pipelines, every model whose features derive from them, every spreadsheet somebody exported last quarter and now refreshes weekly, and every automated email to a customer that quotes a number computed from it. None of that is in the repository. Much of it is not written down anywhere.

    Two results are worth dwelling on.

    The staging layer concentrates risk. A change to users in the application database reaches twelve of the nineteen nodes and all five consumer surfaces. That is not a badly designed graph — it is what happens when a single entity is genuinely central to a business. The lesson is not to avoid the shape, it is to know which nodes have that property before you touch them.

    The change type matters more than the node. Adding a nullable column to the most central table in the graph breaks nothing, because every consumer names its columns explicitly. Renaming one column in a peripheral table breaks whatever selects it. Chapter 6 turns this observation into a rule you can enforce automatically, which is the difference between knowing it and benefiting from it.

    The failure mode that has no alarm

    Compare the "stops loading" and "loads wrong values" options in the demo. The blast radius is identical, but detection is not. An outage is caught by a freshness check within one schedule interval. Wrong values pass every structural test — correct types, no nulls, plausible row count — and are found by a human noticing a number looks off, typically days later, after decisions have been made on it. This is the single most expensive class of data incident, and chapter 21 is largely about closing that gap.

    THE FIRST ARCHITECTURAL MOVE

    Isolating analytical load

    Why "just query the production replica" stops working, and why it stops working suddenly rather than gradually.

    Back to the load problem. The intuition most people have is that adding analytical queries to a transactional database degrades it proportionally: twice the load, twice the latency. That intuition is wrong, and being wrong about it is why these incidents surprise people.

    A database server with requests arriving faster than it can retire them behaves like any other queue. The useful approximation is the simplest queueing model there is: if a server is busy a fraction ρ of the time, the average time a request spends waiting plus serving scales as 1 / (1 - ρ). At 50% utilisation, response time is about twice the pure service time. At 90% it is ten times. At 98% it is fifty times. The curve has a vertical asymptote at 100%, and the last few percent of headroom are worth more than all the rest.

    Why this matters for a data engineer specifically

    Analytical queries are enormous compared to transactional ones. One report scanning 200 million rows can consume the same capacity as several hundred thousand key lookups. So analytics does not nudge ρ upward — it moves it in jumps, and if the transactional baseline is already at 70%, a single scheduled report can be the thing that crosses the knee of the curve.

    Set the baseline utilisation to 0.7 below, then add analysts one at a time and watch where the transactional latency multiplier goes.

    Three things the model shows that are worth stating plainly.

    1. The damage is invisible in the analyst's experience. Their query returns. It may even be fast. The cost lands on a different workload, owned by a different team, measured on a different dashboard.
    2. Routing analytics to a copy makes the transactional side immune — added utilisation goes to zero and the latency multiplier returns to its baseline, no matter how many analysts you add. That is the entire architectural argument for a separate analytical store, and it is a strong one.
    3. The copy is not free; it is paid for in freshness. Toggle it on and the freshness number stops being zero. You have traded a stability problem for a staleness problem, which is a trade worth making almost always — but it is a trade, and pretending otherwise is how people end up promising real-time numbers from a nightly batch.

    That trade — stability and cost against freshness — recurs at every layer of this unit. Chapter 2 makes it quantitative across a whole pipeline, and chapter 13 revisits it when the answer is streaming rather than batch.

    THE NEIGHBOURING ROLES

    What this job is not

    Titles vary wildly between companies. Responsibilities vary less, so define by responsibility.

    The most reliable way to tell these roles apart is to ask what the person is accountable for when something is wrong at 3am, and whether the artefact they produce is a system or an answer.

    The overlap is real and you should expect to be argued with

    At a company of twenty people, one person holds every row in that table. At a company of two thousand, the boundaries are contested and political, particularly the DE and analytics engineer line. The table is a tool for having the conversation, not a settled taxonomy. What matters is that for every table in production, exactly one person can say "that is mine". Tables with no owner are where incidents live.

    WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW

    The skill set, in priority order

    Ranked by how often it decides whether you succeed, not by how impressive it sounds in an interview.

    Roadmaps for this field tend to present a wall of logos. That is misleading about how the time is spent. Ordered by real leverage:

    1. SQL, deeply

    Not "can write a join" — window functions, the semantics of nulls in every clause, what the planner does with your query, why the same result set has three costs. It remains the interface to almost every storage engine in this unit, including the streaming ones.

    2. Data modelling

    Choosing a grain, deciding what is a fact and what is a dimension, handling something that changes over time. This is the skill that most separates pipelines people trust from pipelines people work around. Chapter 9.

    3. One programming language, properly

    Python for most people, sometimes Scala or Java near the JVM ecosystem, occasionally Go for services. Properly means testing, packaging, dependency management and debugging — not just writing scripts that work on your machine.

    4. How distributed systems fail

    Partial failure, retries, at-least-once delivery, why exactly-once is a claim that needs reading carefully. You do not need to implement consensus; you need to stop being surprised. Chapter 15.

    5. The command line and version control

    Unglamorous and constantly load-bearing. Logs, processes, permissions, ssh, and Git as a tool for recovering from your own mistakes rather than just committing.

    6. Cost arithmetic

    The ability to estimate what a design will cost per month before building it. This is rarely taught and disproportionately valued, because cloud data platforms make it trivially easy to spend an unbounded amount of money by accident.

    Conspicuously absent from the top six: any specific tool. Airflow, Spark, Kafka, dbt, Snowflake and their competitors all appear later in this unit and all matter, but every one of them is learnable in a week or two once the six above are in place. The reverse is not true, and a great deal of wasted effort in this field comes from learning tools in place of the model behind them.

    SCALE CHANGES THE JOB

    The same title at four different sizes

    Advice that is correct at one scale is actively harmful at another, which is why so much data engineering writing seems to contradict itself.

    What the role looks like as an organisation grows
    StageTypical shapeThe right answerThe characteristic mistake
    One database
    under ~20 engineers    		 No dedicated data person. Analysts query a read replica. A read replica, scheduled SQL, a BI tool. Total infrastructure: one extra database. Building a lakehouse for 40 GB of data. Months spent on a platform nobody needed.
    First platform
    ~20 to 100    		 One or two data engineers. Load problem has bitten. Metrics disagree. A warehouse, managed ingestion, a transformation tool, an orchestrator. Buy rather than build. Writing bespoke ingestion for standard SaaS sources. High maintenance, no differentiation.
    Real platform
    ~100 to 1000    		 A team. Multiple domains. Streaming appears. Governance becomes a requirement, not a preference. Self-service with guardrails: contracts, tests, lineage, cost attribution, a catalogue. Becoming a ticket queue. Every request routed through the team, which then becomes the bottleneck for the whole company.
    Federated
    1000+    		 Several teams, domain ownership, a platform team serving other data teams. Platform as a product, with published interfaces and genuine internal SLAs. Adopting a mesh structure without the platform maturity to support it, which yields the same silos plus a new vocabulary.

    You will meet all four of these opinions online, stated with total confidence and no mention of scale. When someone insists a warehouse is mandatory, or that orchestrators are overhead, or that streaming is always premature, the useful question is which row of that table they are standing in.

    How to place yourself

    Two questions locate you more reliably than headcount. First: can one person still name every table in production? Second: when a number is wrong, is there a single obvious place to look? Two yes answers mean you are in the first two rows, whatever the org chart says, and the simple answer is still the right one.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. The role exists to solve a load problem and a logic problem. The first is fixed by separating analytical from transactional storage. The second is fixed by having one definition of each metric in one place, and is the harder of the two.
    2. Queueing behaviour is non-linear. Latency scales as 1 / (1 - ρ), so the last few percent of database headroom are worth more than all the rest, and one large analytical query can cross the knee on its own.
    3. You own dependencies you did not create, for consumers you cannot enumerate. A change to a central staging table reached twelve of nineteen nodes and every consumer surface in the demo. Knowing which nodes have that property is a prerequisite for changing anything safely.
    4. Change type dominates blast radius. Adding a nullable column is safe almost everywhere; renaming one is not. That asymmetry is enforceable by machine, which chapter 6 does.
    5. Silent wrongness is the expensive failure. An outage and a corruption have the same blast radius and completely different detection times, because structural checks pass on plausible wrong values.
    6. Predictability beats speed. Reliably four hours stale is more useful than usually-current-and-sometimes-three-days-behind.
    7. Advice in this field is scale-dependent. Before accepting a recommendation, work out which of the four organisational stages it assumes.

    Pick the table at your own company you would least like to be wrong. Trace its consumers by asking around rather than reading code. The gap between what you find and what you expected is your real blast radius.
