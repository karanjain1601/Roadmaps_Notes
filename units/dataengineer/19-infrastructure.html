CHAPTER 19

Infrastructure, cloud and IaC

Data workloads have a shape that infrastructure advice written for web services gets wrong. They are bursty rather than steady, they care about aggregate throughput rather than per-request latency, and they can usually tolerate a machine disappearing mid-job. Those three properties change every sizing and pricing decision in this chapter.

    The one that matters most for data work: spot and preemptible instances

    Spare capacity, sold at 60–90% off, reclaimable at a few minutes' notice. For a web service that is difficult. For a batch job it is close to free money, because chapter 15 established that a task is the unit of retry and a lost task is simply re-run.

    The practical arrangement is a small on-demand core for the driver and the orchestrator, with the bulk of workers on spot. That single decision routinely takes 60% off a compute bill, and it costs you nothing except a retry policy you should have had anyway.

    Two network facts worth carrying. Egress out of the cloud is expensive and traffic within an availability zone is not — chapter 7 priced the first, and the second is why a cluster spread across zones for resilience pays a real shuffle cost. And private connectivity to managed services is worth configuring: routing warehouse traffic over a private endpoint rather than the public internet is usually both cheaper and a compliance requirement you will eventually be asked about.

    THE PRICING QUESTION

    Serverless economics

    Serverless is not cheaper. It is cheaper below a duty cycle and considerably more expensive above it, and the threshold is computable.

    Functions bill per gigabyte-second of execution plus a charge per invocation. An instance bills per hour whether or not it is doing anything. So the comparison is not about volume at all — it is about what fraction of the time you would be using the instance.

    Serverless against a provisioned instance monthly cost, and the duty cycle at which they cross

    PROVISIONED0
    CROSSOVER0duty cycle

    Three results.

    1. Serverless costs about five times as much at full utilisation. £703 a month against £139 for equivalent memory. You are paying for elasticity, and the premium is large.
    2. The crossover is a duty cycle, near 20%, not a volume. Below roughly one-fifth utilisation serverless wins; above it, an instance does.
    3. It holds across invocation lengths. The same duty cycle split into 0.2-second calls or 60-second ones crosses at the same point, because gigabyte-seconds are gigabyte-seconds. The one thing that shifts it is a cheaper instance — on spot, the crossover falls to a few percent.
    Where each genuinely belongs in a data platform

    Serverless for event-driven glue: reacting to a file landing, a webhook, a small transform that runs a few hundred times a day. The duty cycle is tiny and the operational saving is real.

    Provisioned or spot for anything with a sustained schedule: the orchestrator, streaming consumers, and batch clusters. A streaming consumer runs continuously by definition, so it is at 100% duty and serverless is the wrong tool by a factor of five.

    And beware the third case: functions have execution-time and memory limits, so a job that grows past them fails at an inconvenient moment. If a workload might ever need an hour or a hundred gigabytes, do not start it on a platform that caps at fifteen minutes.

    DEFINING IT IN CODE

    Declarative infrastructure and drift

    The argument for IaC is usually reproducibility. The measurable benefit is drift detection, and it depends entirely on how often you reconcile.

    The distinction that matters is not "code versus clicking" but imperative versus declarative. An imperative script says create a bucket; run it twice and you get an error or two buckets. A declarative definition says this bucket should exist with these properties, and the tool works out the difference between that and reality. The second is idempotent by construction, which is the same property chapter 12 demanded of pipeline writes, applied to infrastructure.

    Declarative tools work by keeping state: a record of what they believe exists. Reality can diverge from it — someone changes a security group in the console during an incident, a service adds a default, an autoscaler adjusts something. That divergence is drift, and it accumulates between reconciliations.

    The result to take away is that drift is linear in the reconciliation interval. At twelve manual changes a month: applying only on release, roughly six resources are drifted at any moment; weekly, 1.4; continuously, effectively zero. With imperative scripts it is unbounded — 144 after a year and still climbing — because nothing ever compares intent against reality.

    What drift does to your disaster-recovery story

    The stated benefit of IaC is that you can rebuild everything from code. That guarantee is exactly as good as your drift: if six of four hundred resources differ from their declarations, a rebuild produces something 1.5% different from what was running — and it will be different in precisely the places somebody changed by hand under pressure, which are the least documented and most load-bearing.

    So the DR claim is not "we have Terraform". It is "we reconciled last Tuesday and it was clean". Untested rebuilds are a plan, not a capability.

    Two practical points on state. It contains secrets — connection strings, generated passwords — so it belongs in encrypted remote storage with access control, never in the repository. And it needs locking, because two people applying at once produces exactly the concurrent-writer corruption chapter 11 measured, at the level of your infrastructure rather than your tables.

    The other habit worth adopting is modules over copies. Three environments defined by three copied directories will diverge, and the divergence will be discovered when production behaves differently from staging. One module with three parameter sets cannot diverge by accident.

    PACKAGING

    Containers, and how much Kubernetes you need

    Containers solve a real problem for data work. The orchestrator on top of them frequently solves a problem you do not have.

    The container argument is straightforward and strong: a pipeline has a dependency tree — a Python version, a JDBC driver, native libraries for Parquet and Arrow — and reproducing it identically on a laptop, in CI and on a worker is otherwise miserable. An image pins all of it, and the same artefact runs everywhere. This also makes the training-serving code-path problem in chapter 18 tractable, because both paths can share an image.

    Kubernetes is a separate decision. It genuinely gives you bin-packing across mixed workloads, autoscaling, self-healing and a common deployment model. It also gives you a cluster to upgrade, a networking model to learn, and a new category of incident. The honest test:

  1. Does your organisation already run it? If a platform team operates Kubernetes and will run your workloads on it, the marginal cost is small and the answer is yes.
  2. Do you have enough varied workloads for bin-packing to pay? Three scheduled jobs do not need a scheduler on top of a scheduler.
  3. Could a managed job runner do this? Cloud batch services, ECS tasks and the orchestrator's own executors run containers without a cluster to own.

    4. Where Kubernetes does earn its place in data platforms is running the orchestrator's workers, because chapter 16's tasks are exactly the bursty, heterogeneous, retryable workload it is good at. Running Spark on it is common and reasonable; running it because the pipeline needs somewhere to execute is usually more than the problem requires.

    THE AWKWARD PART

    Environments, and the data problem

    Application teams have this solved. Data teams do not, because the interesting part of the system is the data and you cannot copy it.

    Standard practice is development, staging and production with identical infrastructure. That works for the infrastructure and breaks on the contents. Production data cannot simply be copied into staging: it contains personal data subject to chapter 23's obligations, it is frequently too large, and a staging job pointed at production sources can write to production or overload it.

    Four ways to populate a non-production environment
    ApproachRealismCost and riskBest for
    Synthetic dataLowSafe, and needs maintaining as schemas changeUnit and integration tests
    Sampled productionMediumStill personal data unless masked; sampling breaks joinsDevelopment against realistic shapes
    Masked full copyHighExpensive, and masking must be verifiedPerformance and migration testing
    Read production, write elsewhereHighestLoad on production; needs strict write isolationValidating a change against real data
    The pattern that works best in practice

    Give every developer their own schema or dataset within the production warehouse, reading production tables and writing only to their own namespace. You get real data, real scale and real query behaviour, with no copy to maintain and no masking to get wrong — and the isolation is enforced by permissions rather than by convention. dbt's target schemas are built for exactly this.

    Two conditions make it safe. Write permissions must be genuinely scoped, so a mistake cannot touch a production table. And read cost must be attributed, or a developer's exploratory full-table scan lands on a shared bill with no owner — which is the next section.

    Sampling deserves one specific warning. A 1% sample of each table taken independently destroys referential integrity: the orders in your sample mostly reference customers that were not sampled, so joins return almost nothing and your test passes for the wrong reason. If you sample, sample by entity — pick 1% of customers and take all their related rows.

    THE BILL

    Cost attribution and control

    Cloud data platforms make it trivially easy to spend an unbounded amount of money by accident, and the defence is attribution before it is optimisation.

    The structural problem is that costs are incurred by queries and jobs while bills arrive by service. "Warehouse: £41,000" is not actionable. "This dashboard's hourly refresh: £8,200" is.

  4. Tag everything, and enforce it. Team, environment, pipeline. A resource without tags should fail the plan, because retrofitting tags across a live account never happens.
  5. Attribute query cost to its owner. Warehouses expose per-query bytes scanned and credits; joining that to the user or service account is a small job and the highest-return thing in this section.
  6. Alert on rate of change, not on the total. A monthly total tells you after the money is gone. Daily spend against the trailing average catches the runaway query the day it starts.
  7. Set hard limits where you can. Query timeouts, scan limits, warehouse auto-suspend, budget actions. A limit that fires is an annoyance; its absence is a five-figure surprise.
  8. Review the top ten queries by cost monthly. Consistently a short exercise with a large return, because spend is heavily concentrated.

    9. Three recurring sources of surprise, all of which this unit has already priced. Small files, from chapter 7: identical bytes, tens of thousands of pounds apart, because requests are charged per object. Dashboards auto-refreshing against raw tables, from chapter 17: fifty-nine pointless query storms an hour. And a backfill with no concurrency cap, from chapter 12: an unbounded amount of work submitted at once, on a platform that will happily scale to accept it.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Learn the six primitives, not one vendor's console. They map across clouds; the console does not.
    2. Spot instances are close to free money for batch, because a task is already the unit of retry. On-demand core plus spot workers routinely takes 60% off compute.
    3. Serverless costs about 5× more at full utilisation — £703 against £139 for equivalent memory.
    4. The crossover is a duty cycle near 20%, not a volume, and it holds across invocation lengths from 0.1 s to 60 s. A cheaper instance moves it down sharply.
    5. Streaming consumers run at 100% duty, so serverless is the wrong tool for them by a factor of five.
    6. Declarative beats imperative because it is idempotent, the same property chapter 12 required of writes.
    7. Drift is linear in the reconciliation interval: about 6 resources of 400 on a monthly apply, 1.4 weekly, effectively zero continuous, and unbounded — 144 after a year — with imperative scripts.
    8. Your DR guarantee is exactly as good as your drift, and the drifted resources are the hand-edited, undocumented, load-bearing ones.
    9. State holds secrets and needs locking, or two concurrent applies reproduce chapter 11's corruption at the infrastructure level.
    10. Containers are clearly worth it; Kubernetes is a separate decision. It earns its place running orchestrator workers, less so as somewhere for three jobs to execute.
    11. Give developers a schema in production rather than a copy of it — real data, real scale, isolation by permission.
    12. Sample by entity, never by row, or referential integrity breaks and your tests pass for the wrong reason.
    13. Attribute cost before optimising it, and alert on rate of change rather than the monthly total.
