CHAPTER 09

Modelling for analytics

This is the chapter that most determines whether anyone trusts your data. Storage layout decides what a query costs; modelling decides whether the answer is right. Two specific mistakes account for an enormous share of wrong numbers in production, and both of them preserve the grand total while corrupting every breakdown.

    THE MISMATCH

    Why any modelling is needed

    Source systems are shaped by how an application writes. Analysis is shaped by how a business asks. Those two shapes are not the same, and the gap is what modelling closes.

    An application database is organised to make writes safe and cheap. Each fact lives in exactly one place so an update touches one row, foreign keys prevent contradictions, and the table names reflect the code's internal domain objects. That design is correct for its purpose and produces something almost unusable for analysis.

    A question like "revenue by region by month, split by whether the customer was new" reaches across eight tables, requires knowing which of three date columns means what, and depends on conventions nobody wrote down. Ask ten analysts and you get ten queries and, as chapter 1 showed, several different answers.

    Modelling produces an intermediate representation with three properties the source cannot have:

    It matches how questions are asked
    Organised around business processes and the things you slice them by, rather than around the application's object graph.
    It encodes decisions once
    Whether refunds count, which timestamp is authoritative, which accounts are internal. Settled in one place instead of re-litigated per query.
    It preserves history the source has discarded
    The source knows a customer is in the US. The model can know they were in the UK last March, which is the difference between an answer and a wrong answer.

    THE OTHER TRADITION

    Normal forms, and where they belong

    Normalisation is not wrong. It is optimised for a goal analytics does not have.

    Normalisation eliminates redundancy so that every fact is stored once. The progression is worth knowing compactly:

    Normal forms, and the anomaly each one removes
    FormRequiresPrevents
    1NFAtomic values, no repeating groupsComma-separated lists in a column
    2NF1NF, plus no partial dependency on a composite keyStoring a product's name against every order line
    3NF2NF, plus no transitive dependencyStoring a city's country on every customer row
    BCNF and aboveProgressively stricter functional dependency rulesRarer anomalies with overlapping candidate keys

    The goal is update safety: if a fact exists in one place, it cannot become inconsistent with itself. That is exactly what a transactional system needs, and it is why 3NF is the right default for an application database.

    Why analytics deliberately denormalises

    Analytical workloads almost never update, so the anomalies normalisation prevents cannot occur. Meanwhile normalisation imposes a cost that analytics feels acutely: answering any real question requires many joins, each of which is work the engine must do and complexity the analyst must get right.

    So the trade reverses. Storage is cheap, joins are expensive, updates do not happen — therefore duplicate the country onto every row and skip the join. That is not sloppiness; it is the correct optimisation for a workload with the opposite properties.

    THE STANDARD APPROACH

    Facts and dimensions

    Kimball's dimensional model has survived four decades and several complete changes in hardware, because it describes how people ask questions rather than how machines store bytes.

    Everything divides into two kinds of table.

    Fact tables

    Things that happened, at a point in time. An order, a payment, a page view, a sensor reading. Long and narrow, growing forever. Columns are mostly measures (numeric, additive) and foreign keys to dimensions.

    Dimension tables

    The things you slice by. Customer, product, date, store, campaign. Short and wide, changing slowly. Columns are mostly descriptive attributes you filter and group on.

    The test for which is which: would you sum this, or group by it? Sum it and it is a measure in a fact. Group by it and it is an attribute in a dimension. A quantity you would do both to — a price you sum but also band into ranges — usually belongs in the fact with the banding done in the dimension or at query time.

    Three kinds of fact table, distinguished by what a row means:

    Transaction fact
    One row per event. The default and the most flexible: you can aggregate it to anything. fct_order_line, fct_payment.
    Periodic snapshot fact
    One row per entity per period, recording state. fct_account_balance_daily. Necessary for anything that is a level rather than a flow, because balances cannot be derived by summing transactions once history is truncated.
    Accumulating snapshot fact
    One row per process instance, updated as it advances through milestones. An order with columns for placed, picked, shipped, delivered. Unusual in that the row mutates, and the right shape for pipeline and duration analysis.

    Additivity is the property that makes measures usable, and it comes in three grades worth distinguishing. Additive measures sum across every dimension — revenue, quantity. Semi-additive measures sum across some but not time — an account balance can be summed across customers but not across days. Non-additive measures cannot be summed at all — ratios, percentages, unit prices. Store the numerator and denominator separately and compute the ratio after aggregating; averaging averages is one of the most common quiet errors in reporting.

    THE FIRST BIG MISTAKE

    Grain, and the fan-out trap

    The grain of a fact table is what one row means. Getting it wrong, or mixing two, produces numbers that are inflated by a factor nobody notices.

    Declaring the grain is the first thing you do when designing a fact table, and it should be a sentence you can say out loud: "one row per order line", "one row per payment attempt", "one row per account per day". If you cannot say it in one sentence, the design is not finished.

    The trap is what happens when a measure recorded at one grain is joined to a table at a finer grain. Shipping cost belongs to an order. Line amounts belong to lines. Join them and every order's shipping cost appears once per line — so summing it multiplies the total by roughly the average number of lines per order.

    Three things the model shows that are worth more than the general warning.

    1. The line-grain measure is untouched. Revenue is exactly right. Only the measure that came from the coarser table inflates, which is why the bug survives a review — most of the query is correct.
    2. The inflation factor is not the average line count. With shipping rising with basket size, the model reports about 3.9× where the mean is about 3.5×. The correct factor is the shipping-weighted mean number of lines, and because big baskets both cost more to ship and have more lines, the weighting pushes it up. So the tempting fix of dividing by the average line count is itself wrong. Switch shipping to flat and watch the two numbers converge.
    3. count(*) silently becomes a line count. Labelled "orders" on a dashboard, it is wrong by the same factor and nobody re-reads the SQL.
    Why this is so hard to catch

    Set the maximum lines per order to 1 and inflation is exactly 1.00 — so the query is perfectly correct in development against a small dataset where most orders have a single line, and wrong in production where they do not. The error scales with a data property rather than with anything in the code.

    Of the two fixes, aggregate-then-join is exact and is what you should reach for: collapse the fine grain up to the coarse one before joining, so each order contributes its shipping once. Allocation — dividing shipping by the line count so it sums back correctly — also totals right, and it is the technique to use when you genuinely need shipping available at line grain, for example to compute margin per line. But an allocated measure is no longer meaningful on its own: the average of an allocated shipping cost is not the average shipping cost of an order.

    The structural answer, and the reason dimensional modelling insists on declaring grain, is one fact table per grain. Order-grain measures live in fct_order, line-grain measures in fct_order_line, and the two are combined only by aggregating one to the other's grain or by joining both to shared dimensions. Fact tables should never be joined directly to each other.

    SHAPE

    Star against snowflake

    The same information with the dimensions normalised or not. The default answer is star, and the reasons are worth knowing rather than assuming.

    shapes.txt
    -- star: one flat dimension table, denormalised
dim_product(product_key, name, brand, category, department, supplier_name, supplier_country)

-- snowflake: the hierarchy normalised out into its own tables
dim_product(product_key, name, brand_key, category_key, supplier_key)
dim_brand(brand_key, brand_name)
dim_category(category_key, category_name, department_key)
dim_department(department_key, department_name)
dim_supplier(supplier_key, supplier_name, supplier_country)
    The trade, on the axes that actually differ
    StarSnowflake
    Joins for a typical queryOne per dimensionOne per level of every hierarchy
    Analyst comprehensibilityHigh — one table per conceptLow — requires knowing the hierarchy
    Dimension storageLarger, with repetitionSmaller
    Update of a hierarchy attributeRewrite many rowsOne row
    Interaction with columnar storageRepetition compresses away—

    Star wins for a reason that chapter 8 measured directly. The objection to denormalising is redundancy, and in a columnar store redundancy is almost free: a department name repeated across ten thousand product rows is a low-cardinality column, which dictionary-encodes to a few bits per row. The measured 44× ratio on a four-value column in the previous chapter is exactly this effect. Dimension tables are small to begin with, so the storage argument for snowflaking is close to nil, while the comprehension argument for star is large and permanent.

    When snowflaking is right anyway

    Three cases. A hierarchy genuinely shared by several dimensions, where duplicating it would mean maintaining it twice. A dimension so large that the repetition is material — hundreds of millions of rows, which happens with user dimensions at consumer scale. And a hierarchy level that is itself a dimension with its own facts, such as a supplier you also record deliveries against. Outside those, flatten it.

    THE SECOND BIG MISTAKE

    Dimensions that change

    A customer moves country. Every historical order they placed is now attributed differently, and nothing in your pipeline will tell you.

    This is the problem the source system cannot help with, because it has thrown the old value away. Handling it is the single highest-value thing a dimensional model does, and there are six recognised approaches. The demo runs a query under each and compares.

    Type 0 — retain original
    Never update. The value is the one first seen. Right for genuinely immutable attributes: original signup channel, date of birth, first order date.
    Type 1 — overwrite
    Replace the old value. Simple, cheap, and destroys history. Right for corrections — a misspelled name should not create a historical version — and wrong for anything you analyse over time.
    Type 2 — add a new row
    Insert a new dimension row with a new surrogate key, valid_from, valid_to and an is_current flag. Facts reference the surrogate key that was current when they occurred, so history is exact. The standard answer, and the reason surrogate keys exist at all.
    Type 3 — add a column
    Keep country and previous_country. Answers "compare before and after the reorganisation" and nothing more — you get exactly one step of history and no dates. Narrow but occasionally exactly what is wanted.
    Type 4 — history in a separate table
    The dimension stays type 1 with current values only; a companion history table holds the versions. Keeps the hot dimension small when an attribute changes rapidly, at the cost of a more complex join for historical queries.
    Type 6 — hybrid
    A type 2 row that also carries the current value in a separate column. So the same fact can be reported both by the country the customer was in then and by the country they are in now. Named type 6 because 1 + 2 + 3 = 6.
    The same query under each SCD type generated change histories, facts attributed and compared against the true as-was answer
    DIMENSION ROWS0
    TYPE 1 ERROR0%worst country
    TYPE 0 ERROR0%worst country
    TYPE 2 ERROR0%
    GRAND TOTAL—across all types

    The reason this is never caught by reconciliation

    Look at the grand total tile. Every SCD type reports exactly the same overall revenue — they differ only in which country each pound is assigned to. So a reconciliation that checks total revenue against the source passes perfectly while every regional breakdown is wrong by up to a quarter.

    This is the same shape of failure as the cold-row corruption in chapter 5 and the resurrected rows in the same chapter's stream demo: totals agree, structure does not. Any data quality regime that only checks totals is blind to the entire class.

    Two practical notes on type 2, which is what you will implement most.

    Surrogate keys are what make it work. The fact table cannot reference the natural key — customer 4182 has several versions — so the dimension gets its own meaningless integer key per version, and the fact stores the version current at the moment of the event. This is why dimensional models insist on surrogate keys even when a perfectly good natural key exists.

    Do not make every attribute type 2. A dimension where all thirty columns are type 2 generates a new row on every change to anything, and grows without bound. Decide per column: which attributes will somebody legitimately want to analyse historically? Usually a handful — geography, segment, tier — and the rest can be type 1.

    FIVE MORE

    Other ways models go wrong

    No date dimension
    Using raw timestamps means every query re-implements fiscal periods, week numbering, holidays and "is this a working day". A date dimension is a one-off table of a few thousand rows and one of the highest-return artefacts you will build.
    Nulls in foreign keys
    A fact with a null dimension key drops out of inner joins, so totals silently shrink. Use an explicit unknown member — key -1, labelled "Unknown" — so the row survives the join and the gap is visible rather than absent.
    Many-to-many left unresolved
    A customer in several segments joined naively to facts fans out exactly like the grain demo above, and inflates by the average number of segments. It needs a bridge table with explicit allocation weights, and a decision about whether the weights sum to one.
    Modelling the source instead of the process
    Mirroring the application's tables with dimensional names produces something with all the source's awkwardness and a new vocabulary. Model the business process, and expect the shape to differ from the source.
    One enormous flat table
    Tempting on columnar storage, and it works until an attribute changes, at which point you have no mechanism for history, or until two grains need to coexist, at which point you have the fan-out problem with no structure to fix it.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Normalisation optimises for update safety, which analytical workloads do not need. Denormalising for analytics is the correct optimisation, not laziness.
    2. Facts are what happened; dimensions are what you slice by. The test is whether you would sum it or group by it.
    3. Declare the grain in one sentence before designing a fact table. If you cannot, the design is unfinished.
    4. Joining grains inflates the coarser measure. Line revenue stays exactly right while order shipping multiplies, which is why the bug survives review.
    5. The inflation factor is the measure-weighted mean, not the plain mean — about 3.9× against a mean of 3.5× when shipping rises with basket size. So dividing by the average line count is itself wrong.
    6. The bug is invisible in development. At one line per order the inflation is exactly 1.00; it scales with a property of the data, not of the code.
    7. One fact table per grain, and never join facts to each other. Aggregate to a common grain, or join both to shared dimensions.
    8. Prefer star to snowflake, because columnar dictionary encoding makes the redundancy nearly free while the comprehension benefit is permanent.
    9. Type 2 is the default for anything analysed over time, and surrogate keys exist precisely to make it possible.
    10. Every SCD type reports the same grand total. Only the attribution differs, by up to a quarter per country. Reconciliation on totals cannot detect this class of error at all.
    11. Choose SCD type per column, not per dimension, or the table grows without bound.
    12. Build a date dimension and an explicit unknown member. Both are cheap and both prevent a recurring class of quiet error.

    Find a dashboard tile labelled with a count of something and read the SQL behind it. If it is count(*) over a join to a finer grain, it is wrong, and it has probably been wrong for a long time.
