CHAPTER 10

OLTP, OLAP and the engine families

Databases are usually compared by their feature lists. It is far more useful to compare them by their physical structures, because a structure determines what is cheap and what is impossible, and no amount of engineering effort moves those limits. Two index structures and five data models cover almost the whole market.

    THE OLD DIVIDE

    Two workloads, described physically

    OLTP and OLAP are not categories of product. They are two clusters of access pattern, and every engine choice is a bet on which cluster you are in.

    The two workload shapes, by what they do rather than what they are called
    Transactional (OLTP)Analytical (OLAP)
    Rows per queryOne, or a handfulMillions
    Columns per queryAll of themA few
    WritesConstant, small, in placeBulk, append or replace

    ConcurrencyThousands of sessionsTens

    Implied layoutRow-orientedColumn-oriented

    The last row is the one chapter 8 established, and it explains why the divide has persisted through several complete changes in hardware: it is grounded in a physical fact about contiguity rather than in a product decision.

    HTAP, and why to be sceptical

    A recurring product claim is that one engine can serve both, usually branded HTAP. The honest version is real and narrower than the marketing: several engines now maintain two representations of the same data — a row store for writes and a column store for scans — and keep them in sync. That genuinely works, and it is a good fit for operational dashboards over live data.

    What it does not do is remove the trade. You are paying for two copies, the analytical side lags the transactional side by some interval, and heavy analytical queries still compete for the same machine. Treat HTAP as a convenience for moderate volumes rather than as a reason to skip a separate analytical store.

    THE TWO INDEX STRUCTURES

    B-trees, LSM trees and the RUM trade

    Nearly every storage engine in use is one of these two. They make opposite bets, and the bet is visible in three measurable quantities.

    A B-tree keeps keys sorted in fixed-size pages arranged as a shallow tree. A lookup walks a handful of levels; an update rewrites the leaf page in place. This is Postgres, MySQL's InnoDB, SQL Server, SQLite.

    An LSM tree — log-structured merge tree — buffers writes in memory, flushes them to an immutable sorted file, and merges files together in the background. Nothing is ever updated in place. This is RocksDB, Cassandra, HBase, ScyllaDB, and the storage layer beneath a great many newer systems.

    The comparison is best framed by the RUM conjecture: you can optimise for reads, updates or memory, and you get to pick two. Three amplification factors make it concrete:

    Write amplification
    Bytes written to the device per byte of logical write. A B-tree writing a 100-byte row must eventually flush a whole 4 KB page. An LSM writes the row once, then rewrites it again at each compaction level.
    Read amplification
    Bytes read per byte returned. A B-tree point lookup reads one leaf page. An LSM may have to check several levels, which bloom filters mostly avoid for point lookups and cannot help with at all for range scans.
    Space amplification
    Bytes stored per byte of live data. B-tree pages sit partly empty; LSM levels hold superseded versions until compaction removes them.

    Three results, and the first one contradicts the summary you will usually be given.

    1. Leveled compaction does not win on write amplification. On the default 100 GB dataset with random keys, a B-tree and a leveled LSM both come out around 42×. Set the device to NVMe, where random and sequential writes cost about the same, and leveled LSM never beats a B-tree at any write share, because the write amp is a tie and its reads are slightly worse. The LSM advantage is not the byte count.
    2. The advantage is sequential I/O and space. Move the device to SATA SSD, where a random write costs several times a sequential one, and leveled overtakes the B-tree at about a 1.3% write share — essentially immediately. Its space amplification is 1.10 against the B-tree's 1.50. Those are the real wins.
    3. Tiered compaction is the genuine write specialist, and it is expensive. Its write amplification is about 6× against 42×, but read amplification on range scans is 44× against 4.4×, and space amplification is 5.5×. So it overtakes a B-tree later than leveled does, despite writing far less, because it has to overcome a much larger read penalty first.
    What to take from this practically

    Leveled LSM is the sensible default for a write-heavy store, which is why RocksDB defaults to it. Tiered is for genuinely write-dominated ingestion where you accept slower reads — time-series and log workloads. And a B-tree remains excellent when writes have locality: switch write locality to append-mostly and its write amplification falls from 42× to about 3.6×, because many rows land in the same page before it is flushed. A B-tree on a monotonic key is not a write-amplification problem at all.

    FIVE DATA MODELS

    What you are allowed to say

    Above the index structure sits the data model, which decides what queries you can express at all.

    Relational
    Tables, rows, a declarative language, and a planner that chooses the strategy. The general-purpose default. Its distinguishing property is that you did not have to anticipate the query — a normalised schema answers questions nobody thought of when it was designed. Postgres, MySQL, and every warehouse.
    Document
    Self-contained nested records keyed by id. Excellent when an aggregate is genuinely read and written whole — a product listing, a user profile — because one read gets everything with no joins. Weak the moment you want to query across documents in a way the embedding did not anticipate. MongoDB, DynamoDB in document mode, Couchbase.
    Key-value
    Get and put an opaque blob by key. The narrowest interface and consequently the fastest and most scalable. Ideal for caching, sessions and counters; useless for any question that is not "give me this key". Redis, Memcached, DynamoDB.
    Wide-column
    Rows keyed by a partition key, with a sorted clustering key inside each partition. Effectively a distributed sorted map. Superb at "give me this partition's recent entries in order" at enormous write rates, and it requires you to design the table around the query in advance. Cassandra, HBase, Bigtable.
    Graph
    Nodes and edges as first-class entities, with edges stored as direct references. Its one structural advantage is index-free adjacency, which the next demo measures. Neo4j, Neptune.

    Worth noting explicitly: the data model and the index structure are independent choices. Cassandra is wide-column on an LSM; Postgres is relational on a B-tree; MongoDB is document on a B-tree (WiredTiger can do either); a warehouse is relational on columnar files. Do not infer one from the other.

    MEASURING THE DIFFERENCE

    Where a data model changes the asymptotics

    Most model differences are matters of convenience. One of them changes the complexity class, and it is worth seeing why.

    Relational databases follow relationships with an index lookup: given a user, find their friend rows by searching an index, which costs roughly log₂(N) in the table size. A graph database stores each node's edges as direct pointers, so following one costs a dereference regardless of how large the database is. This is index-free adjacency, and it is the entire technical argument for graph engines.

    For a single hop the difference is unremarkable. For multi-hop traversal it compounds, because the number of edges followed grows exponentially in the hop count while the per-edge penalty stays.

    The traversal result is the sharpest: at a billion nodes, three hops and a branching factor of thirty, a graph engine does about 28,000 pointer dereferences while a relational engine does the same 28,000 edge-follows but pays an index lookup for each, about thirty times the work. Raise the dataset to a hundred billion rows and the graph cost does not change at all while the relational cost rises with log₂(N). That independence from dataset size is the property being sold.

    Switch the pattern to the aggregate and the ranking inverts completely: columnar touches about 1.6 GB where the row store touches 48 GB and the graph engine is worst of all. Neither result is a surprise once you know the structures, and together they make the point that there is no engine that wins everything — only engines matched or mismatched to a pattern.

    CHOOSING

    A decision order that works

    Start from the access pattern, not from the data.

  1. Write down the three queries that must be fast. Not all queries — three. If you cannot name them, you are not ready to choose, and the answer is a relational database because it does not require you to know.
  2. Ask whether the pattern is known in advance and stable. If yes, a specialised model can be dramatically better. If no, relational, because every non-relational model asks you to design around the query.
  3. Check the write rate against the read pattern. Sustained heavy writes with sorted-range reads points at wide-column on an LSM; that combination is what Cassandra exists for.
  4. Check whether relationships are the subject. Multi-hop traversal as the core operation, not an occasional join, is the graph case. Two-table joins are not.
  5. Default to Postgres and be honest about why not. It does relational, JSON documents, key-value, full-text, geospatial and vectors adequately, and adequately in one system usually beats excellently in four. Chapter 3's arithmetic applies.
    6. The failure mode this order prevents

    Choosing an engine from the shape of the data rather than the shape of the queries. Nested JSON does not imply a document database — Postgres handles JSONB well and lets you query across documents afterwards. Data with relationships does not imply a graph database. Large volume does not imply a distributed store; a single machine now handles terabytes. The question is always what you will ask, and how often.

    THE HONEST VERSION

    Polyglot persistence and what it costs

    Using the best engine for each job is correct in principle and frequently a mistake in practice.

    The argument for polyglot persistence is sound as far as it goes: different patterns have different optimal structures, so use several. What the argument omits is that each additional engine carries a fixed cost that has nothing to do with data:

    Per-engine costs

    • Operational knowledge, upgrades, on-call
    • Backup, restore and disaster recovery, tested
    • Monitoring, access control, audit
    • A driver and idioms in every service that touches it

    Cross-engine costs

    • Data must be kept in sync, which needs CDC and is eventually consistent
    • No transactions spanning engines
    • Two sources of truth for the same entity, which will diverge
    • Debugging spans systems with unrelated tooling

    The version that works in practice is one primary store plus purpose-built derived stores. One engine is authoritative — usually relational. Others are populated from it and are explicitly disposable: a Redis cache, a search index, a warehouse copy. Each derived store can be rebuilt from the primary, so a divergence is a rebuild rather than an investigation, and there is never a question about which copy is right.

    That framing also makes the cost visible in the right place. A derived store is a pipeline you now own, with the freshness and correctness obligations of every other pipeline in this unit.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. OLTP and OLAP are access patterns, not products, and the row-versus-column layout that follows is grounded in physical contiguity rather than product strategy.
    2. HTAP is real and narrower than advertised: two representations kept in sync, so you pay for two copies and still get a lag.
    3. The RUM trade is the frame: optimise reads, updates or memory, pick two.
    4. Leveled LSM does not beat a B-tree on write amplification — both about 42× on random keys. On NVMe it never overtakes at any write share.
    5. The LSM advantage is sequential I/O and space. Once a random write costs 4× a sequential one, leveled overtakes at roughly a 1.3% write share, and its space amplification is 1.10 against 1.50.
    6. Tiered compaction writes far less and overtakes later, because 44× range-read amplification and 5.5× space amplification are a large penalty to overcome first.
    7. A B-tree with write locality is fine. Append-mostly keys take its write amplification from 42× to about 3.6×.
    8. Data model and index structure are independent. Do not infer one from the other.
    9. Index-free adjacency is the one asymptotic difference. Graph traversal cost is independent of dataset size; relational grows as log₂(N) — about 30× at a billion rows.
    10. The ranking inverts completely by pattern. Columnar touches 1.6 GB where a row store touches 48 GB on a two-of-sixty-column aggregate, and the graph engine is worst. No engine wins everything.
    11. Choose from the queries, never from the shape of the data. Nested JSON does not imply a document store; relationships do not imply a graph.
    12. One authoritative store plus disposable derived stores beats genuine polyglot persistence, because a divergence becomes a rebuild rather than an investigation.
