CHAPTER 17

Serving analytics and BI

Chapter 1 opened with two problems: analytical load crushing a transactional database, and two people giving different answers to the same question. Fifteen chapters have been spent on the first. This one is about the second, which turned out to be the harder half and has a technical solution people consistently under-invest in.

    THE AUDIENCE

    Who is actually reading this

    Four consumers with genuinely incompatible needs. Trying to serve them all from one artefact is why so many warehouses have a table nobody understands.

    What each consumer needs, and what they will do if they do not get it
    ConsumerWantsToleratesIf underserved
    Executive dashboards A handful of numbers, sub-second, always available Hours of staleness Somebody maintains a spreadsheet, and it becomes the real source of truth
    Analysts Flexible SQL over well-modelled tables, ad-hoc joins Seconds to minutes They query raw tables and rebuild your logic, differently
    Operational users Data in the tool they already work in — CRM, support, ads Minutes to an hour They copy-paste from a dashboard, daily, forever
    Embedded and customer-facing Sub-second at high concurrency, per-tenant isolation Very little Your product is slow, which is a different kind of problem
    The pattern in the last column

    Every one of those failure modes is the consumer routing around you, and each creates a second definition of the truth that will drift from yours. The spreadsheet, the analyst's rebuilt logic and the copy-paste are all the metric-drift problem in the next section arriving through the back door.

    So the serving question is not only "is this fast enough" but "is this convenient enough that nobody builds an alternative". Those are different bars and the second one is higher.

    STRUCTURE

    Marts, and what belongs in one

    The last layer of chapter 11's raw / staging / marts progression, and the one with a discipline problem.

    A mart is a set of tables shaped for one audience or one business process, built on the conformed dimensions and facts of chapter 9. Its purpose is to make the common questions easy and to make the answers consistent.

    The discipline problem is that marts multiply. Every request for something slightly different produces another table, and after two years there are four hundred models, of which perhaps sixty are queried. The usual causes:

    A mart per requester rather than per process
    mart_revenue_for_finance and mart_revenue_for_growth will diverge, and then two teams have different revenue. Build per business process; let audiences filter.
    Pre-computing every combination
    Daily, weekly and monthly rollups by three dimensions is twelve tables that could be one, given a warehouse that aggregates quickly. Pre-aggregate where the demo in the next section says you must, not by default.
    No deprecation path
    Nothing is ever deleted because nobody knows what depends on it — chapter 1's blast radius, unresolved. Query logs answer this: a model with no reads in ninety days is a candidate, and saying so publicly for a fortnight before dropping it is a cheap process.

    A useful rule for what belongs in a mart at all: if two consumers would compute it the same way, it belongs upstream; if only one would, it belongs in their mart. Logic that is shared but implemented twice is the drift problem again.

    THE HARDER HALF

    Metric definitions, and what disagreement costs

    Chapter 1 described this in words. Here is the arithmetic, and it is worse than the description suggests.

    Revenue sounds like one number. Computing it requires four decisions that each have two defensible answers: subtract refunds or not, date by order creation or payment settlement, exclude internal test accounts or not, include tax or not. Nobody wrote them down, so every analyst decides independently.

    Four binary decisions produce sixteen definitions. The demo computes what each one actually reports.

    What independent analysts report for the same metric every combination of the edge-case decisions, evaluated
    POSSIBLE DEFINITIONS162 to the power of 4
    DISTINCT ANSWERS GIVEN0
    TWO ANALYSTS AGREE0%picked at random
    SPREAD0%

    Three results.

    1. The spread is 31%. The lowest defensible definition reports £26,545 and the highest £34,783, from identical underlying data. Not a rounding difference — a third of the number.
    2. All sixteen are numerically distinct. There is no clustering to hide behind: every combination of choices produces its own answer, so two analysts differing on any single decision differ in the reported figure.
    3. Twenty analysts produce eight different answers at a 75% adherence rate, with only four matching the house figure and a 16% chance that any two picked at random agree. The meeting where this surfaces is not a data quality incident; it is the normal operation of a platform with no shared definition.
    Why documentation does not fix it and a layer does

    Set governance to a documented convention and adherence rises, so the spread narrows — and it does not reach one answer, because a document is advisory and people misremember, join a new team, or copy an old query. Set it to a shared semantic layer and the count collapses to exactly one, because the definition is executed rather than described.

    This is the same distinction chapter 6 drew between documentation and a contract: the useful artefact is the one a machine applies.

    A semantic layer is a definition of metrics and dimensions, in version control, that BI tools and SQL clients query through rather than around. Given "revenue by region last quarter" it generates the SQL, applying the four decisions the same way every time. The tooling varies — dbt's semantic models, Cube, LookML, or a warehouse's own metric definitions — and what matters is the property rather than the product: one definition, in one place, that everything reads.

    Two honest caveats. A semantic layer only governs queries that go through it, so an analyst with warehouse credentials can always bypass it — which means adoption is a social problem with a technical assist, not a technical solution. And it adds a layer to debug when a number looks wrong. Both are real, and neither is close to the cost of a 31% spread.

    FAST ENOUGH

    Serving humans who click

    Dashboard workloads are not analyst workloads. Many small identical queries at once behaves completely differently from a few large distinct ones.

    A dashboard tile is a query. A dashboard with eight tiles refreshed by two hundred people every five minutes is a steady stream of concurrent queries, and the constraint is the queueing behaviour from chapter 1 — the same 1/(1-ρ) curve, now applied to your warehouse rather than to the database you were protecting.

    The numbers make the case for pre-aggregation better than any argument. Reading tiles from the raw fact table saturates the warehouse at 124 concurrent users. Reading the same tiles from a mart 500 times smaller raises the ceiling to 62,499. The mart costs one scheduled job and a fraction of the storage.

    Three levers, in order of return

    Pre-aggregate to the grain the dashboard actually shows. A tile displaying monthly revenue by region does not need row-level orders, and a rollup at that grain is orders of magnitude smaller. This is where nearly all the win is.

    Cache results. Two hundred people looking at the same tile is one query and 199 cache hits. An 80% hit rate cuts demand fivefold — utilisation from 1.60 to 0.32 in the model, which is the difference between saturated and comfortable. Most BI tools do this and most have it configured badly.

    Stop auto-refresh. A dashboard refreshing every minute against data updated hourly generates fifty-nine pointless query storms an hour. Set the refresh interval to the data's actual freshness, which chapter 2 taught you to state as a worst case.

    One structural point. If the workload is genuinely customer-facing — thousands of concurrent users, sub-second, per-tenant — a general-purpose warehouse is the wrong engine, and this is the case chapter 10's decision order would send elsewhere. Real-time OLAP stores such as ClickHouse, Druid or Pinot exist for exactly this shape, and the honest signal that you need one is that you are pre-aggregating aggressively and still saturating.

    BACK OUT AGAIN

    Reverse ETL

    Taking modelled data out of the warehouse and pushing it into the tools people work in. Genuinely useful, and it inverts a dependency you should notice.

    A support agent needs to know a customer's lifetime value while looking at a ticket. That number lives in the warehouse and the agent lives in Zendesk. Reverse ETL syncs it across, so the answer is where the work happens rather than in a dashboard the agent will not open.

    The value is real: it removes the copy-paste failure from the first section, and it makes warehouse modelling directly operational rather than merely informative. Three things to be deliberate about:

    The warehouse is now on a critical path
    A dashboard being stale is an annoyance. A CRM field being stale drives a sales conversation, and a wrong one drives a wrong conversation. Reverse ETL upgrades your freshness and correctness obligations without anyone signing off on it — chapter 22's SLA discussion should cover these paths explicitly.
    Writes need idempotency and rate limits
    You are now an API client of a third party, so chapter 4's rate-limit arithmetic applies in reverse, and a retry must not create duplicate records. Sync on a stable external id, upsert, and honour the vendor's limits.
    Loops are possible and unpleasant
    If the CRM is also a source you ingest, a field you write can flow back in, be transformed, and be written again. Mark warehouse-owned fields explicitly and never ingest them back.

    THE GOAL, HONESTLY

    Self-service

    The stated aim of most data platforms, and a term that hides a real trade.

    The motivation is chapter 1's third maturity stage: a central team becomes a ticket queue and the bottleneck for every question in the company. Self-service means consumers answer their own questions.

    The failure is that "give everyone SQL access to the warehouse" is self-service in the same way that giving everyone a compiler is software engineering. What you get is the 31% spread from the demo above, four hundred untracked models, and a warehouse bill nobody can attribute.

    Self-service that works has four things underneath it, and they are all things this unit has already covered:

  1. Well-modelled tables so the easy query is also the correct one — chapter 9. If getting the right answer requires knowing that you must filter is_test = false, self-service will produce wrong answers at scale.
  2. A semantic layer so shared metrics have one definition, as measured above.
  3. Discoverability — a catalogue with ownership, freshness and lineage, so a consumer can tell which of four similarly named tables to use. Chapter 21.
  4. Cost attribution so a badly written query has a visible owner, without which self-service and an unbounded bill are the same project.
    5. The honest trade

    Self-service moves work from the data team to the consumer, and consumers are not data engineers. You get faster answers and more wrong ones. That is frequently the right trade — a fast approximately-right answer often beats a slow exact one — but it should be a decision rather than a drift, and the four items above are what keep "more wrong ones" from becoming "unusable".

    The place not to make the trade is anything reported externally: financial reporting, regulatory returns, board numbers. Those should come through a governed path with one definition and an owner, and that is not a failure of self-service, it is knowing which questions are which.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Every underserved consumer routes around you, and each workaround becomes a second definition of the truth. The bar is convenience, not just speed.
    2. Build marts per business process, not per requester, or two teams end up with different revenue.
    3. Four binary decisions about one metric give sixteen definitions, and all sixteen are numerically distinct.
    4. The spread is 31% — £26,545 to £34,783 from identical data. Twenty analysts produce eight different answers, and two picked at random agree 16% of the time.
    5. Documentation narrows the spread; a semantic layer collapses it to one. Same distinction as chapter 6: the useful artefact is the one a machine applies.
    6. A semantic layer only governs queries that go through it, so adoption is social with a technical assist.
    7. Dashboard workloads are a concurrency problem, and it is chapter 1's queueing curve applied to your own warehouse.
    8. Raw fact tables saturate at 124 concurrent users; a mart 500× smaller reaches 62,499. One scheduled job buys three orders of magnitude.
    9. An 80% cache hit rate cuts utilisation from 1.60 to 0.32 — from saturated to comfortable.
    10. Set refresh intervals from actual data freshness. Refreshing every minute against hourly data is fifty-nine pointless query storms an hour.
    11. Reverse ETL puts the warehouse on a critical path and makes you an API client, with chapter 4's rate limits and a need for idempotent writes.
    12. Self-service needs modelled tables, a semantic layer, discoverability and cost attribution. Without them it is just SQL access, and it produces the 31% spread at scale.
