CHAPTER 21

Observability, quality and cost

Software observability answers "is the system up". Data observability has to answer something harder: the pipeline ran, every task succeeded, the dashboard rendered, and the number is wrong. Every signal in this chapter exists because green pipelines and correct data are different things.

    A DIFFERENT QUESTION

    What observability means here

    Application monitoring watches the machinery. Data observability has to watch the output, because the machinery can be perfectly healthy while producing nonsense.

    Take the failures this unit has measured. The cold rows left stale by a CDC handover in chapter 5: every job succeeded. The late data never reprocessed in chapter 12: the pipeline reported success every morning and captured 36% of events. The stale input from staggered cron in chapter 16: no task failed. The idle partition stalling a watermark in chapter 14: consuming, no lag, no errors, no output.

    In every one of those, a conventional monitoring stack was entirely green.

    What each layer of monitoring can and cannot tell you
    LayerAnswersBlind to
    InfrastructureAre the machines healthyEverything about the data
    Job monitoringDid the task exit zeroWhether it processed the right rows
    Data observabilityIs the output plausible and on timeWhether "plausible" is correct
    ReconciliationDoes it match an independent sourceErrors present in both
    The ordering that follows

    Each layer catches what the one above cannot, and chapter 20 measured the result: unit tests and schema checks together caught 3 of 16 failure modes, and the two highest-value additions were both comparisons rather than inspections. Observability is the production-time half of that argument, and the same conclusion applies — a signal that only looks at your own output can verify structure and cannot verify content.

    WHAT TO MEASURE

    Five signals

    Instrument these and you have detected most of what is detectable. Each maps to a failure this unit has already measured.

    Freshness
    How old is the newest row, per table. The single highest-value signal because it catches the most common failure: the pipeline did not run. Alert in time since expected, not time since last update — a nightly table is not stale at 09:00 and is very stale at 09:00 on Tuesday if it last loaded on Sunday.
    Volume
    Row count per partition against its recent history. Catches partial loads, torn writes (chapter 11) and duplicate retries (chapter 12). Use a band derived from the trailing distribution, and account for weekday seasonality or you will alert every Sunday.
    Schema
    Columns, types and nullability against what was there yesterday. Cheap, and it catches the loud upstream changes — which chapter 4 established are the safe ones, so this is a low-value signal that people over-index on because it is easy.
    Distribution
    Null rate, cardinality, mean, and the share of each category, per column. This is the one that catches silent drift: a new enum value falling into a default bucket, a currency changing units, the training-serving skew of chapter 18.
    Lineage
    Not a signal you alert on, but the structure that makes the other four usable. It answers "what caused this" and "what does this affect", and it is the subject of the next section.

    One practice worth more than any individual signal: record the observations, not just the alerts. A table of daily row counts, null rates and freshness per table costs almost nothing and is the difference between "this number looks odd" and "this number has been drifting since the fourteenth". Most of the value of observability is retrospective.

    THE STRUCTURE

    Lineage, and what it is actually worth

    Three distinct benefits, and they are worth very different amounts. The one usually advertised is the smallest.

    Lineage is the dependency graph made queryable: which tables feed this one, and which dashboards, models and reverse-ETL syncs depend on it. Chapter 1 introduced it as blast radius; here it is a working tool.

    Triage with and without lineage a 40-node dependency graph, one upstream root cause, propagation traced
    ALERTS FIRED0one per affected node
    INCIDENTS, GROUPED1by lineage root
    TIME TO ROOT CAUSE0
    CONSUMERS AFFECTED0knowable only with lineage

    The three benefits, honestly ranked by size:

    1. Alert grouping is the largest. A failure at the root fires an alert on every affected node — 21 of them in the worked example. With lineage they collapse to one incident. Chapter 20 measured what alert volume does to a team's ability to detect anything at all: grouping by lineage root is the single biggest reduction available.
    2. Knowing the blast radius is the second, and it is qualitative. "Which dashboards, models and CRM syncs are wrong right now?" is answered instantly with lineage and is unanswerable without it — you ask around and hope. That is not a speed improvement, it is the difference between being able to communicate during an incident and not.
    3. Faster root-cause search is the smallest, and it is real. Walking upstream with the graph visible takes 5 inspections where reading definitions to discover parents takes 11 — 20 minutes against 44 at four minutes each. Useful, roughly a 2× saving, and much less than the other two.
    Where to get lineage without a project

    Column-level lineage from a dedicated platform is genuinely valuable and is not where to start. Table-level lineage is nearly free if you use a transformation tool that already knows the dependency graph — dbt derives it from the ref() calls, and orchestrators know their own task edges. That covers the warehouse, which is where most incidents live.

    The gap is at the edges: ingestion into the warehouse, and BI on top of it. Extending the graph to source tables and dashboards is where the remaining work is, and it is worth doing before buying anything, because it is the part that makes the blast-radius question answerable.

    DEFINING GOOD

    The dimensions of data quality

    Six words that turn "the data is bad" into something you can measure and assign.

    Each dimension, how to measure it, and the failure it maps to in this unit
    DimensionQuestionMeasured bySeen in
    AccuracyDoes it match realityReconciliation against the sourceCDC handover, ch 5
    CompletenessIs everything hereRow counts, null rates, recompute-and-compareLate data, ch 12
    ConsistencyDo related things agreeReferential integrity, cross-table totalsMetric drift, ch 17
    TimelinessIs it recent enoughFreshness against the promiseScheduling, ch 2
    ValidityDoes it conform to its rulesType, range, enum and format checksContracts, ch 6
    UniquenessIs anything duplicatedDistinct count against row count at the grainRetries, ch 12
    Quality is relative to a use, not absolute

    A dataset that is 98% complete is excellent for a trend chart and unacceptable for a financial reconciliation. A four-hour freshness is fine for a weekly report and useless for fraud detection. So "is this data good?" has no answer without knowing what it is for.

    This is why quality belongs in the contract: the guarantee is per-consumer and stated as a number. "Complete within 0.1% by 07:00" is a quality definition. "High quality" is not.

    One measurement worth adding beyond the six: time to detection. Not whether a problem was caught, but how long it existed first — because a defect found in an hour and one found in three weeks have completely different costs, and only the second involves decisions already made. Tracking the distribution of detection times tells you where to add a check far better than counting incidents does.

    THE OTHER SIGNAL

    Cost, attributed

    Spend is extremely concentrated, which is good news: a small amount of attention covers most of the bill.

    Chapter 19 argued for attribution before optimisation. The reason is the shape of the distribution — query costs follow a heavy tail, so the interesting question is not "how do we spend less" but "which handful of things is the spend".

    Where the warehouse bill actually is 50,000 queries a month with a heavy-tailed cost distribution
    TOP 100 QUERIES0%
    TOP 1%0%500 queries
    BOTTOM HALF0%25,000 queries
    ACTIONABLE SPEND0

    At typical concentration, the top 100 queries of 50,000 are 72% of the bill and the bottom 25,000 are a rounding error. That has two direct consequences: reviewing the top hundred monthly is a short exercise covering most of the spend, and optimising anything outside the top few hundred is effort with no return.

    Untagged spend is unactionable regardless of size

    Move the untagged slider. Concentration tells you which queries cost money; attribution tells you whose they are. With 35% of spend carrying no owner, you can act on at most 65% of the bill however good your analysis is — the rest is a number with nobody to discuss it with.

    Which is why chapter 19's advice was to fail the plan on an untagged resource. Retrofitting attribution across a live account is a project nobody funds; enforcing it from the start costs nothing.

    Three things worth instrumenting alongside the total. Cost per pipeline run, so a change that triples a job's cost is visible in the pull request rather than the quarterly bill. Cost per dashboard, which is how chapter 17's auto-refresh problem becomes a conversation. And daily spend against the trailing average, because a monthly total tells you after the money is gone.

    FINDING THINGS

    Catalogues and discoverability

    The problem a catalogue solves is real. The reason most catalogues fail is also real and predictable.

    Past a few hundred tables, a consumer facing four similarly named candidates cannot tell which to use, and picking wrong produces chapter 17's metric spread. A catalogue answers: what is this, who owns it, where does it come from, how fresh is it, and is anyone using it.

    Catalogues fail for a consistent reason: anything requiring manual upkeep goes stale, and a stale catalogue is worse than none, because it is confidently wrong. So the design rule is that everything possible should be derived rather than entered.

    Derive automatically

    • Schema, types, partitioning
    • Lineage, from the transformation tool
    • Freshness and volume, from the observability signals
    • Usage — who queried this, how often — from query logs
    • Cost, from the same logs

    Ask a human for

    • What this table means, in a sentence
    • The owning team
    • Which columns carry personal data
    • Known caveats

    The right-hand list should be short enough that people actually complete it, and the highest-return item on it is usage — which is on the left, derived. Knowing that a table is queried by forty people daily and its similarly named neighbour by nobody in six months answers the "which one should I use" question more reliably than any description, and it costs one query against the logs.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Every silent failure in this unit happened with a green monitoring stack. Job monitoring answers whether the task exited zero, not whether it processed the right rows.
    2. Five signals: freshness, volume, schema, distribution, lineage. Freshness catches the most common failure; distribution catches the most expensive one.
    3. Alert on time since expected, not time since last update, and use bands with seasonality for volume.
    4. Schema checks are cheap, easy and low-value, because they catch the loud failures that would announce themselves anyway.
    5. Record observations, not just alerts. Most of the value of observability is retrospective.
    6. Lineage's biggest benefit is alert grouping — 21 alerts collapsing to 1 incident — which is the largest lever on chapter 20's fatigue problem.
    7. The second is blast radius, and it is qualitative: "which consumers are wrong right now" is unanswerable without lineage.
    8. Faster root-cause search is the smallest benefit — 20 minutes against 44 — and still worth having.
    9. Table-level lineage is nearly free from a transformation tool; the work is extending it to sources and dashboards.
    10. Quality is relative to a use. 98% complete is excellent for a trend and unacceptable for a reconciliation, which is why the guarantee belongs in a contract as a number.
    11. Track time to detection, not just incident count. An hour and three weeks are completely different costs.
    12. The top 100 of 50,000 queries are 72% of the bill. Reviewing them monthly covers most of the spend; optimising outside the top few hundred returns nothing.
    13. Untagged spend is unactionable at any size. Attribution tells you whose it is, which is the half that makes concentration useful.
    14. Derive everything a catalogue can derive. Usage from query logs answers "which table should I use" better than any description.
