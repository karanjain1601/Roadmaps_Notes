CHAPTER 23

Security, privacy and governance

Two results in this chapter are worth the whole of it. Three ordinary fields identify almost everyone uniquely, so "we removed the names" is not anonymisation. And deleting one person from a date-partitioned lake rewrites hundreds of gigabytes, which is why the right answer is usually not to delete at all.

    TWO WORDS

    Authentication and authorisation

    Consistently conflated, and the distinction decides where a bug lives.

    Authentication establishes who is making the request. Authorisation decides what they may do. Most data platforms get the first right because a cloud provider does it for them, and get the second wrong because it is theirs to design.

    Human identity
    Single sign-on through the corporate identity provider, so that access is revoked when someone leaves — which is the whole point. A warehouse with its own separate user list is a list of people who used to work here.
    Service identity
    Pipelines need credentials too, and long-lived static keys in a config file are the most common serious finding in a data platform audit. Prefer short-lived credentials issued to a workload identity, and if you must have a static secret, put it in a secret manager with rotation and an access log.
    Least privilege, per job
    An ingestion job needs write on one schema and read on nothing else. A transformation job needs read on staging and write on marts. Giving every pipeline the same broad role because it is easier means a bug in the smallest job can drop the largest table.
    The failure that keeps happening

    A developer needs to debug something, is granted a broad role temporarily, and the grant is never removed. Two years later a hundred people have production write access and nobody can say why any individual one does.

    The defence is structural rather than diligent: grants through groups rather than to individuals, membership expiring by default, and an automated report of who holds write access to production reviewed quarterly. Anything relying on somebody remembering to revoke will not work.

    CONTROLLING READS

    Access control that survives contact with analysts

    Four mechanisms, in increasing order of what they cost to maintain.

    Access control mechanisms and where each fits
    MechanismGranularityMaintenance costUse it for
    Schema and table grantsWhole tablesLowThe default. Separate raw, staging and marts, and grant most people marts only
    Column-levelIndividual columnsModerateHiding personal data in a table people otherwise need
    Row-levelIndividual rowsHighPer-tenant isolation, or regional restriction
    Dynamic maskingValue, by roleModerateSame column readable in full by one role and masked for another

    The practical advice is to start at the top and only descend when a real requirement forces it. Row-level security in particular is powerful and expensive: the policy is a predicate evaluated on every query, it interacts badly with caching, and a mistake in the predicate silently shows the wrong rows to the wrong people, which is worse than showing none.

    Two structural points that do more than any mechanism. Separate the layers by grant — if raw data is only readable by pipelines and analysts get marts, most access questions disappear because the sensitive columns never reach the layer people query. And make the default deny, so a new table is inaccessible until someone grants it, rather than visible until someone notices.

    THE MEASURED RESULT

    De-identification, and why removing names is not enough

    Identification does not require an identifier. It requires a combination of attributes rare enough to be unique, and that bar is much lower than intuition suggests.

    The classic demonstration is Latanya Sweeney's: a large majority of a population can be uniquely identified by ZIP code, date of birth and sex alone. None of those is an identifier, and a dataset containing all three with names removed is not anonymous in any meaningful sense — it can be joined against any other dataset sharing those fields.

    The demo generates a synthetic population and computes it directly. k-anonymity is the standard measure: the size of the smallest group sharing the same combination of quasi-identifiers. If k is 1, at least one person is unique and therefore identifiable.

    Re-identification risk under generalisation 50,000 synthetic people, equivalence classes computed directly

    Three results.

    1. Full postcode, exact date of birth and sex identifies 99.9% of people uniquely. A dataset with those three columns and no names is a dataset of named individuals, waiting for anyone with a second dataset to do the join.
    2. Generalising the date to a year still leaves 84.6% unique. Coarsening one field is not enough — the combination is what identifies, so you have to reduce the cardinality of the whole tuple.
    3. Area plus a five-year band, with sex dropped, reaches k = 59 and nobody unique. That is genuinely de-identified for these three fields, and it has also lost most of the analytical value of the location and age columns. That trade is unavoidable and should be made deliberately.
    k-anonymity is a floor, not a guarantee

    It only accounts for the quasi-identifiers you thought of. A dataset generalised on postcode and age can still be re-identified through a purchase history, a sequence of timestamps, or a device model — and high-dimensional behavioural data is essentially never anonymisable, because everyone's behaviour is unique. It also says nothing about attribute disclosure: if all 59 people in a group share the same diagnosis, knowing someone is in the group tells you their diagnosis, which is what l-diversity addresses.

    The practical conclusion is not to give up on de-identification but to stop treating it as a switch. It reduces risk by an amount you can measure and does not reach zero.

    The row worth noticing is hashing. An unsalted hash of an email address or a phone number is not pseudonymisation in any useful sense — the input space is small enough to enumerate, so anyone with the same hash function can recover the original. Hashing protects against casual inspection and not against an attacker.

    FINDING IT

    Knowing where the personal data is

    Every obligation in the next section presupposes that you can answer this, and most platforms cannot.

    You cannot protect, mask, restrict or delete what you cannot locate. So the first governance task is a map: which columns in which tables contain personal data, and of what kind.

  1. Classify at the column level, in the contract. Chapter 6 listed ownership and semantics as contract contents; a personal-data flag belongs there too, declared by the producer who actually knows.
  2. Scan to catch what was not declared. Pattern matching on values and names finds email addresses, card numbers and national identifiers reliably. It will not find a free-text notes column containing whatever an agent typed, which is where personal data most often hides.
  3. Propagate the classification through lineage. A column derived from a personal-data column is personal data. Chapter 21's lineage graph makes this automatic; without it, classification stops at the raw layer and every downstream copy is unlabelled.
  4. Treat free text as sensitive by default. A support ticket body will contain names, addresses and occasionally payment details, whatever the policy says.
  5. Record a lawful basis and a retention period per dataset, because both are needed to answer the questions in the next section and neither can be reconstructed later.
    6. The engineering consequence of ELT

    Chapter 12 chose ELT for a good reason: keeping raw data makes a logic error a re-run rather than an unrecoverable loss. The cost is that raw personal data now lands in your warehouse before any masking, so access control has to be enforced inside the platform rather than at its edge.

    That is a real obligation rather than a footnote, and it is why the layer separation in the access section matters: raw readable only by pipelines, marts readable by people, and the personal columns not present in marts unless someone needed them.

    THE OBLIGATIONS

    What the regulations require of a pipeline

    An engineering summary of the recurring requirements. This is not legal advice and I am not a lawyer — the specifics vary by jurisdiction and by how your organisation uses data, so treat this as what to build for and get the interpretation from counsel.

    Several regimes now impose obligations on data processing, and from a pipeline's perspective they overlap heavily. The recurring engineering requirements are these:

    Common obligations, and what each demands technically
    ObligationWhat it means for a platform
    Purpose limitationData collected for one purpose cannot be freely reused for another. Requires recording why each dataset exists.
    Data minimisationDo not collect or retain more than you need. Argues against the reflex to ingest every column.
    Retention limitsDelete when the purpose expires. Requires a retention policy per dataset and a job that enforces it.
    Access requestsProduce everything you hold on a person. Requires knowing where their data is — the previous section.
    Erasure requestsDelete everything you hold on a person. The subject of the demo below.
    PortabilityExport in a machine-readable format. Usually the easiest of these.
    Breach notificationReport within a short window. Requires access logs good enough to say what was exposed.
    Automated decision transparencyExplain decisions made about a person. Requires knowing which model version and features produced a given decision — chapter 18's logged feature vector.

    Erasure is the one that collides hardest with everything this unit has built. Chapter 7 established that objects are immutable, and chapter 8 that partitioning by ingestion date is the right default. Both are correct, and together they mean one person's rows are scattered across thousands of files that must each be rewritten whole.

    The cost of a single erasure request two years of daily partitions, 20 files a day of 128 MB
    FILES TOUCHED0
    REWRITTEN PER REQUEST0to delete one person
    PER MONTH0
    TIME PER REQUEST0at 400 GB an hour

    Deleting one person's rows rewrites roughly 203 GB, because their 1,620 affected files must each be rewritten whole to remove a handful of rows. At 500 requests a month that is 99 TB rewritten monthly to delete a rounding error's worth of data. And the cost is driven by how widely the rows are spread, not by how much data the person has — which is exactly backwards from the intuition.

    Crypto-shredding is the answer most platforms land on

    Encrypt each subject's sensitive fields with a per-subject key held in a key store. To erase, delete the key. The data remains on disk and is permanently unreadable, the operation is O(1), and it works identically for backups, exports and anything else you cannot practically rewrite.

    Two conditions. The key store must be authoritative and backed up separately — losing it erases everyone. And whether crypto-shredding satisfies a given regulator's definition of erasure is a legal question rather than an engineering one, so confirm it before relying on it. Where it is accepted, it converts an intractable rewrite into a key delete.

    Two lesser techniques worth knowing. Soft delete with filtering — marking a subject as erased and excluding them from every read — is not erasure and will not satisfy a regulator, though it is a reasonable first step while you build the real thing. And partitioning by subject makes deletion cheap and is almost always the wrong trade, because it destroys the ingestion-time clustering that chapter 8 measured as the source of most query pruning.

    MAKING IT STICK

    Governance that survives

    The reason governance programmes fail is the same reason catalogues fail: anything requiring sustained manual effort decays.

    The same principle that ran through chapter 6 and chapter 20 applies here in its strongest form: a rule that is enforced is worth ten rules that are documented. Governance works when it is a property of the pipeline rather than a policy about it.

    Enforce mechanically

    • Deny by default on new tables
    • Fail the build on an undeclared personal-data column
    • Retention jobs that actually delete, monitored like any other pipeline
    • Access grants that expire
    • Classification propagated through lineage

    Accept as human judgement

    • What a dataset means and why it exists
    • Whether a new use fits the original purpose
    • Which fields are genuinely needed
    • Whether a de-identification is sufficient for a given release

    One organisational note. Governance is frequently resisted because it arrives as an imposition from a team with no delivery responsibility — which is the same dynamic chapter 6 described for data contracts, and it has the same answer. Supply the mechanism rather than requesting the compliance: write the check, open the pull request, make it green. Adoption cost near zero beats a policy argued in the abstract.

    The audit question worth being ready for

    Sooner or later somebody will ask: who has accessed this table in the last ninety days, and what personal data does it contain? If you can answer both from query logs and a column classification, you are in good shape and the conversation is short. If you cannot, the answer is a project, and it always arrives at the least convenient moment.

    Both halves are cheap to build early and expensive to retrofit, which is the recurring shape of everything in this chapter.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Authentication is solved for you; authorisation is yours to design and is where the bugs are.
    2. Static long-lived service credentials are the most common serious audit finding. Short-lived workload identity, or a secret manager with rotation.
    3. Temporary grants become permanent. Grant through groups, expire membership by default, and review write access on a schedule.
    4. Separate the layers by grant. If raw is pipeline-only and people read marts, most access questions never arise.
    5. Full postcode, exact date of birth and sex identifies 99.9% of people uniquely. Removing names is not anonymisation.
    6. Generalising one field is not enough — postcode plus year of birth plus sex still leaves 84.6% unique. The combination identifies.
    7. Area plus a five-year band with sex dropped reaches k = 59 and nobody unique, at the cost of most of the analytical value of those columns.
    8. k-anonymity only covers the quasi-identifiers you thought of, and high-dimensional behavioural data is essentially never anonymisable.
    9. An unsalted hash of an email is not pseudonymisation. The input space is enumerable.
    10. Classify personal data at the column level and propagate it through lineage, or the classification stops at the raw layer.
    11. ELT lands raw personal data in the warehouse, so access control moves inside the platform rather than in front of it.
    12. Deleting one person rewrites about 203 GB across 1,620 files, and 500 requests a month is 99 TB — driven by how widely the rows are spread, not by how much data the person has.
    13. Crypto-shredding converts that into deleting one key, works for backups, and needs the key store treated as authoritative. Whether it satisfies a given regulator is a legal question.
    14. Enforce mechanically, judge humanly. A rule that fails a build is worth ten that are documented.

    Pick your largest table and try to answer, from tooling rather than memory, which of its columns contain personal data. The time that takes is your governance maturity.
