CHAPTER 03

Choosing technologies

The data tooling market is designed to make this decision look like a feature comparison. It almost never is. Nearly every choice you will make is decided by three numbers that do not appear on any vendor page: what it costs including the salaries, how long you are locked in, and how much it costs to leave.

    THE ACTUAL DECISION

    What decides it, and what does not

    Feature matrices are a distraction because at this point in the market's maturity, every serious tool in a category does the same things.

    Put the four leading cloud warehouses side by side and you will find columnar storage, separate compute and storage, ANSI-ish SQL, semi-structured support, time travel, and a Python client. Put four orchestrators side by side and you will find DAGs, retries, backfills, sensors and a web UI. The category has converged. The remaining differences are real but small, and they are almost never what determines whether your platform succeeds.

    What determines it:

    Total cost including people
    The licence or usage fee is usually the smaller half. Engineer time to build, then to keep running, is the larger half, and it is invisible because it is already in the payroll budget. The first demo makes this explicit and the result surprises most people.
    Team size and what they already know
    A three-person team cannot operate a self-managed Kafka cluster and also deliver anything. A tool your team already knows has a genuine, quantifiable advantage that "better" tools rarely overcome. This is not conservatism; it is arithmetic about a fixed number of hours.
    Speed of the first useful result
    Time from decision to a stakeholder seeing something they asked for. Platforms that take six months to first value tend to be cancelled at month five, regardless of how good month nine would have been.
    Reversibility
    Not "will we be locked in" — you will — but how much does it cost to change our mind in two years. This is purchasable at design time and extremely expensive to add later. The second demo prices it.
    Whether it is a differentiator
    Building something that is not core to your business is a decision to maintain it forever in exchange for nothing a competitor cannot buy. Building the thing that is core is how you win. Most data infrastructure is emphatically the former.
    The question that shortcuts most debates

    "If this decision turns out to be wrong in two years, what does fixing it cost?" A cheap-to-reverse decision deserves a fast, cheap process — pick something reasonable and move on. An expensive-to-reverse decision deserves weeks of care. Teams routinely invert this, agonising over an orchestrator they could swap in a fortnight while choosing a storage format in an afternoon.

    THE FIRST NUMBER

    Total cost, salaries included

    Open source has no licence fee. It is frequently the most expensive option available to a small team, and the model below shows exactly where the crossover sits.

    The mistake is comparing a vendor invoice against zero. The correct comparison puts three costs side by side for each option: infrastructure, the engineering months to get it working, and the engineering months per year to keep it working. That last one is the term people forget, and it is the one that compounds.

    At the default settings the managed option costs roughly a third of self-hosting and a seventh of building, and every bit of that gap is salary rather than infrastructure. Self-hosting genuinely is cheaper per terabyte — about a quarter the unit price — and that advantage is simply irrelevant until the volume is large enough for it to overcome fifteen engineer-months.

    Two second-order effects worth noticing in the model

    Raise the salary slider and the crossover moves up: expensive engineers make buying more attractive, which is the opposite of the intuition that expensive engineers should build things. And lengthen the horizon and the crossover moves down, because setup cost is paid once while the per-terabyte gap accrues forever. A five-year commitment justifies self-hosting at a considerably lower volume than a one-year one.

    Three honest caveats about the model. It assumes you can hire the people at all, which at small companies is often false and makes managed the only real option. It ignores the risk premium on a build, where the setup estimate is the one number people are worst at. And it treats engineer-months as fungible, when in practice the months spent operating infrastructure are months not spent on the modelling work in chapter 9 that actually determines whether anyone trusts the output.

    THE SECOND DECISION

    Build, buy, or adopt

    Three options, not two. The middle one — adopting a managed open-source service — is where most reasonable answers live.

    The four positions on the spectrum, with the case for each
    OptionExampleChoose it whenThe cost you are accepting
    Buy proprietary Snowflake, Databricks, Fivetran The category is not your differentiator and you want it working this month. Per-unit price, and an exit cost you should price now rather than later.
    Adopt managed open source Managed Airflow, managed Kafka, hosted Postgres Almost always the default. Open formats and a portable API, someone else on call. A premium over raw infrastructure, typically 20–50%, in exchange for the operations.
    Self-host open source Airflow on Kubernetes, Trino, self-run Kafka Volume is past the crossover, or a hard requirement rules out managed — data residency, air gap, a regulator. Permanent operational headcount. Not a one-off; a standing commitment.
    Build Your own ingestion framework or scheduler Genuinely nothing fits, or the thing itself is your product. You now maintain it forever, including the parts nobody remembers writing.

    The failure mode worth naming is the one that arrives disguised as pragmatism. Nobody decides to build an ingestion framework. Somebody writes a Python script to pull one API. Then a second, so a shared function appears. Then retry logic, then a config file, then scheduling, then a state store for watermarks. Eighteen months later there is a bespoke ingestion framework with one expert, no tests and no documentation, and it was never a decision anyone made.

    The tell that you have accidentally started building a product

    When a shared internal component acquires its own configuration format, it has become a product with users. Products need documentation, versioning, a deprecation policy and someone accountable. If you are not prepared to supply those, the honest move is to stop and adopt something — the alternative is not "we saved money", it is "we have an undocumented product with a bus factor of one".

    The counter-argument deserves a fair hearing, because it is sometimes right. Managed tools price by usage, and usage-priced tools have a habit of becoming shockingly expensive at exactly the moment you are most dependent on them. A team that has been through a surprise renewal will argue for control, and they are not being irrational — they are pricing a risk the model above does not include. The resolution is not to pick a side in the abstract but to make the exit cheap, which is the next section.

    THE THIRD NUMBER

    Lock-in and the price of leaving

    Lock-in is not a binary property of a vendor. It is a cost you can measure, and largely a cost you choose.

    Every meaningful tool locks you in somehow. The useful question is which of the four lock-in surfaces you are exposing, because they differ enormously in what they cost to unwind:

    Data lock-in

    Your data sits in a format only one engine reads. The most expensive kind, because the volume grows monotonically and egress fees are charged on the way out. Avoided by keeping data in open formats on storage you control.

    Dialect lock-in

    Transformations written against one engine's SQL extensions, UDFs and procedural language. Cost scales with the number of models, and it is the surface people underestimate most.

    Semantic lock-in

    Metric definitions living inside a BI tool rather than in your own layer. Migrating means rediscovering what every number meant, usually from people who have left.

    Operational lock-in

    Your runbooks, alerts, deployment and access model assume one product. Real but the cheapest to unwind, and the one teams worry about most.

    The portable posture is not a different vendor. It is the same vendor plus four habits: keep raw and modelled data in an open table format on your own object storage, write transformations in standard SQL through a tool that can retarget, keep metric definitions in your repository rather than the BI tool, and never let the only copy of anything live inside a product you do not control.

    Reversibility is bought with a small, continuous tax

    Each of those habits costs something. Open table formats are marginally slower than a vendor's native storage. Standard SQL means declining some genuinely convenient extensions. The tax is real, usually in the region of a few percent of performance and a bit of convenience — and in the model above it turns a six-figure exit into a five-figure one. Whether that trade is worth it depends on how likely you think the exit is, which is a business question rather than a technical one.

    WHAT TO BET ON

    Immutable and transitory technologies

    Some things in this field have been true for forty years. Others will not survive this funding cycle. Commit accordingly.

    A useful distinction: immutable technologies are the ones whose absence would be unimaginable in a decade, and transitory ones are everything else. The distinction matters because it tells you where to invest learning time and where to keep an escape hatch.

    Where to spend commitment, and where to stay loose
    Reliably durableWhy it persistsGenuinely uncertain
    SQL Fifty years old, and every new engine that tried to replace it has ended up adding a SQL interface. Any specific query engine
    Object storage The economics are structural, not a product decision. Cheap, durable, infinitely scalable, everything reads it. Any specific warehouse product
    Columnar file formats Parquet and ORC encode a physical fact about how analytical scans work. See chapter 8. Which open table format wins
    The append-only log The idea behind Kafka, database replication and event sourcing alike. Older than all of them. Any specific streaming platform
    Dimensional modelling It describes how people ask questions, which has not changed with hardware. Whichever modelling framework is fashionable
    Batch and stream as a duality A physical trade between latency and cost, not a technology. Whether the two ever truly unify
    Unix, networking, Git The substrate. Every tool above is operated through them. Any specific orchestrator or catalogue

    The practical consequence is a rule about where to be opinionated. Be extremely conservative about the left column — learn it properly, build on it, assume it will outlast your career. Be cheerfully disposable about the right — pick the one with the best documentation and community today, wrap it thinly enough that swapping it is a fortnight rather than a quarter, and do not build an identity around it.

    The hype-cycle question

    Every eighteen months a category is declared dead by a new one. Warehouses were going to kill lakes, lakes were going to kill warehouses, streaming was going to kill batch, the mesh was going to kill central platforms. In each case the honest outcome was absorption rather than replacement: the new idea became a feature of the old thing. The reliable test is whether the new approach changes a physical constraint — the cost of a byte, the speed of a network, the latency of a disk — or merely rearranges responsibilities. The first kind lasts.

    RECOGNISABLE MISTAKES

    Six ways this goes wrong

    Each of these is defensible in the moment, which is why they are common.

    Resume-driven architecture
    Choosing the tool that is good for the engineer's next job rather than this company's next year. Diagnosed by asking what problem the tool solves that the boring option does not, and listening for whether the answer describes a problem you actually have.
    Building for a scale you do not have
    Distributed compute for data that fits in memory on one machine. A modern server holds hundreds of gigabytes of RAM; a very large fraction of "big data" workloads are a single-node problem being solved with a cluster, at roughly ten times the complexity. Measure before you distribute.
    Buying a platform to avoid a modelling decision
    No product will decide what revenue means. Teams sometimes buy a catalogue, a semantic layer or a governance suite in the hope that the organisational problem from chapter 1 becomes a purchasing problem. It does not.
    Optimising unit price while ignoring the operating cost
    The whole point of the first demo. A 75% saving per terabyte that costs fifteen engineer-months is not a saving at any volume below the crossover.
    Adopting a pattern without its prerequisites
    Data mesh without a platform team, streaming without operational maturity, self-service without contracts or lineage. The pattern was a response to problems a mature organisation had; adopting it earlier imports the overhead and none of the benefit.
    Never revisiting
    The rarer, opposite failure. A choice correct at 5 TB is re-justified indefinitely at 500 TB because it was decided once. Put a date on significant decisions and actually look at them again — the crossover in the first demo moves as your volume grows, and nothing alerts you when you cross it.

    A lightweight practice that addresses most of these: write the decision down. A page, at the time, covering what was decided, what was rejected, what would have to be true to change our mind, and roughly what reversal would cost. It takes an hour and it is the only reliable defence against the sixth failure, because it converts a forgotten assumption into something with a review date.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Feature comparisons rarely decide anything. Categories have converged. Cost including salary, team capacity, time to first value, reversibility and whether it differentiates you are what actually decide.
    2. Open source has no licence fee and is often the most expensive option. At 50 TB a month over three years the model puts managed at roughly a third the cost of self-hosting, entirely because of fifteen engineer-months.
    3. The crossover is a real number you can compute. On the default figures it sits near 250 TB a month — and it moves up with salaries and down with a longer horizon.
    4. There are three options, not two. Managed open source is the usual right answer: portable formats and API, somebody else on call.
    5. Nobody decides to build a framework. It accretes from scripts. The tell is a shared component acquiring its own config format, at which point you have a product with users and no documentation.
    6. Lock-in has four surfaces — data, dialect, semantics, operations — and they cost wildly different amounts to unwind. Data lock-in is the expensive one; operational lock-in is the one people worry about.
    7. Reversibility is purchasable for a few percent of performance and some convenience, and in the model it turns a six-figure exit into a five-figure one.
    8. Commit hard to SQL, object storage, columnar formats, logs and dimensional modelling. Stay loose about every specific product implementing them.
    9. Write decisions down with a review date. The choice that was right at 5 TB stops being right, and nothing will tell you when.

    Take the largest tool your team runs and estimate its engineer-months per year honestly, including on-call and upgrades. Compare that against the managed equivalent's list price. The answer is frequently uncomfortable in one direction or the other.
