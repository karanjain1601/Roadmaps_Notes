Chapter 01 · The job

What the work actually is

A request arrives, a query gets written, a number goes back. That loop is the visible part of the job and the part with the least value in it, and understanding why is the difference between an analyst people rely on and one they route around.

Four kinds of question

The same topic, asked four ways Each needs different data, different methods, and gives a different kind of answer.

Most requests are phrased as diagnostic and answered as descriptive

Someone asks why churn went up. What comes back is a table of churn by segment, by plan, by tenure — which is descriptive, and it is a genuinely useful step. But it does not answer the question, and if it is handed over as though it does, the recipient will pick whichever row moved most and treat that as the cause. That gap is where analysts lose credibility most often, because the failure is invisible: everyone gets a number, everyone acts, and nobody notices for a quarter that the number never supported the conclusion drawn from it. The fix is not more sophisticated analysis. It is saying which of the four questions you are answering, out loud, in the first line of the reply.

the four failure modes
1. THE ORDER-TAKER. Requests arrive, numbers go out, nobody asks what the
   number is for. Output is high and influence is nil, and the role is the
   first thing automated away by a self-serve tool.
   -> ask what decision the number feeds. Every time. Chapter 2 is this.

2. THE PERFECTIONIST. Three weeks on an analysis whose decision needed an
   answer on Tuesday, and needed only to know the direction.
   -> ask how precise the answer has to be, and how soon. Frequently
      "roughly, today" beats "exactly, next month", and knowing which you
      are being asked for is part of the specification.

3. THE UNCRITICAL PIPE. The query ran, the number came out, it went into a
   deck. Nobody checked whether the column means what its name suggests,
   whether the join duplicated rows, or whether the two periods being
   compared are comparable.
   -> chapters 3, 4 and 5 exist because this is the commonest way a wrong
      number reaches a decision.

4. THE UNHEARD EXPERT. The analysis is right, thorough, caveated, and
   twelve slides long, and the decision was made from a different number
   somebody quoted in a meeting.
   -> lead with the answer. Carry the uncertainty in one sentence, not
      five. Chapter 18 is this.

THE PATTERN ACROSS ALL FOUR: the technical work is not the constraint. Two
of these are failures of scoping, one of scepticism, one of communication,
and none is solved by a better model.
"Analyst" describes at least three different jobs

The title spans work that shares a name and little else, and the mismatch between what someone was hired for and what the organisation wants is a common source of a bad first year. Product analytics is experiments, funnels, cohorts and instrumentation, sitting close to engineering and product, and it lives in chapters 13–15. Business intelligence is definitions, reporting, dashboards and stakeholder work, closer to finance and operations, and it lives in chapters 16–17. Analytics engineering is modelling data so that other people's analyses are correct — transformation, testing, the semantic layer — and it is closer to the Data Engineer roadmap than to this one. Ask which of the three a role actually is, because the daily work, the tools and the skills that get you promoted differ substantially between them.

Two things about that allocation.

THE ANALYSIS IS THE SMALLEST BLOCK, and it is the one every course
teaches. That is not an argument against learning it — you cannot skip it
— but it explains why competent-on-paper analysts struggle in practice.
The binding constraints are upstream and downstream of the part that was
taught.

THE CHECK STEP IS THE ONE THAT GETS CUT. It is also the cheapest insurance
available, and it is a short list:
  * does the total reconcile against a number somebody already trusts?
  * did the row count change when it should not have?
  * is the effect still there if I exclude the largest contributor?
  * would a different reasonable choice of window, filter or null handling
    change the direction of the answer?
  * has anything about how this data is collected changed in the period?

If the answer to the last one is unknown, find out before presenting.
Instrumentation changes explain more surprising findings than real
behavioural changes do (chapter 3).
The most valuable sentence you can say early

"What would you do differently depending on the answer?" It sounds like a stalling question and it is the fastest route to a useful analysis. Three things come out of it. If the honest answer is "nothing", you have saved days and can say so kindly — the request was for reassurance or for a slide, both legitimate, and both much cheaper to satisfy. If the answer is a specific decision, it tells you which number you need and how precise it has to be, which usually collapses a vague request into something answerable in an afternoon. And if the person cannot answer, the conversation you are now having is the one worth having, because the request was a proxy for a question nobody had articulated yet. That is chapter 2, and it is the highest-leverage skill in the role.

The short version

  1. Four kinds of question — descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, prescriptive — needing different data and giving different answers.
  2. Most requests are diagnostic and get answered descriptively. Say which one you are answering, in the first line.
  3. Pulling numbers is the lowest-value task. Reframing the question and designing the measurement are the highest.
  4. Saying an effect is not real is valuable and wins no credit. Do it anyway.
  5. The four failure modes are scoping, scoping, scepticism and communication. None is fixed by a better model.
  6. "Analyst" is three jobs. Product analytics, business intelligence and analytics engineering differ substantially.
  7. The analysis is the smallest block of the work and the one every course teaches.
  8. Ask what would change depending on the answer. The fastest route to an analysis worth doing.
