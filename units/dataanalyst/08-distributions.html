Distributions

Looking at the shape before computing anything takes thirty seconds and determines which methods are available to you. Skipping it is how a summary table hides two distinct populations, or a significance test gets applied to data that violates its assumptions.

Look at the histogram before computing a single summary

Two properties of your data are invisible in a summary table and decisive for everything that follows. Bimodality — a mean and a median that agree tell you nothing about whether the data is one population or two, and if it is two, every single-number summary describes nobody (chapter 7). And the shape of the tail — a summary table shows you the standard deviation, and it does not show you whether that number means anything, which depends on whether the distribution has the kind of tail for which variance is a stable quantity. Both are obvious in a histogram and both are hidden in a table of statistics. The cost of looking is one line of code, and the failure mode of not looking is that the entire analysis proceeds on a wrong premise.

the log transform, and when it helps
Taking logs of a right-skewed variable often makes it roughly symmetric,
which unlocks the methods that assume symmetry. It is the most useful
single transformation in analytics.

WHEN IT WORKS WELL
  * the variable is positive and multiplicative in nature — revenue,
    size, duration, counts of things that grow proportionally
  * differences that matter are RELATIVE. A £10 increase means something
    different at £20 than at £2,000, and logs encode that

WHEN IT DOES NOT
  * zeros are present. log(0) is undefined. log1p (log of 1+x) is the
    usual workaround and it changes the meaning near zero
  * negatives are present — no transform of this family applies
  * the skew comes from two populations rather than from one skewed one.
    Logs will not fix bimodality, and a log-scaled bimodal distribution
    is still bimodal

THE COST, AND IT IS THE PART PEOPLE FORGET: your results are now in log
units, and they do not transform back cleanly.

  the mean of the logs, exponentiated, is the GEOMETRIC mean of the
  original values — which is close to the median for a lognormal
  variable, and is NOT the arithmetic mean

So a model fitted on logs predicts something closer to a typical value
than to a total. If somebody needs to multiply your figure by a count to
get a total, the log-derived number is the wrong one, and by a
systematic margin rather than a rounding error.

A PRACTICAL RULE: analyse in logs when the question is about typical
behaviour or proportional change; analyse in raw units when the question
is about totals (chapter 7). And say which you did, because "average
revenue" computed two ways gives two different numbers and both are
defensible.
Normality tests are not the right instrument

Formal tests of normality answer a question you do not have. On a small sample they lack the power to detect real departures, so they pass; on a large sample they detect trivial departures, so they fail — meaning the result tracks your sample size more than your data. What actually matters is not whether the data is normal but whether the departure is large enough to affect the method you were going to use, and that is a judgement about magnitude which a p-value does not express. Look at a histogram and a Q-Q plot instead: they show you the shape and where it departs, which is the information you need. And remember what most methods actually require — many procedures assume the sampling distribution of the *estimate* is approximately normal, not that the data is, and those are very different requirements (chapter 10).

The four charts worth making every time

Before any analysis of a numeric variable, spend two minutes on these. A histogram, for shape, modes and gaps. A box plot or the percentile set, for the centre and the extremes. The variable plotted against time, because a distribution that looks stable in aggregate can be two different distributions before and after a change (chapter 3). And the same histogram split by your most important dimension — segment, plan, region — because a bimodal overall distribution frequently resolves into two clean unimodal ones once you split by the thing that distinguishes them, and that split is the finding. That fourth chart is the one people skip and the one most likely to change what you conclude.

The short version

  1. Bimodality and tail shape are invisible in a summary table and decisive for everything after it.
  2. Most business quantities are right-skewed, because they are bounded below at zero and unbounded above.
  3. Heavy tails make variance unstable. The mean jumps between samples, and the tail is usually the finding.
  4. Logs help when change is proportional, and they put your results in units that do not transform back cleanly.
  5. Exponentiating a mean of logs gives the geometric mean, not the arithmetic one. Wrong for totals.
  6. Logs do not fix bimodality. Split the populations instead.
  7. Normality tests track sample size more than data. Look at the shape and judge the magnitude.
  8. Split the histogram by your key dimension. The chart people skip, and the one most likely to change the conclusion.
