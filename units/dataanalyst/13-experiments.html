A/B tests and experiments

Randomisation is the only tool that settles a causal question, which makes an experiment worth a great deal more than any observational analysis. It also makes the failures more damaging, because a broken experiment carries the authority of one that worked.

Do the power calculation before the test, not after it

The most common experiment failure is not a subtle bias — it is running a test that never had a chance of detecting the effect it was looking for, then reporting "no significant difference" and killing a good idea. The calculation takes two minutes and answers the only question that matters at the design stage: given our traffic, what is the smallest effect we could detect in a reasonable time? If the answer is "a 40% improvement" and nobody expects more than 5%, the test should not be run — and that is a valuable finding, because it redirects effort toward a change large enough to measure, a longer run, or a more sensitive metric. Discovering it afterwards costs you the traffic, the time, and usually the idea.

sample ratio mismatch deserves its own note
You split traffic 50/50 and the arms come back 50.8% / 49.2%. With large
numbers that difference is far outside what chance produces, and it means
the randomisation did not work as intended.

WHY IT MATTERS SO MUCH: whatever caused the imbalance probably also
caused an imbalance in WHO ended up in each arm — and if the arms contain
different kinds of user, the comparison is no longer an experiment. It is
an observational comparison with extra steps.

COMMON CAUSES, and none of them is exotic:
  * the variant fails to load for some browsers, so those users are
    counted in one arm and not the other
  * a redirect drops slow connections, biasing one arm toward fast ones
  * bot filtering applied after assignment removes bots unevenly
  * the tracking event fires later in one variant, so users who leave
    quickly are missing from it
  * assignment happens before a filter that is applied inconsistently

WHAT TO DO: treat SRM as invalidating, not as a caveat. Investigate the
cause, fix it, rerun. A test with SRM and a positive result is the most
dangerous artefact in experimentation, because it looks like a win.

THE RELATED CHECK — an A/A TEST. Run the identical experience in both
arms and confirm you find nothing. It validates the whole pipeline:
assignment, logging, the metric definition and the analysis. Doing this
once when the platform is built, and after any change to it, catches
problems that no amount of care in a single experiment will.
Two weeks minimum, and always whole weeks

Behaviour varies by day of the week in almost every product — weekday and weekend users differ in who they are and what they do — so a test running Tuesday to Sunday weights the weekend differently in each arm's exposure and picks up day-of-week composition rather than the effect. Run in complete weekly cycles. The second reason for a two-week minimum is novelty and primacy: existing users react to a change because it is a change, so a new interface can lift engagement in week one purely through curiosity, or depress it through disruption, and both fade. A one-week test measures the reaction to novelty and reports it as the effect of the feature. If the effect is still there in week two and is a similar size, you are measuring the thing you wanted.

Pre-declare the guardrails, not just the primary metric

Every experiment should name one primary metric — the thing it is trying to move, decided before the test — and a short list of guardrails that must not get worse: revenue, retention, page load time, support contacts, error rate, unsubscribes. This does two things at once. It prevents the metric-shopping problem from chapter 11, where a test that failed on conversion gets reported as a win on time-on-page. And it catches the genuinely dangerous outcome, which is a change that improves the metric it targeted while damaging something nobody was watching — more aggressive notifications lifting engagement and raising unsubscribes, a cheaper shipping option lifting conversion and cutting margin. The guardrails do not need to reach significance to matter; a consistent adverse movement in one is a reason to look harder before shipping.

The short version

  1. Do the power calculation first. Most tests that found nothing were never able to find anything.
  2. Halving the detectable effect quadruples the sample. Precision is expensive in a specific, calculable way.
  3. Peeking inflates the false positive rate substantially — roughly four times with continuous monitoring.
  4. Sample ratio mismatch invalidates a test. Treat it as fatal, not as a caveat, and check it automatically.
  5. Run an A/A test when the platform is built. It validates assignment, logging, metric and analysis at once.
  6. Two weeks minimum, in whole weeks, for day-of-week composition and to let novelty fade.
  7. Pre-declare a primary metric and guardrails. This blocks metric-shopping and catches the harm nobody watched.
  8. Segment findings are hypotheses. Confirm on fresh data before acting on them.
