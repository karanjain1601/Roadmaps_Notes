Metrics and definitions

Three teams report active users and give three different numbers. Nobody is wrong, nobody is lying, and the meeting is spent reconciling arithmetic instead of making a decision — which is the most preventable recurring waste in analytics.

The definition matters more than the measurement

Four ordinary choices — what counts as activity, over what window, how many times, counted at what unit — produce a spread of several times between the largest and smallest defensible answer. Every one of those numbers is correct, and any two teams making different choices will disagree permanently without either being wrong. This is why definitional work is not bureaucracy: it is the highest-leverage thing an analyst can do for an organisation, because a single agreed definition ends a recurring argument that otherwise consumes part of every review meeting forever. It is also why "the numbers don't match" is almost never a data quality problem. Two systems computing different definitions correctly will never agree, and no amount of pipeline debugging will make them.

a metric definition worth writing down
METRIC        Monthly Active Accounts (MAA)
OWNER         Analytics, reviewed with Product quarterly

DEFINITION    An account is active in a calendar month if at least one
              user on that account performed a core action — creating,
              editing or sharing a document — on at least two distinct
              days in that month.

GRAIN         One row per account per month.
SOURCE        events.core_actions, joined to dim_account.

INCLUDES      Paid and trial accounts.
EXCLUDES      Internal staff accounts (domain allowlist), accounts flagged
              as test, and traffic matching the bot ruleset.

AS OF         Account attributes as of the last day of the month, not as
              of today. Historic figures therefore do not change when an
              account is reassigned.

FINALITY      Provisional for 3 days after month end; final thereafter.
              Provisional figures are marked in every report.

WHY THIS      Two distinct days rather than one, because single-day
              activity does not predict retention; the two-day threshold
              is where the retention curve separates.

NOT THIS      Not sessions, not logins, not page views. Not the same as
              Finance's "billable accounts", which counts contracted
              seats — expect MAA to run 15-25% below that figure.

THE LAST TWO SECTIONS DO THE MOST WORK. "Why this" stops the threshold
being relitigated every quarter by someone who was not in the room. "Not
this" pre-empts the reconciliation meeting by naming the other number and
saying how far apart they should be — because the two will be compared,
and it is better to publish the expected gap than to explain it later.
A metric that becomes a target stops measuring what it measured

Once a number is used to evaluate people, behaviour adapts to the number rather than to the thing it was standing in for. Support teams measured on ticket closure close tickets faster and reopen rates rise. Sales measured on pipeline create pipeline. Teams measured on weekly active users find reasons to send notifications. None of this requires anyone to act in bad faith — it is the predictable result of making one measurable proxy stand for a complex goal, and the people responding are doing what they were asked. The practical defences are to pair every target metric with a guardrail that would move in the opposite direction if it were being gamed, to change which metrics are targets occasionally, and — most useful — to keep a clear distinction between metrics used for understanding and metrics used for evaluation. The second set should be short, and everything in it should be assumed to be under pressure.

One definition, one place, everything downstream

The durable fix for definitional drift is not a document, because documents are not enforced. It is to define each metric once in code — a dbt model, a semantic layer, a shared view — and to make every dashboard, report and analysis read from that rather than reimplementing the logic. Then a definition change happens in one place and propagates, and two teams cannot silently diverge because there is only one implementation to diverge from. The document still matters for the reasoning, the exclusions and the "not this" section, and it should live next to the code rather than in a wiki nobody opens. The practical test of whether you have this: if someone asks to change how trials are counted, is that one edit and a rebuild, or is it an unknown number of dashboards to find? The second answer means the definitions live in the dashboards, and they will drift.

The short version

  1. Four ordinary choices produce several times' spread in a headline number, all of them correct.
  2. "The numbers don't match" is usually not a data quality problem. Two correct definitions will never agree.
  3. Pin down event, window, threshold, unit, inclusions, as-of date and finality. Missing any one causes drift.
  4. Write down why the threshold is what it is, or it gets relitigated every quarter.
  5. Name the other number and the expected gap. It will be compared; publish the difference in advance.
  6. A metric used as a target stops measuring what it measured, without anyone acting in bad faith.
  7. Separate metrics for understanding from metrics for evaluation, and keep the second set short.
  8. Define once in code, read everywhere. Documents are not enforced; a shared model is.
