Asking an answerable question

"Why are sales down?" is not a question the data can settle. Converting it into one that can is the highest-leverage thing an analyst does, and the conversion is a specific, learnable procedure rather than a knack.

The five questions

Every vague request yields to the same short interrogation, and it takes about ten minutes. One: what decision does this feed, and what would you do differently depending on the answer? Two: which numbers exactly — what population, what event, what unit of analysis? Three: compared with what — which baseline, which period, which group? Four: how precise does it need to be, and by when? Five: what answer would surprise you? That last one is the most underused and often the most revealing: it surfaces the requester's existing belief, which tells you what will be believed, what will be argued with, and what the analysis is really for. If the honest answer is "nothing would surprise me", you are being asked to confirm something, which is a legitimate request and a much cheaper one.

the specification to write back
Short, sent before you start, and it prevents most rework.

  QUESTION      Did the North region's invoiced revenue, net of credit
                notes, fall in Q3 against Q3 last year, and which accounts
                account for the difference?

  DECISION      Whether to reassign two account managers to North before
                the Q4 planning meeting on the 14th.

  POPULATION    All accounts with North as their billing region as of the
                period start. Excludes accounts created mid-quarter.

  MEASURE       Invoiced revenue net of credit notes, from the finance
                ledger, not the CRM's opportunity value.

  COMPARISON    Q3 this year against Q3 last year. Also shown against
                target, because both were asked about and they differ.

  GRAIN         One row per account.

  PRECISION     Direction and rough magnitude. Attribution to the top ten
                accounts is enough; a full decomposition is not needed.

  BY WHEN       The 12th.

  ASSUMPTIONS   Region assignment changed in June; accounts reassigned
                then are counted under their new region throughout, which
                moves roughly £180k between regions. Flagged because it
                affects the comparison.

  NOT COVERED   Why the accounts did not reorder. That is a diagnostic
                question needing account-team input, and is a separate
                piece of work.

THE LAST TWO SECTIONS ARE THE VALUABLE ONES. Assumptions is where a
disagreement gets caught before it becomes a rebuttal in a meeting. Not
covered is what stops the analysis from being expected to answer a question
it never addressed — which is chapter 1's descriptive-answers-diagnostic-
question problem, headed off in advance.
The request is often a solution, not a question

"Can you pull a list of customers who used feature X last month?" is a request for an artefact, and somewhere behind it is a question that produced it — probably about targeting, or about whether the feature matters. If you deliver the list, you have answered the request and possibly not the question, and the list may be the wrong instrument for it. The move is to ask what they plan to do with it, phrased as curiosity rather than obstruction: "happy to — what are you using it for, in case there's a better cut?" Frequently the answer reveals that the useful list is different (customers who used it and then churned), or that a number would serve better than a list, or that the underlying question is causal and a list cannot address it at all. This is not second-guessing the requester; it is the difference between a service desk and a collaborator.

When to push back

four requests worth declining or reshaping
1. THE DATA CANNOT ANSWER IT. Not "it would be hard" — the information was
   never collected, or the design does not support the claim. A causal
   question with no experiment and no plausible identification strategy is
   frequently in this category (chapter 12).
   -> say what CAN be answered, and what would need collecting to answer
      the original. That second half is the valuable part, and it is how
      instrumentation gets improved.

2. THE ANSWER WOULD NOT CHANGE ANYTHING. Established in question one. Say
   so gently and offer the cheap version — most such requests are served
   by a number in a message rather than an analysis.
   -> and note when the real need is a slide for a meeting. That is a
      legitimate need with a much smaller scope.

3. THE CONCLUSION IS ALREADY DECIDED. "Can you find the data that shows
   the campaign worked?" This is a request to support a position, and
   complying quietly is how an analytics function loses its usefulness.
   -> reframe to the neutral version: "I can look at what the campaign
      did — worth agreeing what we'd count as it having worked before I
      look?" Agreeing the criterion in advance is the whole defence, and
      it is much easier to propose before the numbers exist.

4. THE PRECISION ASKED FOR IS NOT AVAILABLE. Someone wants revenue impact
   to the pound from data that supports a range of plus or minus 20%.
   -> give the range and say what drives the width. A false point
      estimate is worse than an honest interval, because decisions get
      made on the digits.

WHAT PUSHING BACK IS NOT: refusing work, or being the person who explains
why everything is impossible. Every one of these responses includes an
alternative that is deliverable. The pattern is always "not that, and here
is what I can do, and here is what it would take to do the original".
Agree the success criterion before you look

For any question about whether something worked, settle what would count as working before computing anything, and write it down. Which metric, which comparison, and how large a change would be meaningful rather than noise. This costs one exchange and it removes the most corrosive dynamic in analytics: the moving target, where a result is met with "well, the real test is X", and then X is met with "but Y", until something favourable appears. That dynamic is rarely dishonest — people genuinely think of better tests after seeing results — and it is indistinguishable from motivated reasoning after the fact, including to the person doing it. A criterion agreed in advance turns the conversation into one about a finding rather than one about the analysis, which is where it should be. It is also the same discipline that makes an experiment valid (chapter 13).

The short version

  1. Five questions convert any vague request: what decision, which numbers, compared with what, how precise, what would surprise you.
  2. "What would surprise you?" is the most revealing. It surfaces the existing belief and what the analysis is really for.
  3. Words like down, best, working and engaged hide the whole specification. None of them means anything on its own.
  4. Write the spec back before starting. The assumptions and not-covered sections are the valuable ones.
  5. The request is often a solution. Ask what it is for — the useful cut is frequently different.
  6. Some questions the data cannot answer. Say what would need collecting; that is how instrumentation improves.
  7. Reframe requests that have already decided the conclusion. Agree the criterion first, neutrally.
  8. Settle the success criterion before you look. It removes the moving target, which is rarely dishonest and always corrosive.
