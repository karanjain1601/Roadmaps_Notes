Summaries that do not mislead

The average is the default answer to "how much" and it is the wrong number more often than most people realise — not because it is computed incorrectly, but because for a great deal of business data it describes almost none of the observations.

The mean answers a question about totals; the median answers one about members

This is the distinction that resolves most arguments about which to use. The mean is the total divided by the count, so it is the right number whenever the question is really about the aggregate — capacity planning, revenue forecasting, cost allocation, anything you will multiply back up. The median is the value in the middle, so it is the right number whenever the question is about a typical member — what a customer looks like, what a user experiences, what to expect from the next case. For right-skewed data these two questions have very different answers, and reporting the mean to a question about typical members is the most common quantitative misstatement in business. "Our average customer spends £340" when the median is £95 and 70% spend under £150 is not false; it is an answer to a question nobody asked.

why percentiles are the best default
Instead of one number, report five: p10, p25, p50, p75, p90. Frequently
also p99 where the tail matters.

  revenue per account
    p10      £12
    p25      £41
    p50      £95        <- the median
    p75      £280
    p90      £890
    p99    £8,400
    mean     £340       <- above the 75th percentile

THREE THINGS THIS DOES that no single number can.

  IT SHOWS THE SHAPE. A reader can see immediately that this is skewed,
  because the gaps grow as you go up. No statistical literacy required.

  IT ANSWERS SEVERAL QUESTIONS AT ONCE. Typical member, the long tail, the
  bottom end — each is a different decision, and one number serves one of
  them at most.

  IT MAKES THE MEAN'S POSITION VISIBLE. When the mean sits above the 75th
  percentile, everyone in the room can see that "average" is not
  describing a typical account, without anyone having to argue about it.

AND FOR LATENCY, RESPONSE TIMES OR ANY USER-EXPERIENCE MEASURE, the tail
IS the finding. Mean response time is close to meaningless: nobody
experiences the mean, and the 5% having a bad time are the 5% who
complain, churn, or file the ticket. Report p50 and p95 or p99, always.

  "Average page load 1.2s" — and p95 is 9s, so one visitor in twenty
  waits nine seconds, which is the number that explains your bounce rate.
Never average an average

Three regional conversion rates of 10%, 20% and 30% do not average to 20% unless the regions are the same size. If the 30% region has fifty visitors and the 10% region has fifty thousand, the overall rate is close to 10%. The mean of a set of ratios is not the ratio of the totals, and confusing them is the most frequent arithmetic error in reporting — it appears in dashboards that average a per-day rate across days, in decks that average per-region figures, and in any spreadsheet where someone put AVERAGE over a column of percentages. The correct operation is to recompute from the underlying numerators and denominators: sum the conversions, sum the visitors, divide. If you only have the rates, you need the weights, and if you do not have the weights you cannot compute the overall figure at all — which is worth saying rather than approximating.

Report the count alongside every summary

A rate, an average or a percentile without its sample size invites a conclusion the number cannot support, and the cost is close to zero: "conversion 4.1% (n=12,400)" and "conversion 4.1% (n=48)" are the same sentence carrying entirely different information, and only one of them should change a decision. This applies most sharply to segment breakdowns, which are where the small-sample problem hides — a table of twelve segments will typically have three or four with too few observations to say anything, and their rates will be the highest and lowest in the table purely as an artefact of variance. Someone will act on the top row. Including the count, and greying out or footnoting segments below a threshold you decide in advance, prevents the most predictable misreading of your own work (chapter 10).

The short version

  1. The mean answers questions about totals; the median answers questions about typical members. Ask which you were asked.
  2. For skewed data the mean can sit above the 75th percentile, describing almost no observations.
  3. Report five percentiles instead of one number. It shows the shape and answers several questions at once.
  4. For any user-experience measure the tail is the finding. Nobody experiences the mean.
  5. Never average an average. Recompute from the numerators and denominators, or state that you cannot.
  6. Use the geometric mean for rates and growth factors, and the arithmetic mean for quantities.
  7. Standard deviation is hard to interpret on skewed data. The interquartile range works on any shape.
  8. Always report the count. Especially in segment tables, where the extreme rows are usually the small ones.
