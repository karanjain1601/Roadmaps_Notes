Cohorts, funnels and retention

Three analyses that get requested constantly, each with a specific artefact that produces confident wrong conclusions — and in the funnel's case, an arithmetic property that makes the usual prioritisation question the wrong one entirely.

cohorts funnels retention survivorship

Where in the funnel to work

Two ways to state an improvement, two different answers Real arithmetic, and the relative case produces a result most people find surprising.
—biggest drop-off
—best step to work on
—orders gained there
—spread across steps
A relative improvement is worth exactly the same at every step

Final conversion is the product of the step rates, so multiplying any one of them by 1.1 multiplies the whole product by 1.1 — regardless of which step you pick. This is not an approximation; it is exact, and it means the question "which step has the biggest drop-off" is the wrong prioritisation question. A 76% drop-off and a 12% drop-off are equally valuable targets if you can improve either by the same relative amount. What actually differs between steps is not the value of a given improvement but how much improvement is achievable, which depends on headroom and tractability. So the useful question is "where can we get the biggest relative lift", and that is a product question about what is broken and fixable, informed by the data rather than answered by it.

READ DOWN A COLUMN, NEVER ACROSS A DIAGONAL. Each column is the same age
for different cohorts, so it is a fair comparison. Month-1 retention rose
from 42% to 46% — that is the finding, and it is real.

THREE THINGS THIS TABLE WILL BE MISREAD AS:

  1. "RETENTION IS IMPROVING" from the bottom-left corner. Recent cohorts
     look better because they are YOUNGER, not better. Only the column
     comparison is valid.

  2. "THE CURVE FLATTENS, SO THE PRODUCT GETS STICKIER." Partly. It also
     flattens because the people who were going to leave already left, so
     the remaining population is increasingly made of committed users.
     That is composition, not a change in anyone's behaviour — the same
     survivorship effect as chapter 10, inside a single cohort.

  3. "APRIL IS OUR BEST COHORT." Check the acquisition mix first. A cohort
     acquired through a different channel, or during a promotion, is a
     different population. Discounted cohorts almost always retain worse
     and referral cohorts almost always retain better, and neither tells
     you anything about the product.

THE CHECK THAT SAVES YOU: split the cohort table by acquisition channel
before drawing any conclusion. If the pattern holds within each channel it
is real. If it disappears, you were looking at a mix effect (chapter 12).
Monthly churn does not annualise by multiplying

A 5% monthly churn rate is not 60% a year. Retention compounds: 0.95 to the twelfth is 0.54, so annual retention is 54% and annual churn is 46%. Multiplying instead of compounding overstates it, and the error grows with the rate — at 10% monthly, multiplying gives 120%, which is visibly impossible and still gets written down. The reverse conversion causes the real damage: taking a known annual churn of 30% and dividing by 12 gives 2.5% monthly, when the correct figure is 2.9%. That is a 16% understatement of the monthly rate, which compounds into a materially wrong lifetime value and therefore a wrong acquisition budget. Convert with powers, not division, and state the basis, because "churn is 5%" means nothing without it.

Ask what happens to people who leave the funnel

A funnel measures the people who proceed. The interesting question is usually about the ones who do not, and they are exactly the ones absent from the next step's data. So a funnel tells you where people leave and never why, and every "why" derived purely from funnel numbers is a guess dressed as an analysis. Three things that do help: look at what the leavers did instead — went back, went elsewhere in the product, or left entirely, which imply different problems; check whether they return later, since a 30-day window often reveals a "drop-off" was a delay; and go and read some session recordings or support tickets for that step. That last one is qualitative and unrepresentative (chapter 10) and it is the fastest route to a hypothesis worth testing.

The short version

  1. A relative improvement is worth identically the same at every funnel step. Exact, not approximate.
  2. So drop-off size is the wrong prioritisation signal. The right question is where the biggest relative lift is achievable.
  3. Absolute point improvements do differ by step, favouring low-rate steps — because a point is a bigger relative gain there.
  4. Headroom is the real constraint. A step at 88% can gain at most 14% relative; one at 24% could triple.
  5. Read a cohort table down the columns. Comparing across ages is the commonest misreading.
  6. A flattening retention curve is partly composition — the fragile already left.
  7. Split cohorts by acquisition channel before concluding. Mix explains most apparent cohort differences.
  8. Churn compounds. 5% monthly is 46% annual, and dividing an annual rate by 12 understates it.
