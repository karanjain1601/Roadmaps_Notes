Correlation, regression, causation

Everyone knows correlation is not causation and almost every organisation acts on correlations anyway — because the alternative is doing nothing, and because the confounded version of a finding is more actionable-sounding than the honest one.

confounding regression selection

Simpson's paradox, with real numbers

Channel A beats B in every segment, and loses overall Genuine arithmetic. Nothing is being hidden — the mix does all the work.
—wins overall
—wins in each segment
—paradox present?
—correct action
Neither number is wrong. They answer different questions.

The aggregate rate answers "what happened overall", and it is correct as a description of what happened. The within-segment rates answer "which channel performs better for a given kind of customer", and they are correct too. The paradox arises because the channels serve different mixes of customers, so the aggregate is partly measuring the mix rather than the channel. Which number you should act on depends entirely on what you would change: if you would shift budget between channels holding the customer mix fixed, the segment rates are the right guide; if the channels come with their mixes attached and you cannot change that, the aggregate is what you will actually get. The failure is not picking the wrong number — it is not noticing that two exist, and reporting one as though the question had a single answer.

the canonical business confusion
"Customers who use feature X churn 40% less. We should push feature X."

WHY THIS IS USUALLY WRONG, and it is the single most repeated causal error
in product analytics:

  Engaged customers do two things. They adopt features, and they stay.
  Engagement causes both, so adoption and retention correlate strongly
  with no causal link between them at all.

  Pushing feature X onto disengaged customers changes their adoption and
  not their engagement, so the retention effect does not follow.

THE SAME SHAPE APPEARS EVERYWHERE:
  * customers who call support churn more -> support is not the cause
  * users who install the mobile app spend more -> the keen install it
  * accounts with a dedicated manager renew better -> big accounts get one
  * employees who attend training stay longer -> the committed attend

HOW TO TELL WHAT YOU HAVE. In order of strength:

  1. AN EXPERIMENT. Randomise who gets the intervention. This is the only
     method that settles it, and it is often available and not attempted
     (chapter 13).

  2. A STAGGERED OR PHASED ROLLOUT. If a full experiment is impossible,
     order the rollout deliberately and compare early against late. Much
     weaker than randomisation and far better than nothing.

  3. A NATURAL EXPERIMENT. Something outside your control assigned the
     treatment quasi-randomly — a policy change, an outage, a threshold.
     Look for these; they are underused.

  4. CONTROLLING FOR CONFOUNDERS. Matching or regression on observed
     variables. Handles what you can measure and nothing else — and the
     strongest confounder is usually the unobserved one.

  5. NOTHING. Report the association, say plainly that it is one, and
     propose the experiment that would settle it. This is a legitimate
     and useful deliverable, and it is the honest answer more often than
     analysts feel able to say (chapter 2).
Controlling for a variable can make things worse

The instinct that adding controls to a regression makes an estimate more causal is wrong in a specific and common way. Controlling for a confounder — something that causes both X and Y — helps. Controlling for a mediator — something on the causal path from X to Y — removes exactly the effect you were trying to measure: if a feature increases retention by increasing engagement, controlling for engagement makes the feature's effect vanish, and the conclusion "no effect" is precisely backwards. Controlling for a collider — something caused by both X and Y — can create a correlation where none existed. So "we controlled for everything we had" is not a virtue; it is a description of a model whose coefficients may mean nothing. The choice of controls requires a causal argument about how the variables relate, made before fitting, and there is no statistical test that identifies which kind you have.

Say which of the three you are claiming

Almost every misunderstanding in this chapter is prevented by one sentence in the write-up. "These move together" is a correlation and commits you to nothing. "Accounting for size and region, this relationship still holds" is a conditional association and says which confounders you addressed — and, by implication, which you did not. "Changing this would change that" is a causal claim and needs an experiment or an identification argument behind it. Analysts blur these because the causal phrasing is what gets acted on, and blurring them is how an organisation ends up pushing a feature that does nothing. Naming the level costs six words and it protects you twice: the reader cannot over-read the finding, and when someone later asks why the intervention did not work, the record shows you never claimed it would.

The short version

  1. Simpson's paradox is a mix effect. Both the aggregate and the segment rates are correct; they answer different questions.
  2. Which to act on depends on what you would change — and whether the mix comes attached to the choice.
  3. Check whether a relationship holds within every segment. If it reverses, the mix is doing the work.
  4. "Users of feature X churn less" is the canonical error. Engagement causes adoption and retention both.
  5. Only an intervention settles causation. A staggered rollout or a natural experiment is the fallback.
  6. Controlling for a mediator removes the effect you wanted to measure. More controls is not more causal.
  7. The strongest confounder is usually unobserved, so matching on what you have is an upper bound, not an estimate.
  8. Name the level of claim: they move together, it holds conditionally, or changing one changes the other.
