Hypothesis testing

A p-value below 0.05 does not mean there is a 95% chance the effect is real. The gap between that reading and the correct one is responsible for a large share of confidently wrong findings, and the correction is arithmetic rather than philosophy.

The p-value answers a question you did not ask

It answers: if there were no effect, how often would I see data at least this extreme? What you want to know is: given this data, how likely is it that there is an effect? Those are different questions and the second cannot be answered from the first alone — it also requires how plausible the hypothesis was before you tested, and how good your test is at detecting a real effect. The demo above is Bayes' theorem doing that arithmetic, and the uncomfortable result is that when you test many marginal hypotheses with modest power, a p-value just under 0.05 is close to a coin flip. This is not an argument against testing. It is an argument for caring about the prior plausibility of what you are testing and about having enough data to detect what you are looking for — both of which are decisions you make before running anything.

the practices that break a test
These are not exotic misconduct. They are what a well-meaning analyst does
under pressure to find something, and each inflates the false positive rate
far above the nominal 5%.

  TESTING MANY THINGS AND REPORTING ONE. Twenty segments at α=0.05 gives
  a 64% chance of at least one spurious "significant" result. Report how
  many you tested, always. That single number lets a reader calibrate.

  STOPPING WHEN IT TURNS SIGNIFICANT. Checking daily and stopping at the
  first p<0.05 roughly triples the false positive rate. Fix the sample
  size in advance, or use a method designed for sequential looks
  (chapter 13).

  CHANGING THE METRIC AFTER LOOKING. Trying conversion, then revenue,
  then retention, and reporting whichever worked. Each is a test.

  CHANGING THE SEGMENT AFTER LOOKING. "It works for mobile users in
  Europe" found by searching is a hypothesis, not a finding. It needs
  confirming on fresh data.

  DROPPING INCONVENIENT ROWS. Outlier removal decided after seeing its
  effect on the p-value is not cleaning (chapter 4).

  HARKing — writing up an exploratory finding as though it had been the
  hypothesis. The analysis is identical; the claim is not.

THE DEFENCE IS PROCEDURAL, NOT STATISTICAL. Write down the metric, the
segment, the sample size and the threshold before looking. That one habit
removes most of the list, and it is the same discipline as agreeing the
success criterion in chapter 2.

AND WHEN YOU HAVE EXPLORED — which is legitimate and valuable — say so.
"We tested 14 segments; this one reached p=0.03 uncorrected, which we
treat as a hypothesis for next quarter" is honest and useful. The same
finding presented as a result is not.
Statistical significance is not business significance

With a large enough sample any difference becomes significant, including differences too small to matter. A conversion improvement of 0.02 percentage points across ten million sessions will produce an impressive p-value and may not repay the engineering that produced it. The reverse is equally common and more damaging: a genuinely useful effect that fails to reach significance in an underpowered test, reported as "no difference", and a good idea gets killed by insufficient data rather than by evidence. So report the effect size and its confidence interval first, and the p-value second if at all — the interval carries everything the p-value does plus the magnitude, and it makes both failure modes visible. "Conversion rose 0.4 points, interval −0.1 to +0.9" tells a reader that the effect is probably positive, possibly negligible, and worth more data. "p = 0.11, not significant" tells them to stop, which is usually the wrong conclusion.

Choosing the test matters less than the assumptions

Analysts spend disproportionate effort on which test to use and too little on whether its assumptions hold. Three checks catch most problems. Independence — are the observations independent, or are there repeated measures from the same users, sessions from the same accounts, or orders from the same customers? Clustered data analysed as independent gives intervals that are far too narrow, and this is the most common violation in product analytics. Shape — heavy-tailed data breaks tests that assume stable variance, and a rank-based alternative or a bootstrap is the fix (chapters 8 and 10). And what is being compared — proportions, means and ratios each have their own machinery, and a ratio of two random quantities is harder than it looks. Get those three right with a standard test and you will be closer to correct than someone using an exotic test on data that violates its premises.

The short version

  1. A p-value answers a different question from the one you have. Converting between them needs the prior and the power.
  2. With many marginal hypotheses and modest power, p just under 0.05 is close to a coin flip.
  3. Twenty tests at α=0.05 gives a 64% chance of a spurious result. Report how many you tested.
  4. Stopping when it turns significant roughly triples the false positive rate. Fix n in advance.
  5. Non-significant is not "no effect." It means the data did not settle it.
  6. Report the effect size and interval first. They carry the p-value's information plus the magnitude.
  7. Significance is not importance in either direction — trivial effects pass, useful ones fail.
  8. Check independence, shape and what is being compared. Clustered data analysed as independent is the usual violation.
