Knowing your data

A surprising finding is far more often an artefact of how the data was collected than a discovery about the world. Checking the boring explanations first is not pessimism — it is the highest-yield habit in the job, and it takes twenty minutes.

Data is a record of a process, not a record of the world

Every dataset was produced by something — a form somebody filled in, an event a developer chose to fire, a system that writes a row when a job succeeds. What it contains is shaped by that process, and the gap between "what happened" and "what got recorded" is where most wrong analyses live. Three questions expose it: what was this collected for, who or what decides whether a row exists, and what happens when something goes wrong? A support system records tickets, not problems — problems that customers gave up on are absent. A CRM records what salespeople typed, which is a record of reporting incentives as much as of activity. An events pipeline records what the client successfully sent, so users on poor connections are systematically under-represented. None of these is bad data; each simply answers a narrower question than its column names suggest.

the twenty-minute orientation
Before analysing a table you have not used before. It is quick and it
prevents most of the errors in this unit.

  1. COUNT THE ROWS, and count the distinct values of the column you think
     is the key. If they differ, the grain is not what you assume, and
     every aggregate you write will be wrong (chapter 5).

  2. PLOT THE ROW COUNT BY DAY over the whole history. This one chart
     finds more problems than any other single check: gaps, a step change
     when tracking was added, a backfill, duplicated loads, a partial
     final day, a change in load schedule.

  3. FOR EACH COLUMN YOU WILL USE — null rate over time, distinct count,
     and min and max. A null rate that changes at a date is a change in
     collection, not in the world.

  4. FIND THE ODDEST VALUES. Sort ascending and descending and look. The
     dates in 1970 and 2099, the negative quantities, the test accounts,
     the 'REDACTED' strings, the placeholder emails.

  5. RECONCILE AGAINST SOMETHING TRUSTED. One total, one period, against a
     finance report or an established dashboard. If you cannot make them
     agree, understand why before continuing — that difference is usually
     the definition you were about to get wrong (chapter 16).

  6. ASK WHO OWNS IT and what changed recently. Ten minutes with the
     engineer or the operations person who lives in this system routinely
     saves days, and they know things no documentation contains.

WRITE THE ANSWERS DOWN somewhere the next person will find them. Most
organisations rediscover the same six facts about a table repeatedly, and
the marginal cost of recording them is a paragraph.
Survivorship and selection are in the data before you touch it

A table of current customers cannot tell you why customers leave, because the ones who left are not in it. A table of completed orders cannot tell you about abandonment. A table of resolved tickets under-represents the problems nobody could resolve. Every table has an inclusion rule, and it is a filter on your conclusion whether you applied it or not. The habit worth building is to ask, of any dataset, what is missing by construction — and then whether the missing part is relevant to the question. This is the same error as the classic wartime study of returning aircraft: the damage patterns on planes that came back tell you where damage is survivable, and the analysis that mattered required reasoning about the planes that were absent. Most analytical tables are returning aircraft.

Instrumentation changes explain more surprises than behaviour does

When a number moves sharply, the base rates favour a mechanical cause: a release changed which event fires, a consent banner changed who gets tracked, an ad blocker update changed collection, a definition changed in the transformation layer, a job ran twice, a currency conversion changed, a bot filter was added or removed. Real behavioural changes are usually gradual; instrumentation changes are usually step functions on a specific date. So the shape of the change is diagnostic — a clean step at a date boundary is nearly always mechanical, and a gradual drift is more likely real. Get the deployment log or the release calendar and overlay it on the chart before writing anything. Finding the deploy that explains the step is a good day's work; presenting the step as a behavioural insight and having someone else find the deploy is not.

The short version

  1. Data records a process, not the world. Ask what it was collected for and who decides whether a row exists.
  2. Check the boring explanations first: definition, tracking, pipeline, population, calendar. They win on base rates.
  3. A clean step at a date is nearly always mechanical. Gradual drift is more likely real.
  4. Column names lie by omission. created_at, revenue and user_id each hide several possible meanings.
  5. Plot row count by day over all history. One chart, and it finds more problems than any other check.
  6. Reconcile one total against something trusted before continuing. The difference is usually the definition you were about to get wrong.
  7. Every table has an inclusion rule that filters your conclusion whether you applied it or not.
  8. Ten minutes with the person who owns the system saves days, and they know what no documentation contains.
