Dashboards and reporting

Every dashboard is a permanent liability that someone has to maintain, and most of them are opened twice. Deciding what should not be a dashboard is more valuable than building a better one.

A dashboard is a product with an owner, not an artefact

The build is the cheapest part. What follows is permanent: the definitions drift as the business changes, a source table gets renamed and a panel silently returns nulls, someone asks for one more breakdown, and the person who built it leaves. An unowned dashboard degrades into something worse than nothing — a screen people half-trust, showing numbers nobody can defend, which quietly informs decisions anyway. So the question at build time is not "can I build this" but "will anyone own this in a year", and if the answer is no, deliver the number some other way. The most useful thing many analytics teams could do is audit their dashboards, find the ones nobody has opened in three months, and delete them — which reliably removes a large fraction and frees the attention that was maintaining them.

the five failures that kill a dashboard
1. IT BREAKS QUIETLY. A source column is renamed, a join starts returning
   nulls, a scheduled job fails. The dashboard renders, shows zeros or
   flat lines, and nobody notices for three weeks.
   -> add freshness and row-count checks that FAIL LOUDLY. A panel showing
      "data last updated 19 days ago" in red is worth more than any chart
      on the page.

2. THE LAST BAR IS ALWAYS SHORT. The current period is incomplete, and
   every viewer reads the partial month as a decline. Every time.
   -> exclude the incomplete period, or shade it and label it clearly.
      This single fix prevents more false alarms than anything else here.

3. DEFINITIONS DRIFT FROM THE REST OF THE ORGANISATION. The dashboard was
   built with one definition of active user, Finance uses another, and
   nobody reconciles until a board meeting (chapter 16).
   -> read from a shared model, not a bespoke query.

4. IT GROWS. Every request adds a panel, nothing is ever removed, and the
   thing becomes a data warehouse with a filter bar.
   -> one dashboard, one audience, one question. A second audience gets a
      second, smaller dashboard rather than four more panels.

5. IT ANSWERS "WHAT" AND NOBODY CAN ASK "WHY". Someone sees a dip and has
   no route from the number to an explanation, so they message the analyst
   — which is the workload the dashboard was meant to remove.
   -> provide one level of drill-down on the dimensions people actually
      ask about, and stop there. Unlimited exploration is a different
      product and most people do not want it.
Self-serve does not remove the analyst; it changes the work

The promise is that if people can answer their own questions the analyst is freed for deeper work. What happens in practice is that people answer their own questions incorrectly — they pick the wrong date grain, filter out the segment that mattered, compare a partial month against a complete one, or find a difference between two small segments that is entirely noise. The analyst's queue does not shrink; it fills with reconciliation. Self-serve works when the semantic layer makes wrong answers hard to construct: pre-joined models with the grain fixed, metrics defined once, sensible defaults, and small samples flagged automatically. That is real engineering effort, and it is what the promise actually costs. Handing people a query tool and a table list is not self-serve, it is delegation of the errors.

Put the freshness and the definition on the panel

Two small additions eliminate most of the questions a dashboard generates. A freshness stamp on every panel — "data through 2 March, updated 06:15" — so nobody has to ask whether it ran, and a stale panel is visibly stale rather than silently wrong. And the definition one click away, on the panel itself rather than in a linked document: what counts, over what window, what is excluded. The definition is where every dispute about a dashboard actually lives (chapter 16), and having it adjacent turns a Slack thread into a five-second check. Neither is a design flourish; both are the difference between a dashboard people trust and one they verify with the analyst before quoting, which is the state most dashboards are in.

The short version

  1. A dashboard is a permanent liability, and the build is the cheapest part of it.
  2. Ask who will own it in a year. If nobody, deliver the number another way.
  3. Audit and delete. The unopened ones are a large fraction, and removing them frees real attention.
  4. Five to nine panels on one screen. Above that nothing is read.
  5. Put the finding in the title, not the dimension. "North is the only region below last year".
  6. Handle the incomplete last period — shade it or exclude it. It causes more false alarms than anything else.
  7. Fail loudly on staleness. A quiet break is worse than an outage because it keeps informing decisions.
  8. Self-serve needs a semantic layer, or it just moves the errors to people who cannot see them.
