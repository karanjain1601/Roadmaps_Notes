Communicating a finding

An analysis that nobody acts on has the same value as one that was never done. The difference between the two outcomes is usually not the quality of the work — it is the first sentence.

Lead with the answer, not the journey

The instinct is to present the work in the order it was done — the question, the data, the cleaning, the method, the result — because that is the order in which it makes sense to the person who did it. It is close to the worst possible order for a reader, who needs the conclusion to know whether the rest is worth their attention. Invert it: the answer, then what it means for the decision, then the confidence, then the method for anyone who wants it. The reader who stops after the first sentence has the finding; the one who reads on gets the support. Presenting chronologically produces the opposite — the reader who stops early has the methodology and not the answer, which is exactly backwards, and in a meeting they will stop early.

Carrying uncertainty without drowning in it

Instead ofWrite
"The data may not be fully reliable""Excludes the 4% of orders missing a region"
"Results are directional""Between 2% and 7%; the direction is clear, the size is not"
"Further analysis is needed""An experiment would settle this; here is the design"
"Correlation is not causation""Engaged users do both, so pushing the feature may not transfer"
"Small sample""n=48, so this segment could plausibly be anywhere from 3% to 19%"
"Roughly 12%""12%, ±2 points"
"There may be a seasonal effect""January is typically 18% below December; this year it was 20% below"

The pattern is that a specific caveat is short, informative and increases trust, while a generic one is long, uninformative and reads as either hedging or boilerplate. A reader can act around a specific limitation; a generic one gives them nothing to act around, so they either ignore it or discount the whole finding.

a structure that works for almost anything
1. THE ANSWER, in one sentence, with the number.
     "North's revenue fell 12% year on year; two accounts explain 9 of
      those points."

2. WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE DECISION.
     "That is recoverable with account coverage — both accounts cited the
      March delivery failure, which is fixed."

3. THE CONFIDENCE, specifically.
     "The 12% is solid; the attribution to those two accounts is from
      sales notes rather than data, so treat it as their account of it."

4. THE ONE CHART that supports the headline. Not five.

5. WHAT YOU DID, briefly — exclusions, window, definitions. For the
   reader who needs to trust it or reproduce it.

6. WHAT YOU DID NOT DO. The questions this does not answer, so nobody
   assumes it did (chapter 2).

FOR A MEETING, points 1 to 3 are what you say. Everything else is
available if asked, and preparing it is what lets you answer the question
you did not expect — which is the actual test of whether an analysis was
done properly.

THE SENTENCE TO PRACTISE: "I don't know, and here is how we could find
out." It is the most credibility-preserving thing an analyst says, and it
is much harder to say than to read.
Being wrong is survivable; being wrong twice in the same way is not

Every analyst ships a wrong number eventually — a join that fanned out, a definition that shifted, a period that was not comparable. What determines whether it costs you is entirely the response. Say it early, say it plainly, say what the correct number is, and say what you changed so it does not recur. "The regional figures I sent on Tuesday double-counted reassigned accounts; the corrected North figure is £2.3m rather than £2.1m, and I have added a row-count check to that query" takes one message and mostly restores the position. What destroys credibility is discovering it and hoping, minimising it when raised, or fixing it silently so that two versions of the number exist. The asymmetry is worth internalising: an analyst known for catching their own errors is trusted more than one who has never visibly made any, because the second is indistinguishable from one whose errors have not been found.

Where the unit ends up

Almost everything in these eighteen chapters is downstream of two things, and neither is statistical.

Knowing what the numbers mean before computing with them
Which is why chapter 3 comes before chapter 7, why the boring explanations get checked first, why a join needs its grain established, why a definition determines a metric more than the measurement does, and why the same series compared four ways gives four correct answers. The arithmetic is rarely the hard part; knowing what it is arithmetic about is.
Being clear about what you are claiming
Which is why the four kinds of question matter, why the level of a causal claim has to be named, why an interval belongs next to every estimate, why exploratory findings are hypotheses, and why the specification says what is not covered. Most wrong decisions built on analysis come from a reader over-reading a claim the analyst did not quite make and did not quite prevent.

The statistical content — distributions, intervals, tests, power — is real and it is a smaller part of the job than its share of the curriculum suggests. The errors that actually reach decisions are a short list: the wrong question answered well; a column that did not mean what its name suggested; a join that duplicated rows; two groups that were never comparable; a segment small enough that its rate was noise; and a finding written so that the reader concluded more than the data supported. Six things, none of them requiring advanced methods to avoid, and every one of them preventable by a habit rather than a technique.

Which is the encouraging version of the argument. The parts of this job that are hardest to learn — asking the answerable question, noticing that a number is suspicious, saying "I don't know" in a room that wants certainty — are also the parts that do not go out of date, do not depend on which tool your employer uses, and are not automated by the next thing.

The short version

  1. Lead with the answer. A reader who stops after one sentence should have the finding, not the methodology.
  2. Specific caveats build trust; generic ones destroy it. "Excludes 4% missing a region" beats "may not be fully reliable".
  3. Say what the analysis does not answer, so nobody assumes it did.
  4. One chart for the headline. The rest is available if asked.
  5. "I don't know, and here is how we could find out" is the most credibility-preserving sentence available.
  6. Own errors early and plainly, with the corrected number and what you changed.
  7. Most wrong decisions come from a reader over-reading a claim the analyst did not quite make and did not prevent.
  8. The errors that reach decisions are a short list, and every one is prevented by a habit rather than a technique.
