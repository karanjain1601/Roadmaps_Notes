Charts that carry the argument

Most misleading charts are made by honest people using a tool's defaults. The distortions are a short, specific list, and knowing them protects you in both directions — from producing one, and from being persuaded by one.

Bars must start at zero; lines need not

This is the rule people half-remember and misapply in both directions. A bar encodes value as length, so the reader compares lengths, and a truncated axis makes a 3% difference look like a 60% one — the distortion is a direct consequence of what the mark means. A line encodes change as slope, so the reader compares the shape of the movement, and forcing a zero baseline on a series that varies between 94 and 98 flattens it into a straight line that conceals real variation. So: zero baseline for bars, always; a sensible range for lines, with the axis clearly labelled. The test is what the reader's eye is being asked to compare — length, or shape — and the honest choice follows from that rather than from a blanket rule.

the distortions, in order of how often they occur
1. TRUNCATED AXIS ON A BAR CHART. The most common by a distance, and
   frequently a tool default when values are clustered. Small differences
   become dramatic.

2. DUAL AXES. Two series on independent scales, and the crossing point is
   chosen by whoever set the ranges. Any two series can be made to look
   correlated, or to diverge, by adjusting one axis. Almost always better
   as two stacked charts sharing an x-axis, or as indexed values on one
   scale.

3. AREA SCALED BY RADIUS. Doubling a circle's radius quadruples its area,
   so a 2× difference reads as 4×. Scale by area. Most libraries do this
   correctly and hand-built graphics frequently do not.

4. CHERRY-PICKED WINDOW. A start date chosen so the trend goes the desired
   way. Test by extending the window in both directions and seeing whether
   the story survives (chapter 15).

5. AGGREGATION THAT HIDES THE PATTERN. Monthly totals concealing a
   within-month cycle, or a national average concealing regional
   divergence. Also the reverse — daily data so noisy the trend is
   invisible.

6. MISSING DENOMINATOR. Counts where rates are the question. "Complaints
   doubled" while the customer base tripled is a fall.

7. UNEQUAL BINS OR IRREGULAR TIME AXIS. A category axis with a gap where a
   period had no data, plotted as though the periods were adjacent.

8. STACKED AREAS FOR COMPARISON. Only the bottom series has a flat
   baseline; every one above it is distorted by the ones below, and nobody
   can read the middle bands.

9. TOO MANY SERIES. Above five or six lines nothing is legible. Highlight
   one, grey the rest, and repeat the chart if several matter.

10. RED AND GREEN AS THE ONLY DISTINCTION. Around one man in twelve cannot
    reliably separate them. Vary lightness or shape as well as hue.
Dual axes deserve their own warning

Plotting two series against two independent y-axes lets you produce any visual relationship you like, because the axes' ranges are free parameters and the point at which the lines cross or diverge is entirely a choice. There is no honest default — the reader cannot know what scaling was applied, so they read a relationship that the chart's author selected rather than one the data contains. Three better options: two charts stacked with a shared x-axis, so each series has an honest scale; both series indexed to 100 at a common starting point, which puts them on one comparable scale and makes relative change the subject; or a scatter of one against the other, if the relationship itself is the claim. When you see a dual-axis chart in someone else's deck, the useful question is what the chart would look like with the right-hand axis scaled differently — and it is worth asking, because the answer is often "completely different".

Write the headline before choosing the chart

State the one sentence the chart exists to support — "North fell while every other region grew", "the top decile accounts for 60% of revenue", "conversion is flat despite the redesign" — and then pick the encoding that makes that sentence visible without explanation. This ordering prevents the two commonest chart failures at once. It rules out charts with no argument, which are the ones that get skipped in a deck because the reader cannot tell what they are meant to notice. And it rules out the wrong encoding, because once the sentence is about a comparison between categories you will reach for a bar rather than a pie, and once it is about change over time you will reach for a line. Then put the sentence in the title. A chart titled "Revenue by region" makes the reader do the work; one titled "North is the only region below last year" has already done it.

The short version

  1. Bars start at zero; lines need not. The rule follows from whether the eye compares length or slope.
  2. Position on a common scale is the most accurately read encoding. Angle, area and volume are the least.
  3. Dual axes have no honest default. Use stacked charts, indexed values, or a scatter.
  4. Scale bubbles by area, not radius, or a 2× difference reads as 4×.
  5. Test the window by extending it both ways. If the story does not survive, it was the window.
  6. Check for the missing denominator. Counts rising while the base rises faster is a fall.
  7. Above five or six series nothing is legible. Highlight one, grey the rest.
  8. Write the headline first, then choose the chart, and put the headline in the title.
