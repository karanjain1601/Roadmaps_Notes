Sampling and uncertainty

There are two kinds of error in a sample estimate and only one of them shrinks when you collect more data. Confusing them is why organisations trust large biased samples over small clean ones, which is exactly the wrong way round.

A biased sample of a million is worse than a random sample of a thousand

Sampling error is the random variation between samples, and it shrinks with the square root of the sample size — quadruple the data and the interval halves. Sampling bias is a systematic difference between who is in your sample and who you want to describe, and it does not shrink at all. Collecting more of a biased sample makes you more confident about the wrong number, which is strictly worse than being uncertain about it, because a narrow interval around a wrong value is the thing people act on. This is the failure behind survey results that miss elections despite enormous samples, and behind the everyday version: reading customer sentiment from the customers who wrote in. The question to ask of any estimate is not "how big is the sample" but "who is missing from it, and are they different?" (chapter 3).

what a confidence interval actually says
THE CORRECT READING: if you repeated this sampling procedure many times
and computed an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would
contain the true value.

THE COMMON MISREADING: "there is a 95% probability the true value is in
this interval." That is a statement about the truth being random, and the
truth is fixed — it is your interval that varies between samples.

In practice the distinction rarely changes a decision, and one consequence
of it does: THE INTERVAL SAYS NOTHING ABOUT BIAS. It quantifies how much
your estimate would bounce around if you resampled the same way, and if
the method is systematically off, every one of those intervals is
systematically off with it.

  a 95% interval of 22.1% to 23.4% from a biased sample is a precise
  statement about a number that is not the one you wanted

THE PRACTICAL RULES

  ALWAYS REPORT THE INTERVAL, not the point estimate alone. A point
  estimate invites a comparison it cannot support, and someone will make
  it (chapter 7).

  THE WIDTH SHRINKS WITH THE SQUARE ROOT OF n. Four times the data halves
  the interval. Sixteen times quarters it. This is why doubling a sample
  feels disappointing.

  FOR A PROPORTION, a usable approximation of the 95% half-width:
      1.96 × sqrt( p(1-p) / n )
  At p=0.1 and n=400 that is about ±2.9 percentage points.

  WITH ZERO OCCURRENCES IN n TRIALS, the upper bound is roughly 3/n. Zero
  complaints in 50 orders is consistent with a true rate up to 6%.

  THE POPULATION SIZE BARELY MATTERS. A sample of 1,000 gives roughly the
  same precision whether the population is 100,000 or 100 million — which
  surprises people, and is why national surveys use a few thousand.
  (It only changes when the sample is a large fraction of the population.)
You still need intervals when you have "all the data"

Analysts working with a full warehouse often conclude that sampling theory does not apply, because there is no sample — every order is there. That is right for a descriptive claim about the past: last quarter's revenue is a fact, not an estimate, and putting an interval on it is a category error. It is wrong the moment the claim is about anything beyond the period observed, and almost every useful claim is. "Conversion improved" is an inference that next month will differ too; "this segment converts better" is a claim about the segment, not about those particular 200 people; "the redesign worked" projects forward. In each case the observed period is a sample from the process that generated it, and the variation between periods is real (chapter 15). The test: if the same number would plausibly come out differently had you looked at a different but equally valid window, you are estimating, and you owe the reader an interval.

Bootstrap when the formula is awkward

Analytical formulas for confidence intervals exist for means and proportions and get unpleasant for medians, ratios, percentiles, differences of differences, and anything computed through several steps. The bootstrap sidesteps all of it: resample your data with replacement to the same size, recompute the statistic, repeat a few thousand times, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the results. Ten lines of code, no distributional assumption, and it works for essentially any statistic you can compute — including the one you invented for this analysis. Two cautions: it captures sampling error and cannot detect bias, like every interval; and it needs a reasonable sample to resample from, so it does not rescue you from having forty observations. But for "what is the interval around this median" or "around this year-on-year ratio", it is the practical answer and it removes the excuse for reporting a bare point estimate.

The short version

  1. Two kinds of error: random and systematic. Only the random one shrinks with sample size.
  2. A biased sample of a million is worse than a random sample of a thousand — more confidence about the wrong number.
  3. Ask who is missing from the sample, not how large it is.
  4. Support tickets and survey responses are volunteer channels, strongly unrepresentative, and routinely read as sentiment.
  5. The interval quantifies sampling error only. It says nothing about bias, however narrow it is.
  6. Width shrinks with the square root of n. Four times the data halves the interval.
  7. Having all the data does not remove uncertainty for any claim that projects beyond the period observed.
  8. Bootstrap when the formula is awkward. Ten lines, no assumptions, works on any statistic.
