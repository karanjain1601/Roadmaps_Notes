Chapter 04 · Getting to clean

Cleaning without lying

Every cleaning decision moves the answer. That is unavoidable and it is fine — what is not fine is making the decisions without knowing which way they move it, which is the default when cleaning is treated as a chore to get through before the analysis starts.

Why a value is missing decides how to handle it

There are three regimes and they demand different responses. Missing completely at random — a flaky integration dropped rows independently of their content — means dropping them costs precision and introduces no bias, and it is the benign case. Missing at random given what you know — a field absent for one client version, and you have the version — means you can adjust, because the missingness is explained by something observed. Missing not at random — the value is absent because of what it would have been — is the dangerous case: free trials have no amount, declined transactions have no total, unhappy customers skip the survey. Here dropping rows and imputing both encode the bias, and the only honest options are to model the missingness explicitly or to report the two groups separately. The regime is not visible in the data; you establish it by asking how the field is populated (chapter 3).

Duplicates

four kinds, and only one is a duplicate
1. AN EXACT REPEATED ROW. Usually a pipeline artefact — a load ran twice.
   Safe to deduplicate, and worth telling whoever owns the pipeline.

2. THE SAME ENTITY RECORDED TWICE, DIFFERENTLY. Two customer records for
   one company, with different spellings and different totals. Not a
   duplicate row and a genuine data problem — resolving it is entity
   resolution, it is hard, and it is frequently out of scope. Say that it
   exists and estimate its size rather than silently picking one.

3. LEGITIMATE REPETITION. The same customer ordered the same item twice.
   These are two facts and deduplicating them destroys real data. This is
   the error that a blind DISTINCT introduces, and it is invisible
   afterwards.

4. FAN-OUT FROM A JOIN. Rows multiplied by joining to a table at a finer
   grain. Not a data problem at all — an analysis bug you created two
   lines ago, and chapter 5 is entirely about it.

THE TEST THAT DISTINGUISHES THEM: before deduplicating, count the rows,
then count distinct values of what you believe is the key. If they differ,
find out WHY before choosing an action.

  -- what is actually duplicated?
  SELECT order_id, COUNT(*) AS n
  FROM orders GROUP BY order_id HAVING COUNT(*) > 1
  ORDER BY n DESC LIMIT 20;

Then look at a few of them. Twenty seconds of looking tells you which of
the four you have, and each has a different correct response. Reaching for
DISTINCT without looking is how case 3 gets destroyed.

A statistical rule is not a reason to remove a real observation

"More than three standard deviations from the mean" and "outside 1.5 times the interquartile range" are detection heuristics, not decision rules, and applying them mechanically removes the most informative rows in a heavy-tailed dataset. Revenue, session length, order value, company size and file size are all heavy-tailed by nature: in a business where 5% of customers produce 60% of revenue, a three-sigma filter deletes the business. The rules also assume roughly normal data, and if your data were normal you would not have outliers to worry about — which is the circularity that makes them so misleading. Use them to find candidates for inspection, then decide each candidate on its merits: is it impossible, is it an error, or is it real? Only the first two justify removal, and both justify counting and reporting how many.

the cleaning log
Every analysis needs one, and it takes five lines. It is what makes a
finding auditable, and it is where most disagreements actually live.

  STARTING ROWS                        1,000,000
  - test and internal accounts            -4,210   documented rule
  - orders before the tracking change    -38,900   comparability
  - rows with missing amount              -1,180   0.12%, appears random
  - one duplicated load (2024-03-14)      -2,940   confirmed with data eng
  ROWS USED                             952,770    95.3% of original

  KEPT DELIBERATELY
  - 6 orders over £50k. Real, verified with sales. Reported with and
    without, because they move the mean by 8% and the median not at all.

  NOT RESOLVED
  - ~900 accounts appear to be duplicate records of the same companies.
    Roughly 0.4% of revenue. Not resolved; flagged to the CRM owner.

TWO THINGS THIS BUYS YOU. It is REPRODUCIBLE — someone else can arrive at
your number, and if they cannot, the discrepancy is locatable. And it
PRE-EMPTS the challenge, because the most common objection to a finding is
"did you exclude X?", and the log answers it before it is asked.

THE RED FLAG TO WATCH FOR IN YOUR OWN WORK: if the excluded fraction is
large — say above 10% — the exclusions are no longer cleaning. They are a
population definition, and it belongs in the specification as a stated
choice rather than in a cleaning step (chapter 2).
Run the analysis twice, with the choice reversed

For each cleaning decision that could plausibly have gone the other way, compute the answer both ways. It costs a few minutes and it produces the single most useful sentence you can put in a finding: "the direction of the result does not depend on how the missing values were handled" — or, when it does, "the effect is present if we drop these rows and absent if we impute them, which means the data cannot settle this". That second sentence is uncomfortable and it is the honest one, and delivering it is far better than picking whichever handling gives a cleaner story. This is the same discipline as a sensitivity check in any quantitative field, and in analytics it is unusually cheap because rerunning the query is a keystroke.

The short version

  1. Why a value is missing decides the handling. Completely at random is benign; not at random is dangerous and invisible.
  2. Filling with the mean shrinks the variance and makes every interval too narrow. It is rarely the right choice.
  3. Zero and missing are different. Conflating them is a common and consequential error.
  4. Four things look like duplicates and only one is. Count rows against distinct keys, then look at examples.
  5. A blind DISTINCT destroys legitimate repetition, invisibly.
  6. Three-sigma rules assume normality, and heavy-tailed business data is the norm. Use them to find candidates, not to decide.
  7. Keep a cleaning log. It makes the finding reproducible and pre-empts the usual objection.
  8. Run it both ways. Either the direction is robust, or the data cannot settle the question — both worth saying.
