Time series and seasonality

The same series compared four different ways produces four different stories, all arithmetically correct. Choosing the comparison is therefore an editorial act, and pretending otherwise is how a routine seasonal dip becomes an emergency.

Decompose before comparing

Any business series is roughly the sum of three things: a trend (where the level is going), seasonality (a repeating pattern of known period), and noise (everything else). Almost every reporting error comes from attributing movement in one component to another — reading a seasonal trough as a decline, or a noisy month as a turning point. Splitting them takes one function in any statistical library and it changes what you say: instead of "revenue fell 18% in January", you get "revenue fell 18%, of which roughly 20 points is the usual January pattern, so the underlying trend improved slightly." That second sentence is longer, correct, and prevents a quarter of unnecessary panic. Do the decomposition once for each important series and keep the seasonal profile; it rarely changes fast.

the traps, in the order they occur
DIFFERENT NUMBERS OF DAYS. February has 10% fewer days than January, and
  a 28-day month against a 31-day month is a 9.7% headwind before anything
  happens. Compare daily rates, or use 4-week periods.

DIFFERENT NUMBERS OF WEEKENDS. Two months with the same day count can
  contain a different number of Saturdays, which matters a great deal for
  B2B and for retail in opposite directions.

MOVING HOLIDAYS. Easter shifts between March and April; Ramadan moves
  through the calendar; Black Friday can land in a different fiscal week.
  Year-on-year silently compares a holiday month against a non-holiday one.

THE BASE PERIOD WAS UNUSUAL. Year-on-year against a month that contained a
  one-off — an outage, a large contract, a promotion — makes the current
  month look wrong in the opposite direction. Always look at the base.

THE PARTIAL PERIOD. The current month is incomplete and gets compared
  against complete ones. Obvious when stated and constantly done in
  dashboards, where the last bar is always short (chapter 17).

LATE-ARRIVING DATA. Recent periods are understated because records are
  still landing — refunds, corrections, delayed events. The last few points
  of almost every series revise upward, so the apparent recent decline is
  an artefact. Know your settlement lag and grey out unsettled periods.

RATIOS OF RATIOS. "Growth is slowing" from comparing this month's YoY
  growth against last month's YoY growth is a second derivative computed
  on noisy data, and it is almost always noise.

CHERRY-PICKED WINDOWS. Extend the window in both directions. If the story
  only holds from one particular start date, it was the start date.
Year on year is the default for a reason, and it has two specific failure modes

It removes seasonality without any modelling, which is why it is the right first choice for most business reporting. But it compares against a single month twelve months ago, so it inherits everything unusual about that month — and one-off events in the base period produce dramatic, meaningless swings in the current one. It is also slow: a change in trend that started four months ago is still being averaged against a year-old baseline, so year-on-year growth keeps looking healthy for months after the business has turned. The practical combination is to report year on year for the headline, and a rolling three-month average alongside it for the recent direction. When the two disagree, that disagreement is the finding — it means something changed recently enough that the annual comparison has not caught up.

Never call a single point an anomaly

Series bounce. A month that looks extreme is usually within the normal range of variation for that series, and the way to know is to look at how much it has bounced historically rather than to judge by eye. Compute the typical size of month-to-month movement over two or three years, and only flag points outside that band — for a series with a monthly standard deviation of 8%, a 10% move is unremarkable and will happen several times a year. This is the same discipline as chapter 10's confidence intervals applied to time: a point estimate with no sense of the noise invites a story, and someone will supply one. The corollary is that two or three consecutive points in the same direction are far more informative than one large one, which is why a rolling average earns its lag.

The short version

  1. Decompose into trend, seasonality and noise before comparing. Most reporting errors attribute one to another.
  2. Month on month reports the season for any seasonal series, and most business series are seasonal.
  3. Year on year removes seasonality free, inherits everything odd about the base month, and lags a turning point.
  4. Report year on year and a rolling average together. When they disagree, the disagreement is the finding.
  5. Check day counts, weekend counts and moving holidays before believing a period comparison.
  6. Recent periods revise upward. Know the settlement lag and grey out unsettled points.
  7. Growth of growth is a second derivative on noisy data and is almost always noise.
  8. Never call one point an anomaly. Measure the historical bounce first; consecutive points beat one big one.
