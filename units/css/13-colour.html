Colour

Mix blue and yellow in sRGB and you get grey — measured, rgb(128, 128, 128). That is not a browser quirk; it is what averaging the channels of two opposite hues does, and it is why a default gradient between complementary colours goes muddy in the middle. Choosing a different interpolation space changes the answer entirely.

Where the mixing happens

color-mix(in …, blue, yellow)
Six spaces, six different midpoints
sRGB gives grey
Measured color(srgb 0.5 0.5 0.5). Blue is (0,0,1) and yellow is (1,1,0); average the channels and every one lands on 0.5.
hsl rotates the hue instead
Measured color(srgb 0 1 0.5) — a bright spring green, a colour present in neither original. Interpolating an angle takes the short way round the wheel.
oklch preserves the chroma
Measured oklch(0.71 0.262 186.9) — a saturated cyan, and the perceptually sensible answer.
And that oklch midpoint is outside sRGB
Converted back it has a negative red channel, -0.63. It has to be gamut-mapped before an ordinary screen can show it; a wide-gamut display shows more of it.
css — choosing the space explicitly
linear-gradient(in oklch, blue, yellow)     /* no grey in the middle */
linear-gradient(in oklch longer hue, …)     /* the long way round the wheel */
color-mix(in oklab, var(--brand) 70%, white)

/* relative colour syntax: derive from an existing value */
--hover: oklch(from var(--brand) calc(l + 0.1) c h);
--faded: rgb(from var(--brand) r g b / 40%);

/* wide gamut, with a fallback that older engines take */
.accent { color: #0af; color: color(display-p3 0 0.66 1) }

/* the modern spaces, roughly */
oklch(L C H)     /* perceptual. L 0–1, C 0–0.4ish, H 0–360        */
oklab(L a b)     /* the same space in cartesian form               */
lab / lch        /* older perceptual space, less even in blues     */
hsl / hwb        /* convenient, NOT perceptual — see the measurements */

Contrast, and why oklch lightness is not the whole answer

Perceptual spaces make one thing much easier: two colours with the same L in oklch genuinely look about equally light, where two colours with the same L in hsl do not — hsl(60 100% 50%) is a blazing yellow and hsl(240 100% 50%) is a dark blue, both at "50% lightness". That makes oklch the right space for building a tonal palette where every step is an even change. It does not make it a contrast checker. WCAG contrast is a specific ratio computed from relative luminance in sRGB, and passing it is what accessibility conformance is measured against — chapter 21. Equal oklch lightness is a good starting point and not a substitute for computing the ratio, and the newer APCA model disagrees with WCAG in places, particularly on light text over mid-tones.

Check yourself

Self-check

  1. Why is the sRGB midpoint of blue and yellow grey?
    Because averaging the channels cancels them. Blue is (0, 0, 1) and yellow is (1, 1, 0), so the halfway point is (0.5, 0.5, 0.5) on every channel — measured exactly as color(srgb 0.5 0.5 0.5). Nothing has gone wrong; the arithmetic is doing what it says. The consequence is that a default gradient between any two roughly complementary colours passes through grey in the middle, which almost nobody wants and which used to be worked around by inserting intermediate colour stops by hand. Interpolating in a different space fixes it properly: measured, oklch gave a saturated cyan and hsl gave a bright green. The syntax is linear-gradient(in oklch, blue, yellow), and it costs nothing to add.
  2. Why does hsl interpolation give green?
    Because it interpolates the hue angle, and the short way round the wheel from blue (240°) to yellow (60°) passes through green at 150°. Measured as color(srgb 0 1 0.5), a bright spring green that appears in neither original colour. That is sometimes exactly what you want — a rainbow sweep between two hues — and sometimes a surprise. The longer hue keyword takes the other route round the wheel, and shorter, increasing and decreasing give you the remaining options. It also illustrates why hsl is convenient rather than perceptual: its "lightness" is a geometric coordinate, so hsl(60 100% 50%) is a blazing yellow and hsl(240 100% 50%) is a dark blue at the same stated lightness.
  3. The oklch midpoint has a negative red channel. What does that mean?
    That the colour is outside the sRGB gamut — measured, converting it back gave a red channel of −0.63, and a negative amount of red is not something a screen can emit. Perceptual spaces can describe colours more saturated than sRGB can reproduce, which is a feature rather than a fault: on a wide-gamut display more of that colour is actually shown, and on an ordinary one the browser gamut-maps it to the nearest reproducible colour. The honest limit of this chapter's measurements is right here — the harness reads computed styles rather than screen pixels, so the gamut-mapped result is not something it can report, and the chapter says so rather than implying the numbers describe what you would see.
  4. What does getComputedStyle report for oklch(70% 0.4 30)?
    oklch(0.7 0.4 30) — it stays in its authored space. That is a deliberate change from the older model, where every colour resolved to rgb(): measured, red and hsl(240 100% 50%) both computed to rgb() values, because those notations were only ever ways of writing sRGB. The modern functions keep their space because flattening them would lose information that a wide-gamut display can use, and because conversion is deferred until paint. The practical consequences are that you cannot read a computed style to find out what a colour looks like on screen, and that comparing two computed colours for equality is unreliable when they were authored in different spaces — rgb(from …) is the way to force a comparable form.
  5. What is relative colour syntax for?
    Deriving one colour from another in CSS itself, without a preprocessor. rgb(from red r g b / 50%) destructures red into channel keywords and rebuilds it with a new alpha — measured as color(srgb 1 0 0 / 0.5). The useful version works on a custom property: oklch(from var(--brand) calc(l + 0.1) c h) gives a lighter variant of whatever the brand colour currently is, so a theme switch that changes one variable updates every derived state automatically. Measured, that expression on red produced oklch(0.828 0.258 29.2). Doing this in oklch rather than hsl matters, because a fixed lightness step in a perceptual space is an even visual step across hues, where the same arithmetic in hsl is not.
  6. When should you use display-p3?
    When a colour genuinely benefits from being more saturated than sRGB allows, and always with a fallback. The pattern is two declarations — color: #0af; followed by color: color(display-p3 0 0.66 1); — where an engine that does not understand the second simply ignores it and keeps the first, which is the cascade doing its job. Measured, the p3 value stays in its space through to the computed value. The honest caveat is that most interfaces gain very little: body text, borders and surfaces are not improved by wider gamut, and the extra saturation can make an accent harder to read against. It earns its place on brand accents, on data visualisation where distinguishable categories matter, and on imagery — and it should never be the only declaration, since a fallback costs one line.
  7. Does equal oklch lightness mean equal contrast?
    No — it means roughly equal perceived lightness, which is a different and weaker claim. Two colours at the same L in oklch do look about equally light, which is exactly what you want when building a tonal palette where every step should be an even change, and which is not true in hsl at all. But WCAG contrast is a specific ratio computed from relative luminance in sRGB, with defined thresholds, and it is what accessibility conformance is actually measured against — chapter 21. So oklch is the right tool for designing a palette and the wrong one for verifying it; you still compute the ratio. Worth knowing that the newer APCA model disagrees with WCAG in places, particularly on light text over mid-tones, and that neither is a substitute for looking at the result.
