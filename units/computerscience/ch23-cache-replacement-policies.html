Cache replacement policies

Reading a loop of eleven items through a cache of ten gives LRU a hit rate of exactly zero — worse than evicting at random, worse than doing nothing clever at all. And giving FIFO a bigger cache can make it slower. Both are real, and both are measured below.

Why the policy matters so much

A cache is small and the data is large, so something must be thrown out. The replacement policy is the guess about what will be needed again, and the arithmetic makes small differences in that guess expensive.

Average access time when a hit costs 1 ns and a miss costs 100
Hit rateAverage costAgainst a perfect cache
90%10.9 ns10.9×
95%6.0 ns6.0×
99%2.0 ns2.0×

Read that as a warning about intuition. Going from 90% to 95% — five percentage points — nearly halves the average cost, a factor of 1.83. The last nine points from 90% to 99% save 8.9 ns, which is more than the entire cost of a perfect cache. Hit rate is not linear in value, and a cache that "already hits 90% of the time" may still be where all your time goes.

The policies

FIFO
Evict whatever arrived first. Trivial to implement, ignores usage entirely, and has the anomaly in section four.
LRU — least recently used
Evict what has gone longest without being touched. The default answer, and a good one whenever recency predicts reuse.
MRU — most recently used
The opposite. Sounds absurd until you meet a scan, where the item just touched is the one you are certain not to need again soon.
LFU — least frequently used
Evict the least-accessed. Better than LRU when popularity is stable, and it suffers from cache pollution: an item that was hot once keeps its count forever and never leaves.
CLOCK — second chance
An approximation of LRU in constant time and one bit per entry. Entries sit in a ring; a hand sweeps, and an entry with its reference bit set gets the bit cleared and survives one pass. This is what real operating systems use.
Random
Evict anything. Needs no metadata, has no pathological case, and is a surprisingly competitive baseline — which is the point of including it.
ARC — adaptive replacement
Keeps two lists, recently-used-once and used-more-than-once, plus ghost entries recording what was recently evicted, and shifts the balance between them based on which ghost list is being hit. Self-tuning between recency and frequency.

Belady's optimum, and what it is for

Evict the item whose next use is furthest in the future.

Belady's MIN is provably optimal, and it is unimplementable, because it requires knowing the future. It is not an algorithm; it is a yardstick. Simulating it on a recorded trace tells you the best any policy could possibly have done, which converts "our hit rate is 57%" from a number into a judgement.

On the working-set trace the optimum is 78.1% and LRU manages 56.9%, so there is 21 points of headroom and a better policy is worth chasing — LFU finds 73.3% of it. On the uniform-random trace the optimum is 48.7% and every real policy sits near 19%, which says something different: with no structure to exploit, the gap is irreducible and no policy change will close it. Knowing which situation you are in is the whole value of computing the bound.

The offline-online distinction

Belady's MIN is an offline algorithm: it sees the whole input before deciding. Every deployable policy is online, deciding with no knowledge of what comes next. The ratio between an online algorithm's cost and the offline optimum is its competitive ratio, and there is a clean theorem here: no deterministic online policy can do better than k-competitive on a cache of size k. LRU achieves that bound, so it is optimal among deterministic policies in the worst case — which is not the same as being good on your workload, as the next section shows.

Belady's anomaly: a bigger cache, more misses

Intuition says more memory cannot hurt. For FIFO it can.

Belady's reference string is 1 2 3 4 1 2 5 1 2 3 4 5. With three frames, FIFO takes 9 misses. With four frames it takes 10. Across sizes 1 to 6, FIFO's miss counts run 12 12 9 10 5 5 — the jump from 9 to 10 is the anomaly.

LRU on the same string runs 12 12 10 8 5 5: never increasing. That is not luck. LRU is a stack algorithm, meaning the contents of a cache of size k are always a subset of the contents of a cache of size k+1 at the same point in the trace. If a bigger cache always contains everything a smaller one does, it cannot miss on something the smaller one hit. FIFO has no such property: eviction order depends on arrival order, which a different capacity changes wholesale.

Why this is worth knowing operationally

If your cache uses FIFO or a FIFO-like eviction — many queue-based and TTL-based caches effectively do — then "the hit rate dropped after we doubled the cache" is a possible outcome rather than evidence of a bug elsewhere. LRU, LFU and Belady's optimum are all stack algorithms and cannot exhibit it. CLOCK, being FIFO with a survival bit, can.

The loop that destroys LRU

Cycle through 11 items with a cache of 10 and LRU's hit rate is exactly zero. Every item is evicted precisely one access before it is needed again, because "least recently used" is, in a cycle, always the next thing you will want. Over 2,200 accesses it scores zero hits.

MRU matches the optimum exactly, and even evicting at random beats LRU by 81 points. Sequential scans over data larger than the cache are the same failure in a different shape, and they are extremely common — a table scan, a batch job, a log replay, an ls -R. This is why database buffer pools detect scans and route them through a small separate region, and why LFU-based and adaptive policies exist at all.

What ARC actually does about it

ARC keeps two LRU lists — items seen once, and items seen more than once — plus ghost lists remembering what was recently evicted from each. A hit in the "seen once" ghost list means recency is being under-served, so it grows that side; a hit in the other ghost list means frequency is, so it grows the other. The result adapts to a shifting workload without tuning, resists scan pollution because a scanned item only ever reaches the "seen once" list, and cannot exhibit Belady's anomaly. It is what ZFS and several database buffer pools use.

Implementing them

LRU in O(1) — a hash map plus a doubly linked list
map:  key   -> node          // O(1) lookup
list: head <-> … <-> tail    // head = most recent, tail = least

get(key):
    node = map[key] or return miss
    moveToHead(node)                  // unlink + relink: 6 pointer writes
    return node.value

put(key, value):
    if (map.size === capacity) {
        victim = tail.prev
        unlink(victim); map.delete(victim.key)
    }
    insertAtHead(newNode); map[key] = newNode

This is the structure chapter 13 promised: the map gives Θ(1) lookup and the list gives Θ(1) reordering, and neither alone is enough. It is also the most-asked cache question in interviews, and the reason is that it forces you to notice that the two data structures do different halves of the job.

Why CLOCK exists

LRU needs a list update on every hit, which means a write on the hot path and, in a kernel, a lock. CLOCK needs one bit set on a hit and does its work only on eviction. It gives most of LRU's benefit — the demo measures CLOCK within 2 points of LRU on a working-set trace — for a fraction of the cost.

Why LFU is awkward

Finding the minimum count is Θ(n) naively. Doing it in Θ(1) needs a bucketed structure of frequency lists. And counts must decay, or an item that was popular last year stays resident forever — TinyLFU solves both with a count-min sketch and periodic halving, and is what Caffeine uses.

Two things recur in that list. Almost nothing implements true LRU, because maintaining exact recency costs more than the accuracy is worth — Redis samples a handful of keys and evicts the oldest of those. And most real caches have a constraint the textbook policies ignore: entries have different sizes and different costs to recompute. Evicting one 10 MB object to make room for ten 1 MB ones is a different calculation from anything above, and it is why CDN policies score by cost-per-byte rather than by recency alone.

Check yourself

Your cache hits 90% of the time. A miss costs 100× a hit. How much is going to 99% worth?

More than everything else combined. At 90% the average access is 10.9 ns; at 99% it is 2.0 ns. The 8.9 ns saved is larger than the 1 ns a perfect cache would cost, so most of your remaining time is in that last 9%. Even the smaller step from 90% to 95% cuts the average by a factor of 1.83. Hit rate is not linear in value, which is why "we already hit 90%" is not a reason to stop.

Why does LRU get a 0% hit rate on a loop of 11 items through a cache of 10?

Because in a cycle, the least recently used item is always precisely the next one you will need. Every item is evicted one access before its reuse, so nothing is ever found. Over 2,200 accesses LRU scores zero hits, random scores 81.3%, and MRU scores 89.6% — exactly matching Belady's optimum, because in a loop the item just touched genuinely is the one you need least soon. Sequential scans over data larger than the cache are the same failure, and they are extremely common.

What is Belady's optimum for, given that it cannot be implemented?

It is a yardstick, not an algorithm. Simulating "evict whatever is used furthest in the future" on a recorded trace tells you the best any policy could have achieved, which turns a raw hit rate into a judgement. On the working-set trace the optimum is 78.1% and LRU gets 56.9% — 21 points of headroom worth chasing, and LFU recovers most of it. On uniform random the optimum is 48.7% and every real policy sits near 19%, so the gap is irreducible and no policy change will help. Those are opposite conclusions from similar-looking numbers.

Explain Belady's anomaly, and why LRU cannot have it.

On the string 1 2 3 4 1 2 5 1 2 3 4 5, FIFO takes 9 misses with 3 frames and 10 with 4 — more memory, worse performance. LRU is immune because it is a stack algorithm: the contents of a size-k cache are always a subset of the contents of a size-(k+1) cache at the same point in the trace, so the bigger cache cannot miss on anything the smaller one held. FIFO has no such property, because eviction order depends on arrival order and a different capacity reshuffles it entirely. LFU and Belady's optimum are also stack algorithms; CLOCK, being FIFO with a survival bit, is not.

When is LFU better than LRU, and what goes wrong with it?

LFU wins when popularity is stable and skewed. On a Zipf distribution over 200 items with a cache of 20 it hits 62.8% against LRU's 49.5%, and on a working-set trace 73.3% against 56.9%, because a genuinely hot item survives a burst of one-off accesses that would flush LRU. What goes wrong is pollution: an item popular last week keeps its high count forever and cannot be evicted no matter how stale it becomes. The fixes are ageing the counts — TinyLFU halves them periodically — or adapting between recency and frequency, which is what ARC does.

Why do operating systems use CLOCK instead of LRU?

Because LRU requires updating a list on every hit, which puts a write, and in a kernel a lock, on the hottest path in the system. CLOCK sets one reference bit on a hit and does all its real work only when it must evict: a hand sweeps a ring, and an entry with its bit set gets the bit cleared and one more pass. The demo measures CLOCK within 2 points of LRU on a working-set trace, for a small fraction of the bookkeeping. The same reasoning explains why CPU caches use pseudo-LRU and why Redis approximates LRU by sampling a few keys.

Your CDN cache has plenty of capacity but a poor hit rate. What do the textbook policies not model?

Size and cost. Every policy in this chapter assumes entries are interchangeable — one slot, one item — but a CDN holds objects from a few kilobytes to gigabytes, with wildly different costs to fetch again. Evicting one 10 MB video to admit ten 1 MB images may raise the hit count and lower the bytes served from cache, or the reverse, depending on what you are optimising. Real policies therefore score candidates by something like cost per byte per unit of recency, and admission control — deciding whether an object deserves to enter at all — often matters more than eviction.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Hit rate is not linear in value. 90% to 99% saves more time than a perfect cache would cost you in total.
  2. Every policy is a bet on an access pattern, and each one has a pattern that defeats it.
  3. LRU bets on recency and loses completely to a loop or a scan — 0% on a loop of 11 through a cache of 10.
  4. MRU is not absurd. On that same loop it matches Belady's optimum exactly, at 89.6%.
  5. LFU bets on stable popularity, beating LRU by 13 points on Zipf and polluting the cache when tastes change.
  6. Belady's optimum is a yardstick, not an algorithm, and the size of the gap tells you whether a better policy exists.
  7. FIFO can get worse with a bigger cache — 9 misses at 3 frames, 10 at 4.
  8. Stack algorithms cannot do that, because a bigger cache always contains everything a smaller one holds.
  9. Nobody implements true LRU. CLOCK, pseudo-LRU and sampling all trade exactness for keeping the hot path cheap.
  10. Real caches have variable entry sizes and costs, which none of the classic policies model.
