Security foundations

An eight-character password from the full printable set survives less than a day against MD5 on one GPU, and about ten and a half thousand years against bcrypt at cost 12. The password did not change. Everything that matters here is about choosing the right primitive.

Encoding, hashing, encryption

These three get confused constantly, and the confusion is load-bearing in real vulnerabilities. They have different purposes and only one of them is a security mechanism on its own.

Three operations that look similar and are not

Base64 is not encryption, and a JWT is not encrypted

Base64 exists to move binary through channels that expect text — it is chapter 4's problem, not a security one, and anyone can decode it instantly. The most common real-world instance of this confusion is the JSON Web Token: the header and payload of a standard JWT are base64-encoded, not encrypted. Paste one into any decoder and read it. The signature proves the token was not altered; it does nothing to hide what is inside. Putting anything confidential in a JWT payload is putting it in public.

Cryptographic hashes

A cryptographic hash must satisfy three properties, and they are progressively harder to guarantee.

Pre-image resistance
Given h, it is infeasible to find any m with hash(m) = h. This is what makes a hash one-way.
Second pre-image resistance
Given m, it is infeasible to find a different m′ with the same hash.
Collision resistance
It is infeasible to find any pair colliding. This is the weakest link, because chapter 25's birthday bound means it costs only 2n/2 rather than 2n.

That square root is why hash widths are what they are. A 256-bit hash has a collision bound of 2128 ≈ 3.4 × 10³⁸, which is the actual security level. SHA-1's collision resistance was broken in 2017 at a cost of about 263 ≈ 9.2 × 10¹⁸ operations — real money, but achievable — which is why git and everything else moved to SHA-256.

Storing passwords

The whole problem is that a general-purpose hash is designed to be fast, and speed is exactly what an attacker with your database wants.

Three findings come out of the arithmetic, and they are worth stating separately.

Length beats complexity. Twelve lowercase letters give 9.5 × 10¹⁶ combinations; eight characters from the full 95-symbol set give 6.6 × 10¹⁵ — the simpler, longer password is fourteen times stronger. Six diceware words carry 77.5 bits of entropy against 78.8 for twelve random printable characters, and one of those is memorable.

The hash choice dominates everything. Eight full-set characters fall to MD5 in under a day and survive bcrypt at cost 12 for about 10,500 years. That factor of ten million comes entirely from the work factor, and no password policy can substitute for it.

Salt does not slow down a single guess. It stops the attacker amortising. Without salt, cracking one million stolen hashes costs the same as cracking one, because every candidate is tested against all of them at once; with a unique salt each, it costs a million separate searches. A precomputed rainbow table for all 8-character lowercase MD5 hashes needs 2.09 × 10¹¹ entries and about 3.3 TB — entirely feasible. The same table for every 16-byte salt would need 7.1 × 10⁴⁹ entries, which is not.

The rules, in order

Use Argon2id, or scrypt or bcrypt if you cannot. Use the library's defaults for the work factor and raise them as hardware improves — the parameter exists to be tuned upward over the decades. Never invent a scheme; never store the password reversibly; never log it; never email it. Enforce a minimum length rather than composition rules, and check candidates against a breached-password list, because "P@ssw0rd1!" satisfies every complexity policy ever written and is in every wordlist.

Symmetric and public-key cryptography

Symmetric

One key encrypts and decrypts. AES is the standard, and it is fast — gigabytes per second with hardware support. The problem it cannot solve is getting the key to the other party.

Asymmetric

A public key encrypts and a private key decrypts, or a private key signs and a public key verifies. RSA and elliptic curves. Orders of magnitude slower, and it solves exactly the key-distribution problem symmetric cannot.

Every real protocol uses both. TLS does asymmetric work once to agree a symmetric session key, then encrypts the actual traffic symmetrically — chapter 28's handshake. Signal, SSH and PGP all do the same, because asymmetric is too slow for bulk data and symmetric cannot bootstrap itself.

The mode and the nonce are where it goes wrong

AES encrypts a 16-byte block; the mode decides how blocks are chained, and the choice is not a detail. ECB encrypts each block independently, so identical plaintext blocks produce identical ciphertext blocks — the famous encrypted penguin image is still recognisably a penguin. Use an authenticated mode such as GCM, which provides integrity as well as confidentiality: without it, an attacker who cannot read your ciphertext can often still modify it usefully.

And a nonce or IV must be unique per key, never reused. Reusing a GCM nonce with the same key does not weaken the encryption slightly; it can reveal the plaintext outright and leak the authentication key. This is the single most common way a correct algorithm is deployed insecurely.

Signatures invert the operation: sign with the private key, verify with the public one. That gives authenticity — this came from the holder of the private key — and non-repudiation. Certificates are signed statements binding a public key to an identity, and a chain of them ends at a root your system already trusts. The entire web PKI is that chain, plus the difficult social problem of deciding who the roots are.

Why escaping is not equivalent

Parameterisation works because the query and the data travel over separate channels: the database parses the query once, with a placeholder, and the value is bound afterwards where it can never be parsed as syntax. Escaping tries to make dangerous characters safe, and it fails whenever the context changes — an unquoted numeric parameter needs no quote to break out of, so id = 1 OR 1=1 defeats any amount of quote-escaping. The parameterised skeleton is identical for every input in the demo, including all four attacks. That invariance is the security property.

The same shape appears everywhere: command injection when input reaches a shell, XSS when it reaches HTML, LDAP, XPath and template injection in their respective parsers. The fix is always the same in kind — keep data out of the code channel — and the specific mechanism differs: parameterised queries, argument arrays rather than shell strings, and context-aware output encoding for HTML.

Broken access control is worth singling out, because it is now the most common category and it is the one no library fixes for you. The typical instance is an endpoint like GET /invoices/1042 that checks the user is logged in and forgets to check the invoice is theirs. Changing the number in the URL is the entire attack. Authentication answers "who are you"; authorisation answers "may you do this to that", and the second question has to be asked on every request, for every object.

Principles that generalise

  1. Defence in depth. Assume each layer will fail. A WAF is not a reason to skip parameterisation.
  2. Least privilege. The application's database user does not need DROP TABLE, and giving it that right is what turns an injection into a catastrophe.
  3. Fail closed. When authorisation cannot be determined, deny. An exception in a permission check must not fall through to "allowed".
  4. Never roll your own crypto. Not the algorithm, not the protocol, not the password scheme. The failures are invisible until they are exploited.
  5. Validate on the server, always. Client-side checks are a usability feature; anything the client enforces, an attacker can disable.
  6. Keep secrets out of source control, and remember that git history is forever — a committed key must be rotated, not deleted.
  7. Prefer boring, current defaults. Most breaches exploit known problems with published fixes, not novel cryptanalysis.
Kerckhoffs's principle

A system must remain secure even if everything about it except the key is public. Security through obscurity fails because obscurity is not a property you control: source leaks, binaries are decompiled, and protocols are reverse-engineered. Design as though the attacker has read your code — because in the cases that matter, they have.

Check yourself

Is a JWT encrypted?

No. A standard JWT's header and payload are base64-encoded, which is a text representation anyone can reverse instantly. The signature proves the token has not been altered and came from someone holding the signing key — that is integrity and authenticity, not confidentiality. Anything you put in a JWT payload is readable by anyone who holds the token, including the user and anyone who intercepts it. If you need the contents hidden, you need JWE, or you need to keep the data server-side and put only an opaque identifier in the token.

Which is stronger: 8 characters from the full printable set, or 12 lowercase letters?

The 12 lowercase letters, by a factor of about fourteen. 95⁸ = 6.6 × 10¹⁵ against 26¹² = 9.5 × 10¹⁶. Length enters the exponent and alphabet size only the base, so adding characters wins far more than adding character classes. This is why modern guidance sets a minimum length and drops composition rules, and why six diceware words — 77.5 bits — beat twelve random printable characters at 78.8 bits for all practical purposes while being memorable.

Why is SHA-256 the wrong hash for passwords, even with a salt?

Because it is designed to be fast, and speed is what the attacker wants. A single GPU does roughly 10 billion SHA-256 hashes a second, so an 8-character full-set password falls in a few days; bcrypt at cost 12 does about ten thousand a second and the same password takes 10,500 years. The salt does not help with this at all — it prevents amortisation across accounts, not the speed of one guess. Password hashes need a deliberately expensive function with a tunable work factor: Argon2id, scrypt or bcrypt.

What does a salt actually prevent?

Amortisation. Without salt, every candidate password is hashed once and compared against your entire stolen database, so cracking a million hashes costs the same as cracking one. With a unique salt per user, each account requires its own search — a million separate efforts. It also kills precomputation: a rainbow table for all 8-character lowercase MD5 hashes needs 2.09 × 10¹¹ entries at about 3.3 TB, which is buildable; the same table for every possible 16-byte salt would need 7.1 × 10⁴⁹ entries, which is not. What salt does not do is slow down a single guess.

What does the admin' -- payload do, and why does escaping not reliably stop it?

It does not add anything — it removes the password check. The concatenated query becomes … WHERE name = 'admin' --' AND password = '…', and everything after -- is a comment, so the skeleton loses its AND PASSWORD = ? clause and the login succeeds as admin. Escaping quotes would stop this particular payload but not the class: an unquoted numeric parameter such as id = 1 OR 1=1 needs no quote to escape from. Parameterisation works because the query and the data travel over separate channels, so the parameterised skeleton is identical for every input — including all the attacks.

Why is reusing a nonce more dangerous than it sounds?

Because it is not a gradual weakening. In a counter mode such as AES-GCM, encrypting two different messages with the same key and nonce produces two ciphertexts whose XOR equals the XOR of the plaintexts — the keystream cancels out and the messages can often be recovered outright. Worse, GCM nonce reuse can leak the authentication subkey, letting an attacker forge valid ciphertexts. This is why nonce handling is the most common way a correct algorithm is deployed insecurely, and why misuse-resistant modes and library-managed nonces exist.

Your endpoint GET /invoices/1042 requires a valid session. Is that sufficient?

No — that is authentication, and what is missing is authorisation. The endpoint has established who the caller is and not whether this invoice is theirs, so changing the number in the URL reads someone else's data. This is broken access control, currently the most common category in the OWASP Top 10, and no framework fixes it for you: the check must be made server-side, on every request, against the specific object. Hiding the link in the UI does nothing, because the UI is not what makes the request.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Encoding is not encryption, and a JWT payload is base64 — readable by anyone holding the token.
  2. Collision resistance costs 2n/2, not 2n, which is why 256-bit hashes exist and SHA-1 fell at 2⁶³.
  3. Length beats complexity. 12 lowercase letters are 14× stronger than 8 from the full printable set.
  4. The hash choice dominates the password policy — under a day against MD5, 10,500 years against bcrypt cost 12.
  5. Salt stops amortisation, not guessing. Unsalted, a million hashes cost the same as one.
  6. Every real protocol uses both kinds of cryptography: asymmetric to agree a key, symmetric for the data.
  7. The mode and the nonce are where crypto fails, and nonce reuse can reveal plaintext outright.
  8. Injection is data crossing into the code channel, and O'Brien triggers it by accident.
  9. Parameterisation works because the skeleton never changes, which escaping cannot guarantee.
  10. Broken access control is the biggest category and the one no library fixes: authorise every request, for every object.
