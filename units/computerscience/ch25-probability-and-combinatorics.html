Counting

Every probability question begins as a counting question, and there are only a few shapes.

The four basic counts
QuestionFormulaExample
Independent choices in sequencea × b × c3 shirts, 4 trousers, 2 hats → 24 outfits
Arrangements of all nn!10 items in a row → 3,628,800 orders
Ordered picks of k from nP(n,k) = n!/(n−k)!Podium from 10 runners → 720
Unordered picks of k from nC(n,k) = P/k!Committee of 3 from 10 → 120; poker hands → 2,598,960

The only question you have to answer correctly is does order matter? A podium is ordered, a committee is not, and the two differ by exactly k! — 720 against 120 for the same 10 people. Note also C(n,k) = C(n,n−k): choosing 3 to include is the same as choosing 17 to exclude, which is often the cheaper computation.

The pigeonhole principle is the crudest tool here and one of the most useful: n+1 items in n boxes forces a shared box. It is why 366 people guarantee a shared birthday, why chapter 13's hash collisions are inevitable, and why lossless compression cannot shrink every input.

The birthday problem

The pigeonhole bound says 366 people. The interesting number is much smaller, and the trick is to count the complement — collisions are hard to count directly, non-collisions are easy.

count what does not happen
P(no collision with n people, m days)
    = (m/m) x ((m-1)/m) x ((m-2)/m) x … x ((m-n+1)/m)

P(at least one collision) = 1 - that

m = 365:   n = 22 -> 47.6%      n = 23 -> 50.7%

The rule of thumb is worth memorising: the 50% point sits at about 1.177 × √m. For 365 days that is 22.5, which rounds to the exact answer of 23. It is not an approximation that degrades at scale — for 64, 4,096 and 65,536 buckets it gives 10, 76 and 302, and each is exactly right.

Why this governs hash sizes

Collisions arrive at √m, not at m, so a hash's usable capacity is the square root of its space. A 64-bit hash collides after about 5.1 billion items — reachable by a large system in a day. A 128-bit hash needs 2.2 × 10¹⁹. Halving the bit width does not halve the safety margin; it square-roots it. This is why git moved from SHA-1 to SHA-256, why UUIDs are 128 bits, and why chapter 13's hash tables collide far sooner than intuition suggests.

Expectation, and its limits

The expectation of a random variable is the average over all outcomes weighted by probability. Its most useful property is linearity: E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y], whether or not X and Y are independent. That exception-free rule turns many hard calculations into easy ones.

the coupon collector, solved by linearity
How many random draws to see all n distinct coupons?

Split into phases. In phase i you already have i coupons, so each
draw is new with probability (n-i)/n, and the expected number of
draws in that phase is n/(n-i) — the mean of a geometric variable.

E[total] = Σ n/(n-i)  for i = 0..n-1
         = n x (1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … + 1/n)
         = n · H(n) ≈ n ln n + 0.577n

n = 50:  50 x 4.499 = 225.0 draws

The phases are not independent — how long one takes affects nothing about the next, but they share the same coupon pool — and linearity does not care. That is the whole reason the derivation is three lines.

Across 4,000 coupon-collector trials with 50 coupons the mean came out at 224.5 against a theoretical 225.0 — but the median was 213, the best trial finished in 98 draws and the worst took 533. The distribution is right-skewed, so more than half of all runs beat the average and a minority are dramatically worse. Reporting only the mean would have described almost nobody's experience.

The mean is not the typical case

This is the single most common misuse of expectation in engineering. Average latency, average queue depth and average cache miss cost are all means over skewed distributions, and the mean of a skewed distribution sits above the median and below the tail. It is why service level objectives are written as p95 and p99 rather than as averages: the 95th percentile of those coupon trials was 340, half again the mean. When someone quotes an average, ask for the distribution.

Bounding the tail

If the mean is not enough, what can you say about how far from it you might land? Three bounds, each requiring more information and each giving more in return.

The concentration ladder, with measurements from the coupon trials
BoundNeedsSaysAt 2× the mean
MarkovOnly the mean, and X ≥ 0P(X ≥ a) ≤ μ/aAllows 50%; measured 0.40%
ChebyshevMean and varianceP(|X−μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k²At 2σ allows 25%; measured 4.55%
ChernoffA sum of independent variablesDeviation probability falls exponentiallyTypically astronomically small

Both bounds held on every measurement, and both were extremely loose. That is the point of them: they are guarantees requiring almost no knowledge of the distribution, not predictions. Markov knows only the mean and still promises you will not exceed twice it more than half the time — a promise that is true of every non-negative random variable in existence, which is precisely why it cannot be tight.

Chernoff is the one that does real work in algorithm analysis, because it applies to sums of independent variables and gives exponential decay. It is why "run it k times and take the majority" turns a barely-better-than-chance algorithm into a reliable one, and why a load balancer distributing n requests over n servers has a maximum load of Θ(log n / log log n) rather than something alarming.

Randomised algorithms

Randomness is not a last resort. Deliberately introducing it converts a guarantee about the input into a guarantee about your own coin flips, which is a much better thing to depend on.

Las Vegas

Always correct, random running time. Randomised quicksort always sorts; how long it takes depends on the pivots. You are betting time, never correctness.

Monte Carlo

Bounded running time, small chance of being wrong. Miller-Rabin runs in fixed time and may call a composite prime — with probability you choose.

Randomised quicksort

Chapter 19 showed quicksort with a first-element pivot doing 499,500 comparisons on 1,000 sorted values — its worst case, triggered by the most ordinary input there is. Choose the pivot at random and the expected count becomes 2(n+1)H(n) − 4n = 10,986, independent of the input. Across 300 measured runs the mean was 10,970, the best 9,653 and the worst 12,540 — a spread of under 1.2×.

The worst case is still Θ(n²). What changed is what triggers it: no longer a property of the data that an adversary can supply, but a run of unlucky coin flips whose probability is astronomically small. That is the whole trade, and it is the same one hash seeding made in chapter 13.

Miller-Rabin

Fermat's little theorem says ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p) for prime p, which suggests a test. It fails: 561 = 3 × 11 × 17 is composite and satisfies the congruence for every base coprime to it — a Carmichael number. Bases 2, 4, 5, 7 and 8 all pass, and the test learns nothing.

Miller-Rabin strengthens the check by also looking for non-trivial square roots of 1, and catches 561 with base 2 immediately. Each round has at most a ¼ chance of being fooled by a composite, so k rounds give error 4−k:

And for bounded ranges it stops being probabilistic at all: bases 2, 3, 5 and 7 are provably exact for every number below 3.2 million, which the harness confirms by checking all 9,999 odd numbers under 20,000 against trial division — zero disagreements.

Using this without getting it wrong

  1. Count the complement. "At least one" is almost always easier as 1 − P(none), which is the entire birthday derivation.
  2. Use linearity of expectation aggressively. It holds without independence, and it turns most "expected number of X" questions into a sum of indicator variables.
  3. Never report a mean alone for a skewed quantity. Give the median and a high percentile, or the distribution.
  4. Treat a bound as a guarantee, not a prediction. Markov allowing 50% while the truth is 0.4% is the bound working correctly.
  5. Check independence before assuming it. Correlated failures are why "three nines each, so nine nines together" is wrong when all three services share a data centre.
  6. Randomise the algorithm, not the input. That is what converts an adversary's advantage into your own coin flips.
Conditional probability is where intuition fails hardest

A test is 99% accurate and a disease affects 1 in 10,000. A positive result means roughly a 1% chance of having it, not 99% — because among a million people there are 100 true positives and about 10,000 false ones. This is Bayes' theorem, and the failure mode is ignoring the base rate. The same error appears constantly in engineering: an anomaly detector with a 0.1% false positive rate, run against ten million events, produces ten thousand false alarms a day, and if genuine anomalies are rare then nearly every alert is noise. Rare events make accurate tests useless.

Check yourself

Why does the birthday problem need only 23 people when there are 365 days?

Because it asks about pairs, not people. 23 people form C(23,2) = 253 pairs, and each is a chance to collide. Counting the complement makes it exact: P(no collision) is the product (365/365)(364/365)…(343/365), which falls below one half at 23 — precisely 50.7%, against 47.6% at 22. The general rule is that the 50% point is about 1.177 × √m, so collisions arrive at the square root of the space rather than anywhere near its size.

Why is a 64-bit hash considered too narrow for collision resistance?

Because its usable capacity is the square root of its space. A 64-bit hash collides with even chance after about 1.177 × 2³² ≈ 5.1 billion items — a figure a large system can reach in a day, and an attacker can reach deliberately. A 128-bit hash needs 2.2 × 10¹⁹. The critical intuition is that halving the width does not halve the margin, it square-roots it, which is why git moved to SHA-256 and why UUIDs are 128 bits rather than 64.

Why is linearity of expectation so useful, and what makes it unusual?

Because it holds without independence, which almost nothing else in probability does. E[X+Y] = E[X]+E[Y] even when X and Y are entangled, so you can decompose a messy quantity into indicator variables, take each one's expectation separately, and add. The coupon collector falls out in three lines this way: split into phases, each phase has expected length n/(n−i), sum to get n·H(n) = 225 for n = 50. Doing it without linearity means confronting the joint distribution, which is far harder.

Collecting 50 coupons takes 225 draws on average. What does that not tell you?

Almost everything about the spread. Across 4,000 trials the median was 213 — below the mean, because the distribution is right-skewed — the best run finished in 98 draws and the worst took 533, well over twice the mean. The 95th percentile was 340. More than half of all runs beat the average, and a minority were dramatically worse, so the mean describes almost nobody's experience. This is exactly why latency objectives are written as p95 and p99.

Markov's bound allows 50% where the measured value is 0.40%. Is the bound useless?

No — it is doing exactly what it is for. Markov requires only that the variable is non-negative and that you know its mean; it knows nothing else about the distribution, so its promise must hold for every non-negative variable in existence, including the worst-behaved. That universality is what makes it loose. Adding the variance gets you Chebyshev, which was tighter here (25% allowed against 4.55% measured), and adding independence gets you Chernoff and exponential decay. Bounds are guarantees, not predictions.

What is the difference between Las Vegas and Monte Carlo, and which is quicksort?

Las Vegas is always correct with a random running time; Monte Carlo has bounded running time and a small chance of being wrong. Randomised quicksort is Las Vegas: it always sorts, and the pivots decide how long it takes — measured across 300 runs on 1,000 elements, 9,653 to 12,540 comparisons around an expected 10,986. Miller-Rabin is Monte Carlo: fixed time, and an error probability of 4−k that you choose. You can usually convert Monte Carlo to Las Vegas by verifying the answer and retrying, if verification is cheap.

A 99% accurate test for a disease affecting 1 in 10,000 comes back positive. What is the chance you have it?

About 1%. In a million people, 100 have the disease and 99 of them test positive; the other 999,900 are healthy and about 9,999 of them test positive anyway. So roughly 10,098 positives, of which 99 are real — under 1%. The error is ignoring the base rate: when the condition is rare, false positives from the huge healthy population swamp true positives from the tiny sick one. The same arithmetic governs anomaly detectors, spam filters and fraud alerts, and it is why a "highly accurate" detector against rare events produces almost nothing but noise.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. The only counting question that matters is whether order matters, and the two answers differ by exactly k!.
  2. Count the complement. "At least one" is nearly always easier as 1 − P(none).
  3. Collisions arrive at √m, not m — 23 people for 365 days, and 1.177 × √m in general.
  4. A hash's usable capacity is the square root of its space, so 64 bits buys 5.1 billion items and 128 buys 2.2 × 10¹⁹.
  5. Linearity of expectation holds without independence, which is what makes the coupon collector a three-line derivation.
  6. The mean of a skewed distribution is not the typical case. 225 expected, 213 median, 98 best, 533 worst.
  7. Markov, Chebyshev and Chernoff are guarantees, not predictions, and their looseness is the price of assuming almost nothing.
  8. Las Vegas risks time, Monte Carlo risks correctness, and you can often convert the second into the first.
  9. Randomising the algorithm moves the worst case from the input to your coin flips, which is the entire point.
  10. Base rates dominate rare-event testing. A 99% accurate test for a 1-in-10,000 condition is right about 1% of the time it fires.
