CHAPTER 18

Incidents and debugging

Scoring the new model against the old one — the thing everyone reaches for — gains 0.386 bits for 45 minutes, which is 23 times less efficient than glancing at the health dashboard. And rolling back restores serving in under a minute while the training data takes thirty days, which is how the next scheduled retrain quietly reintroduces the problem.

    ORIENTATION

    What makes an ML incident different

  1. The service is usually healthy. Chapter 1's central case: no errors, no latency, no failed deploys, and predictions that are quietly wrong. The ordinary incident signals are all green.
  2. The cause is in one of five layers, and they look identical from the outside: infrastructure, features, upstream data, the model itself, or the metric you are measuring it with.
  3. The metric can be the broken thing. A label pipeline failure looks exactly like a model regression, and treating it as one leads to a rollback that changes nothing.
  4. Rollback is incomplete. Shifting traffic back does not remove what the bad model wrote into your data, which is the second demo below.
  5. The clock is set by detection, not response. Chapter 16 measured detection times spanning a factor of 5,700; the response minutes are the smaller term for most failures, but they are the one you control.

    The bits-gained column spans 0.342 to 0.493 — a narrow range. The bits-per-minute column spans 0.0086 to 0.1948, a factor of 23.

    So the discriminating question is not "which test tells me the most" but "which test tells me nearly as much for two minutes instead of forty-five". Scoring the new model against the old is the instinctive first move and the single worst use of the first hour.

    Feature freshness is the most informative single test at 0.493 bits, which is worth noticing on its own: the most likely cause is a data problem, and the cheapest data check is also the most decisive one.

    The fast ordering is not free

    Ordering by bits per minute reaches a diagnosis in a median of 32 minutes against 67 for looking at the model first — 2.2× faster on the mean. But it identifies the cause correctly 73.9% of the time against 76.7%.

    The reason is straightforward once you see it: cheap tests individually tell you less, so you reach the confidence threshold on thinner evidence. The trade is 40 minutes for 2.8 points of accuracy.

    Which is usually worth taking, because a wrong first guess is not a wrong answer — it means you keep testing, while the incident runs. Given chapter 16's multiplication of severity by time, forty minutes off every incident buys more than three points of first-guess accuracy.

    A runbook that costs nothing to adopt

    The fixed order — health, freshness, skew, golden set, holdout, model comparison — reaches 37 minutes, close to the adaptive strategy's 32 and half of the model-first 67. You do not need anyone computing posteriors during an incident; you need the cheap checks written down above the expensive ones.

    RECOVERY

    What a rollback does not undo

    A bad model that ran for a week wrote a week of its own decisions into the data the next retrain will use.

    Excluding the span wins almost all the way out

    A model that ran badly for seven days contaminates 23% of a 30-day window. Excluding that week costs 28,000 rows of estimation error and is still five times better than keeping it.

    Excluding stays the better option out to 29 days at this severity — essentially the whole window. Only when you would be discarding nearly everything does the variance penalty catch up with the contamination.

    So the action item is concrete and rarely taken: after a bad model is rolled back, mark the affected span and exclude it from training until it falls out of the window. That needs the span recorded somewhere the retraining job reads, which is what chapter 11's lineage is for.

    The incident is not over when the traffic is shifted back

    Rollback restores serving in under a minute and the training data in thirty days. In between sits the next scheduled retrain, which will happily fit the contaminated window and reintroduce a version of the problem — with a clean deployment, a passing canary and no alert.

    And the last row is the one that does not appear on any dashboard. The feedback loop means a model that declined the wrong applications has permanently removed those outcomes from your data. The random-holdout fix from that chapter is the only thing that keeps a slice of the world unaffected by the model — and it is worth having in place before the incident, because it cannot be added retroactively.

    AFTERWARDS

    Post-mortem questions specific to ML

  6. How long did it run before anyone knew? This is the number that determined the cost, and it is the one worth attacking. Chapter 16: 96% of residual damage came from failures taking over ten hours.
  7. Which layer was it, and which layer did we look at first? If those differ, the runbook order is wrong.
  8. Would a check have caught it, and what would that check have cost in false alarms? "Add a monitor" is not free — chapter 16 measured twenty noisy monitors catching fewer incidents than five.
  9. What did the bad model write into the data, and have we excluded it? If nobody can answer, the next retrain is uncontrolled.
  10. Was the offline evaluation blind to this? If the model looked fine offline and failed online, the evaluation is the artefact to fix — chapter 7's point-in-time gap was exactly this, at 0.9487 offline against 0.3805 online.
  11. Could we have rolled back faster? If rollback required a deploy, that is a fixable minutes-per-incident cost.
  12. Is the fix a check, a process, or a design change? Checks catch recurrence; design changes prevent the class.
    13. The one question that is usually skipped

    "Would we have found this if it had been half as bad?" Almost every ML incident that gets a post-mortem is one that crossed a threshold. The same failure at half the severity is the one still running, and the answer to that question is the honest measure of your monitoring.

    Chapter 14 put a number on it: a 10% regression was caught 100% of the time and a 2% regression 63%, at a median of 4.3 hours.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about an incident process

  13. Is there a written diagnostic order? A fixed runbook reached 37 minutes against 67 for improvising from the model.
  14. Can you tell which model version served a given prediction? Without it, narrowing to a layer is guesswork.
  15. Does rollback need a deploy? If so, your rollback time is a deploy time.
  16. Do you record the affected span when a model misbehaves? The retraining job needs it, and nobody writes it down during the incident.
  17. Do you have a random holdout the model never touched? It cannot be added after the fact, and it is the only clean signal during an incident.
