Training as a pipeline step

    THE SHIFT

    A job, not a session

    Everything that makes interactive training pleasant makes it unschedulable.

    What has to change when training moves into a pipeline
    InteractiveAs a step
    ConfigurationEdited in placePassed in, and recorded
    OutputA variable in memoryAn artefact plus a model card
    FailureYou notice and rerunRetried automatically
    ProgressVisible in the notebookLogged, or invisible
    ResourcesWhatever the machine hasDeclared in advance
    DurationAs long as it takesBounded, or it holds a slot forever
    The two that bite immediately

    Automatic retries. Chapter 4's idempotence requirement applies with force: a training step that appends to a metrics table or writes to a fixed path produces a mess on the second attempt. Derive the output location from the input hashes and a retry overwrites its own previous attempt.

    A bounded duration. A training job with no timeout will eventually hang on a stuck data loader and hold a GPU slot until someone notices, which on a shared cluster is measured in days. A timeout that kills at 2× the expected duration costs nothing and prevents that entirely.

    What adding GPUs actually buys

    Synchronous data-parallel training: every step waits for the slowest worker, then pays a communication cost that grows with the worker count.

    The wall clock bottoms out at 32 GPUs. Sixty-four takes longer — 0.14 hours against 0.13 — and costs 2.1 times as much, because the communication cost has overtaken the shrinking per-worker compute.

    And the ceiling is 4.73× against a nominal 32×. Eight GPUs give 3.93× for 2.0× the money, which is usually the sensible trade; thirty-two give 4.73× for 5.6× the money, which usually is not.

    This is chapter 5's critical path in a different costume: a serial fraction you cannot remove sets a floor, and everything past it is billing.

    The two losses need opposite fixes

    At eight GPUs, 14% of the loss is waiting for the slowest worker. At sixty-four it is 2%, because communication has taken over completely.

    Stragglers want homogeneous hardware, balanced shards, and no other tenant on the node. Communication wants fewer and larger workers, gradient compression, or a bigger batch per worker.

    Applying the straggler fix to a communication-bound job does nothing, which is why the decomposition is worth computing before optimising. Measure the step time at one worker and at N, and the gap tells you which regime you are in.

    Grid returns the same answer at 27 runs and at 729

    0.8898 in every row. Twenty-seven times the compute, identical result — because a three-level grid gives you three distinct values of each hyperparameter whatever the budget, and every extra run buys resolution in a dimension that does nothing.

    Random search improves throughout: 0.9056 → 0.9145 → 0.9170 → 0.9195. The same budget buys 243 distinct values of the two dimensions that matter, rather than three.

    And it is not even monotone

    Five levels does worse than four, at four times the cost. Grid quality depends on whether the optimum happens to fall near a grid point, which is luck rather than resolution — and 46,656 runs at six levels still reaches only 0.9172, which 243 random runs already beat.

    The grid cannot be rescued by more compute. Its problem is structural: it allocates budget uniformly across dimensions, and the dimensions are not uniformly important.

    Early stopping, and its honest limit

    Running many configurations briefly and keeping the best half each round explores 512 configurations on a budget of 64 full runs, and reaches 0.9175 against random's 0.9145.

    But the advantage shrinks as the budget grows: +0.0100, +0.0031, +0.0012, −0.0001 at budgets of 27, 64, 125 and 243. Early stopping is worth most exactly when you cannot afford many full runs — which is the situation it is usually proposed for, and also the reason it looks less impressive in a benchmark with a generous budget.

    DURABILITY

    Checkpointing, briefly

    Chapter 4 did the arithmetic. This is what it means inside a training job.

  1. The numbers from chapter 4 apply directly. A 400-minute job with no checkpoints expects 5,360 minutes of wall clock at one failure per hundred minutes; with twenty checkpoints, 443.
  2. Checkpoint the optimiser state, not just the weights. Resuming with a fresh optimiser restarts momentum and the learning-rate schedule, which is a different training run wearing the same name.
  3. Include the data loader position. Otherwise a resume replays the same examples and the epoch structure quietly changes — chapter 2's ordering nondeterminism, introduced by your own recovery.
  4. Write to a new file, then rename. A checkpoint written in place and interrupted is a corrupt checkpoint, and you find out at the worst moment.
  5. Keep the last few, not all of them. But keep the one the registered model came from, for as long as the model — chapter 3's retention argument.

    6. WHAT IT PRODUCES

    The output of a training step

    Weights are the least interesting part.

    train_step_outputs/
    model.bin                 the weights
model_card.json           chapter 3's document: inputs, metrics, interface
metrics.json              per-epoch curves, not just the final number
feature_schema.json       what serving must supply, and in what form
requirements.lock         the exact environment, hashed
resource_usage.json       peak memory, GPU-hours, step timings
sample_predictions.parquet  a few hundred scored rows, for comparison later
    The last two are the ones people leave out

    Resource usage is what lets you size the next run rather than guessing, and it is the input to the scaling table above. Peak memory in particular is the number that decides whether a retrain fits on the same hardware after the feature set grew.

    Sample predictions are how you compare two models on the same inputs later. When a retrained model behaves oddly, scoring the old sample through the new model takes a minute; reconstructing what the old model would have said takes an afternoon, and chapter 2 says you may not manage it at all.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a training job

  6. Has anyone measured the scaling curve? If the worker count was chosen by feel, it may be past the point where more is slower.
  7. Are you straggler-bound or communication-bound? 14% against 2% here, and the fixes are opposite.
  8. Does it have a timeout? A hung job holds a GPU slot until a human notices.
  9. Is the search a grid? If so, it is returning the same answer it returned at a twentieth of the budget.
  10. Does the step emit resource usage and sample predictions? Both are nearly free to produce and expensive to reconstruct.
