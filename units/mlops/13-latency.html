CHAPTER 13

Latency, throughput and cost

A model that computes in 20 ms returns a p99 of 461 ms at 80% utilisation. Split the same traffic across eight servers at the same 80% and the p99 falls to 102 ms — nothing got faster, the queue just stopped being blocked by whichever request happened to be long.

    Throughput barely moves while latency explodes

    Going from 50% to 80% utilisation buys 60% more throughput and costs 2.5× the mean latency. Going on to 95% buys another 19% and costs another 4×.

    At 95% the p99 is 87 times the actual compute time. The server is idle five per cent of the time, and a request at the tail still waits 1,741 ms for 20 ms of work.

    The simulated mean matches S/(1−ρ) at every utilisation, so this is the standard result rather than a quirk of the simulation — but it is worth seeing in milliseconds rather than as a formula, because the formula does not convey how sharp the corner is.

    The mean is a number nobody experiences

    The ratio is 4.6× even at thirty per cent load

    A dashboard showing average latency is showing a number that roughly nobody experiences, and it is off by the same factor at light load as at heavy load. Watching the mean will not warn you about the tail, because the mean does not lead the tail — they move together, and the tail is always several times higher.

    This matters more than it sounds when a request fans out. A page that makes ten model calls in parallel waits for the slowest, so it experiences roughly the p90 of a single call on every page load — and a service whose p99 is 461 ms will produce visibly slow pages long before anyone's average looks wrong.

    Pooling is free tail latency

    Every server here is the same speed and every configuration runs at the same 80% utilisation. Eight servers give a p99 of 102 ms against one server's 461.

    Nothing got faster. A long request now blocks one server out of eight rather than the only one, so the queue behind it drains through the other seven.

    The practical consequence: splitting a fleet into per-tenant or per-region instances throws this away. Four separate single-server deployments at 80% have a 461 ms p99; the same four machines behind one queue have 137. That is the usual reason a sharded service has a worse tail than its capacity suggests, and it is invisible if you only look at utilisation.

    Above 105 req/s, batching is not an optimisation

    Unbatched, this server tops out at 105 requests per second — 8 ms of fixed cost dominates 1.5 ms of work. A batch of 64 sustains 615, and the ceiling approaches 1/perItem = 667 req/s as the fixed cost is fully amortised.

    So there is a traffic level above which batching is the only way to serve the load at all, and it is much lower than people expect. The latency discussion below only applies underneath that ceiling.

    The control is the timeout, not the batch size

    At 20 req/s a maximum batch of 64 fills to 1.2 on average and costs almost nothing: a p99 of 22.5 ms against no batching's 22.3. A large maximum is nearly free at low traffic, because the batch simply never fills.

    What costs you is the timeout. Holding for 50 ms instead of 2 takes the p99 from 18.8 to 64.5 ms and buys 3.24 ms per item. That is a rate you can read off directly and trade against your budget.

    Which corrects the usual worry. Setting a generous maximum batch size is safe; setting a generous timeout is the decision that needs justifying.

    So a fixed batch size is wrong at some traffic level

    The optimum doubles with each step: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. A batch size tuned during a load test is tuned for load-test traffic, and the setting that survives a quiet Sunday is not the one that survives Monday morning.

    This is the argument for adaptive batching — take whatever is queued, up to a generous maximum, with a short timeout. That policy lands near the optimum at every row of this table without anyone tuning it, which is exactly what the simulation above implements.

    COMPOSING

    Adding up a latency budget

  1. Budget at the p99, not the mean. The ratio was 4.6× at every load level here, so a budget built from averages is out by roughly that factor before you start.
  2. Feature fetch is usually the biggest term. A model that computes in 20 ms and fetches features in 40 is a feature-store problem wearing an inference costume.
  3. Serial calls add; parallel calls take the maximum. Ten parallel calls means every request experiences roughly the p90 of one call, which is worse than the mean by more than people expect.
  4. Leave utilisation headroom deliberately. The corner between 80% and 95% is where a routine traffic increase turns into an incident, and the throughput on offer past 80% is small.
  5. Put a deadline on the inference call itself. A request that has already blown its budget should fail to the fallback rather than complete slowly — and chapter 12's warning applies: count the fallback rate or you will not notice.

    6. JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a serving deployment

  6. What utilisation are you running at? Between 80% and 95% the p99 goes from 23× to 87× the compute time.
  7. Is there one queue or several? Eight pooled servers gave 102 ms against 461 for one, at identical utilisation.
  8. Do you watch the p99 or the mean? The gap is 4.6× even at light load.
  9. What is your unbatched ceiling? Here it was 105 req/s, which is far lower than the hardware suggests.
  10. Is the batch timeout justified? A large maximum is nearly free; the timeout is what costs 46 ms of p99.
