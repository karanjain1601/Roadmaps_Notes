CHAPTER 16

Monitoring at three levels

A serving crash costs £2,400 an hour and £432 a year. Concept drift costs £340 an hour and £173,400, because it runs for a week before anyone notices. Damage is severity multiplied by detection time, and detection time varies across these failures by a factor of 5,700 while cost per hour varies by eleven.

    The bottom row is the problem

    System monitoring exists because the platform team built it for every service. Data monitoring exists if someone wrote it. Model monitoring exists if labels arrive, which for most systems means weeks later or not at all.

    So the level that sees everything is the one that is usually missing, and the level that is always present is the one that goes green while the model is quietly failing — which is chapter 1's silent failure with a dashboard on top.

    The loudest failure costs the least

    A serving crash is the most expensive thing on the list per hour — £2,400 — and it costs £432 a year, because it is detected in two minutes. Concept drift costs less than a seventh as much per hour and does 401 times the annual damage.

    The reason is the multiplication. Cost per hour across this table varies by a factor of 11; detection time varies by a factor of 5,700. When one term ranges over three and a half orders of magnitude and the other over one, the ranking is set almost entirely by the first.

    Which means prioritising monitoring by "how bad is this failure" gets the order wrong. The question is how long would it run, and the answer for the worst offenders is bounded by how long labels take to arrive.

    The three levels are worth roughly the same

    £817,898, £754,360 and £744,600 — within 10% of each other. That is the useful correction, and it is not what the effort ratio suggests.

    System monitoring gets built first because the platform team already has it, and for many teams that is where the investment stops. It covers about a third of what is on the table.

    And even with all three in place, 96% of the remaining damage comes from failures that take over ten hours to detect. At that point, halving the detection time on covariate shift and concept drift is worth more than any number of additional fast checks.

    THE BINDING CONSTRAINT

    Labels arrive late, and that sets the floor

    Model monitoring compares predictions with outcomes. Everything about it is governed by when the outcome is known.

    Typical label delays, and what they imply
    PredictionLabel arrivesModel monitoring can detect drift in
    Will this click?SecondsHours
    Is this fraudulent?Days to weeksWeeks
    Will they churn this month?A monthTwo months
    Will this loan default?Months to yearsNever, usefully
    Two ways the delay is worse than it looks

    You need a window, not a label. Detecting a change needs enough labelled outcomes to see it, so the detection time is the label delay plus the accumulation period. A month-delayed label with a month of accumulation gives two months.

    Labels are biased by the model. If the model declines an application you never learn what would have happened, so the labelled data describes only what the model approved. The feedback loop from the data scientist unit is this exact mechanism, and it made true accuracy oscillate by 24 points while the dashboard read a correlation of 0.033 with the truth.

    The fix there was cheap and applies directly: approve a small random fraction regardless of the model. It cut the swing to 5.5 points, and it is the only way to get unbiased labels out of a system that acts on its own predictions.

    Proxies, in the order they are worth having

    Prediction distribution. Free, immediate, and catches a surprising share of real failures — a model whose score distribution has shifted is either seeing different inputs or behaving differently, and both are worth knowing about.

    Calibration on whatever labels have arrived. Partial and delayed, but chapter 22 found calibration caught damaging cases the input monitor missed entirely.

    Agreement with a cheap reference model. A simple model retrained frequently on recent data disagreeing more with production is a useful early signal, and it needs no labels at all.

    Business metrics by segment. Slow and noisy, but they are the thing you actually care about, and the segment breakdown often shows a problem before the aggregate does.

    Perfect coverage, and a third of the detection

    Twenty monitors cover 100% of the failure modes and catch 0.37 incidents a week. Five monitors cover 87% and catch 1.09 — three times as many.

    Every monitor here works exactly as designed. What they are all spending is the same shared resource, and nobody accounts for it: the rota's willingness to look. At thirty alerts a week the response rate is 19%, so the extra coverage arrives attached to a page nobody opens.

    The optimum in this configuration is 3 monitors. Not because coverage stops mattering, but because the binding constraint is attention rather than coverage — which is chapter 10's gate ratio and chapter 6's alarm budget, arriving a third time.

    The answer is not fewer monitors

    Twenty quiet monitors catch 1.75 incidents a week — better than twenty noisy ones at 0.37, and better than the best possible number of noisy monitors, which was three at 1.10.

    So you can have full coverage and a responsive rota at the same time. What you cannot have is full coverage with default thresholds. Reducing false alarms by a factor of seventeen bought back 59% more incidents caught than the best coverage-reduction could manage, and it did it without giving up a single failure mode.

    Which reframes the usual advice. "Too many alerts, delete some monitors" is the second-best answer; the first is to make each one fire only when something is wrong, and chapter 6 gives the arithmetic for doing that.

    PRACTICE

    What to alert on, and what to leave on a dashboard

  1. Page on symptoms, not causes. "Predictions are stale" is actionable at three in the morning; "feature table row count is 12% below the seven-day mean" is a dashboard line that generates the 30-alerts-a-week problem.
  2. Every page needs a runbook and an action. If the response is "look at it and probably nothing", it is not a page. That single rule removes most of the volume in the table above.
  3. Alert on the age of the newest prediction. Chapter 12's point: a batch failure produces no errors, no latency and no failed checks — only staleness.
  4. Alert on the fallback rate. An uncounted fallback is a model switched off that passes every health check.
  5. Use a deadline alert for absence. Chapter 5: a task-level alert cannot fire on a task that never started.
  6. Review fired alerts monthly and delete or tune the ones that led nowhere. The false-alarm rate is a maintained quantity, not a fixed property.
    7. The one dashboard worth having

    Per model: prediction volume, prediction distribution, fallback rate, feature freshness, p99 latency, and the newest label's age. Six lines, all cheap, and between them they cover the fast half of the damage table.

    The last one is the least common and the most revealing. A team that cannot say how old their newest label is does not know how long a concept drift would run — which the first demo says is the number that determines almost all of the cost.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a monitoring setup

  7. How long would concept drift run before you noticed? At seven days it was 401 times the annual cost of a crash.
  8. Which of the three levels do you actually have? They were worth £818k, £754k and £745k here — within 10% of each other.
  9. How old is your newest label? That number is the floor on model-monitoring detection time.
  10. How many alerts fire per week, and what share led to an action? At 7% precision the response rate was 19%.
  11. When did you last delete an alert? A false-alarm rate is maintained, not fixed.
