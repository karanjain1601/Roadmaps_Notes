    SCOPE

    What tracking is actually for

    Three jobs, and most tools do the first one well and the third one badly.

    A record of what happened
    Every run, its parameters, its metrics, its artefacts. Cheap, automatic, and the thing every tool provides.
    A basis for comparison
    Deciding which of two runs was better. This is where the statistics of the next two demos live, and where a dashboard that shows only point estimates actively misleads.
    An explanation of a difference
    Not "which run was better" but "why". Answerable only if the thing that differed was recorded — and the thing that differed is usually the training data, which is usually the thing not recorded.
    The distinction from chapter 3

    Chapter 3's model card describes the one model you deployed. Tracking describes the hundreds you did not.

    They overlap in what they record and differ in retention and in purpose: a registry entry outlives the model, and a tracking run is usually worth keeping for a few months. Recording the same input hashes in both is what lets you go from "this run looked good" to "and here is exactly what produced it".

    The winner's curse, in a dashboard

    The reported best overstates itself by 0.0013 at fifty configurations and 0.0016 at two hundred. Rerun the winner and it comes back at 0.8484 rather than 0.8497.

    Nothing broke, nothing is irreproducible in chapter 2's sense, and no seed changed meaningfully. The first number was simply a maximum taken over noise, so it selected the configuration that was partly the luckiest as well as good.

    This is the exaggeration factor from the data scientist unit, arriving through the tuning sweep. The more configurations you try, the higher the top of the board reads and the less of that height is real.

    One ratio decides all of it, and the dashboard looks identical either way

    When the configurations barely differ, the winner is the genuinely best one 6% of the time — sorting a table of noise. When they differ clearly it is 81%.

    The tracking UI is the same in both cases: a sorted list, a highlighted top row, a number to four decimal places. Nothing in it distinguishes a sweep that found something from a sweep that found nothing.

    Which is the practical recommendation of this chapter: log enough repeated runs to know your σ. Without it, every comparison on the dashboard is uninterpretable, and with it most of them become easy.

    Same budget, same code, opposite advice

    When the configurations genuinely differ, exploring wins: one run each across 64 configurations gives 0.84869, and every repeat costs quality. Chapter 8's advice holds.

    When they barely differ, it reverses — two repeats across 32 configurations beats 64 singles, and at τ/σ = 0.37 the optimum moves to four repeats, which raises the chance of picking the genuinely best configuration from 8% to 29%.

    Notice the "picked best" column rises monotonically with repeats in both cases. Replication always makes the selection better; the question is whether that is worth the configurations you gave up to buy it.

    And τ/σ is measurable before you tune

    σ: run one configuration five times with different seeds and take the spread. That is chapter 2's measurement, and it takes an afternoon of compute.

    τ: look at the spread of results in any sweep you have already run. It is an overestimate, since it contains σ too, but subtracting in quadrature fixes that.

    Two numbers you already have, and they decide whether your search strategy is right. Most teams never compute either, run each configuration once, and sort by the result.

    Logging defaults matters more than logging what you set

    You will remember that you changed the learning rate. You will not remember that the library's default weight decay changed in a minor version between March and June, and if only the parameters you explicitly passed were logged, that difference is invisible in the record.

    Log the resolved configuration — every parameter the run actually used, after defaults and overrides — rather than the arguments you supplied. It is usually one call, and it converts a class of unexplainable difference into a diff.

    The seed row is the one people skip

    Without recorded seeds you cannot tell a repeated configuration from a distinct one, which means you cannot estimate σ, which means the entire second demo is unavailable to you and every comparison on your dashboard is uninterpretable.

    It is one integer. It is the cheapest field in the table and the one that unlocks the most.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a tracking setup

  1. What is your run-to-run σ? If nobody knows, no comparison on the dashboard is interpretable.
  2. Has anyone reran the winner? It came back 0.0013 lower here, and that is the normal case rather than a fault.
  3. How many configurations were in the sweep? The shortfall grows with the count, and at 200 the winner is the best 51% of the time.
  4. Are seeds recorded? Without them σ is unmeasurable.
  5. Is the resolved configuration logged, or only the arguments passed? Defaults change between versions and you will not remember.
