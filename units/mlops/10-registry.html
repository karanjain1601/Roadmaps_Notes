CHAPTER 10

The model registry

Five approval gates, each 90 to 99 per cent specific on its own, pass only 78.8% of good candidates — and 71% of everything they block is a perfectly deployable model. Then comparing a candidate against the incumbent's recorded metric rejects twice as many genuine improvements, because that recorded figure is exactly why the incumbent was promoted.

    PURPOSE

    The question it exists to answer

    A registry is not storage. It is the answer to "what is running, and why that one".

    The separation that matters

    Chapter 8 split producing a model from registering one, and the registry is why. Every successful training run produces a candidate; almost none of them should ship.

    Merging the two means every green pipeline deploys, which removes the only place a human or a policy can intervene. Keeping them separate costs one extra step and creates the entire subject of this chapter.

    STRUCTURE

    Stages, and what each transition means

    Registered
    A candidate exists, with chapter 3's input hashes attached. No claim is being made about it. Cheap, automatic, and every training run should do this.
    Staged
    It has passed the automated gates and is running somewhere non-production — shadow traffic, a canary, an offline replay. This is where chapter 14's evidence gets collected.
    Production
    Serving. Exactly one model per role holds this at a time, and the registry is the authority on which. If your deployment system and your registry disagree, the registry is wrong and you have a problem worse than either.
    Archived
    Superseded but kept, with its inputs. This is what makes a rollback possible, and it is where the retention argument from chapter 3 bites: an archived model whose training snapshot was garbage-collected is an artefact you can no longer explain.
    Record the transition, not just the state

    "Model 9f2c is in production" answers less than "model 9f2c was promoted on 14 August by this pipeline, superseding 1d70, on this evidence". The second is a decision log, and it is what an incident review actually needs.

    The supersedes field from chapter 3's model card does most of this for free: it gives you the chain, so "what changed between the model that worked and the one that does not" becomes a diff of two documents.

    Chapter 1's conjunction, now as a cost

    Five gates cut the bad pass rate from 100% to 2.1%, which is the point of having them. They also pass only 78.8% of good candidates — more than a fifth of perfectly deployable models are stopped.

    Each gate is 90 to 99 per cent specific on its own. Five in series are 78.8%, for the same reason six mostly-fine components multiplied to 7.1% in chapter 1. The difference is that here the compounding is working against you.

    And because only 8% of candidates are bad, 71% of everything the gauntlet blocks is a good model. Accurate gates spend most of their blocking on the wrong candidates — which is the base-rate result from the data scientist unit, arriving at the approval step.

    Same gates, opposite verdicts

    At a 2% bad rate, 91% of blocks are false and the gauntlet is mostly an obstacle. At 50% it is 18% and the gauntlet is clearly earning its place.

    The gates are identical in both rows. What changed is the base rate — and almost nobody measures theirs, which means most gate suites are tuned by intuition about a number their team has never looked at.

    It is measurable: over the last year, how many candidate models would have caused a problem if shipped unchecked? If the answer is "two out of ninety", your gauntlet is blocking mostly good work.

    The last row is the uncomfortable one

    Manual review blocks 0.03 bad models for every good one it stops — thirty-three good candidates rejected per genuine catch, plus the review time itself.

    That is not an argument against human judgement. It is an argument against human judgement as a gate. A reviewer who sees every candidate and can block any of them has poor specificity by construction, because most candidates are fine and any doubt reads as a reason to hold.

    The same person is far more valuable choosing what the automated gates should check, or reviewing the ones that failed a gate. Order gates by that ratio, add them while it exceeds 1, and be suspicious of anything below it.

    THE DECISION

    Promoting on a comparison

    The cheap comparison jams the ratchet

    Comparing against the number already in the registry is free — you have it, and re-measuring the incumbent costs an evaluation run. It promotes 6.0 times in twenty cycles against re-measurement's 10.0, and ends at a true quality of 0.84934 against 0.85059.

    The mechanism is chapter 9's winner's curse, one step later. The incumbent's recorded figure is why it was promoted, so it is an inflated number, and every genuine improvement now has to clear a benchmark that got lucky. It rejects 2.4 real improvements against 1.1.

    The registry's own record drifts too: it sits 0.00199 above the truth rather than 0.00068. The stated quality of your production model becomes progressively more optimistic, one promotion at a time.

    The fix is one evaluation run per cycle. Re-measure the incumbent on the same fresh data as the candidate, and compare like with like.

    If deployment were free, you should promote greedily

    No margin wins outright on final quality, even though 2.7 of its ten promotions actually made the model worse. Those get corrected on a later cycle, and the extra chances to move up more than pay for them.

    That is the honest answer to "why not just promote on any improvement", and it is wrong for exactly one reason: deployments are not free. Each one carries risk the metric cannot see — an incident, a rollback, review time, users served by a worse model in the interim.

    So the margin is a price, not a statistical correction

    The best margin rises monotonically with the cost of deploying: 0.0000, 0.0005, 0.0010, 0.0020, 0.0040.

    Which means it should be set from what a deployment actually costs you, not chosen as a round number or copied from a blog post. A team that can deploy and roll back in ten minutes with a canary should genuinely use a smaller margin than a team whose deployment is a fortnightly release with a change board — and the arithmetic says how much smaller.

    THE PAYOFF

    What the registry makes possible

  1. Rollback is a stage change, not an archaeology project. The previous production model is already registered, already has its inputs recorded, and already passed the gates it needed to.
  2. "What is running" has one answer. If the deployment system and the registry can disagree, you have two sources of truth and neither is one.
  3. The promotion decision is auditable. Who, when, on what evidence, superseding what — the questions an incident review opens with.
  4. Retention is coupled to the model, not the calendar. Chapter 3's argument: keep a model's data snapshot as long as the model, or a rollback gives you an artefact you cannot explain.
  5. Serving reads the registry rather than a path. A deployment that hardcodes an artefact location works until someone moves the file, and then fails in a way the registry would have prevented.

    6. JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a registry

  6. What fraction of your candidates would actually harm production? The gate arithmetic is meaningless without it, and 2% versus 50% inverts the verdict.
  7. How many good models did your gates block last quarter? At an 8% bad rate, 71% of blocks are false.
  8. Does manual review block more bad models than good ones? Here it was 0.03 to 1.
  9. Do you re-measure the incumbent, or trust the recorded number? The recorded number is inflated by exactly the amount that got it promoted.
  10. Where does your promotion margin come from? If the answer is not "the cost of a deployment", it is a guess.

    11. Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. A registry answers "what is live and why", which storage and tracking do not.
    2. Producing a model and registering one are separate steps, or every green pipeline deploys.
    3. Record the transition, not just the state. A decision log is what an incident review needs.
    4. Five gates cut the bad pass rate from 100% to 2.1%.
    5. And pass only 78.8% of good candidates — chapter 1's conjunction working against you.
    6. 71% of everything the gauntlet blocks is a good model, at an 8% bad rate.
    7. At 2% it is 91%; at 50% it is 18%. Same gates, opposite verdicts.
    8. Almost nobody measures their base rate, and it is the number the whole design depends on.
    9. Order gates by bad-blocked per good-blocked; the metric threshold scores 2.03.
    10. Manual review scores 0.03 — thirty-three good candidates stopped per genuine catch.
    11. Comparing against the recorded metric promotes 6.0 times against 10.0.
    12. Because the recorded figure is why the incumbent was promoted, so it is inflated.
    13. It rejects 2.4 genuine improvements against 1.1, and the registry drifts 0.00199 above truth.
    14. Re-measure the incumbent on the same fresh data. One evaluation run per cycle.
    15. If deployment were free, promote greedily — bad promotions get corrected later.
    16. The margin is a price, not a correction, and the best one rises with the cost of deploying.
