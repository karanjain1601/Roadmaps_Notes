Choosing a stack

MLOps has an unusually crowded vendor landscape and an unusual amount of writing that is really product marketing. The useful question is never "what does a mature ML platform look like" but "what is the next thing that will hurt, and what is the smallest thing that prevents it".

The test for whether a component is premature

Ask: does this prevent a failure we have actually had, or one we can specifically name as imminent? If the failure it prevents comes from a conference talk, a vendor's case study or a blog post about how a company with two hundred models operates, it is premature — not because it is bad, but because you will pay its operational cost every month and receive nothing back. The corollary is worth stating too: an incident is the cheapest possible justification for platform work, and the right response to one is not "build the whole layer" but "build the specific thing that would have caught it". Teams that follow that rule end up with a platform shaped like their actual failure history, which is the only shape that fits.

The order that works

roughly universal, and roughly in this sequence
 1. PREDICTION LOGGING           an afternoon    chapter 1
      every prediction, with inputs, output, model version, timestamp.
      Unlocks drift measurement, real performance, debugging, comparison.
      Nothing else gives this much for this little.

 2. EXPERIMENT TRACKING          half a day      chapter 7
      MLflow, or a hosted equivalent. A library call.

 3. CODE AND DATA VERSIONING     two days        chapter 3
      training code in git, data snapshotted, environment locked, seeds
      set. This is the one that makes everything after it verifiable.

 4. FEATURE CODE IN A PACKAGE    two days        chapters 2, 14
      one implementation, imported by the notebook, the pipeline and the
      service. Prevents the most damaging failure in the unit.

 5. INPUT VALIDATION             two days        chapter 6
      volume, freshness, schema, null rates. Start with the row count.

 6. A TRAINING PIPELINE          one to two weeks  chapter 8
      parameterised steps, cached, runnable in CI on a sample.

 7. A REGISTRY AND A GATE        one week        chapter 9
      versioned artefacts, an alias serving resolves, promotion gates as
      code. Rollback becomes a pointer move.

 8. DRIFT AND PERFORMANCE MONITORING  one week   chapter 15
      input PSI against the training baseline, prediction distribution,
      labelled performance by prediction week.

 9. SLICE-BASED EVALUATION       two days        chapter 10
      ten to twenty slices, logged from every run, gated at promotion.
      Cheap, and it changes which model you ship.

10. SHADOW DEPLOYMENT            one week        chapter 12
      before any canary, on every model.

The assembled stack's real risk is that it becomes a product nobody owns

Every team that assembles its own stack starts with three sensible components and ends up with glue: a deployment script, a wrapper around the registry, a custom feature-materialisation job, a bespoke drift dashboard. Each addition was justified. Collectively they are an internal platform with no roadmap, no documentation and one person who understands it — and chapter 1's glue-code debt applied to the infrastructure rather than the model. The mitigations are unglamorous and effective: write down which component owns which responsibility, keep the glue in one repository with tests, and periodically ask whether a piece of glue has grown into something a product now does better. The alternative failure — a vendor platform where you use three of nine components — is more expensive and considerably easier to escape.

What is genuinely premature for most teams

Defer until you can name the problem

  • A feature store — until definitions are shared and diverging (chapter 5)
  • Kubernetes — unless your organisation already runs it (chapter 16)
  • Automated retraining on drift triggers — a schedule plus performance-triggered retraining is most of the value
  • Distributed training — until it genuinely does not fit (chapter 8)
  • Online serving — until the prediction cannot be precomputed (chapter 11)
  • A multi-environment IaC estate — one environment in code beats three that drifted
  • Unsupervised performance estimation — get labelled performance working first
  • A vector database — if the answer is "we might do RAG later"

Never premature, at any size

  • Prediction logging with model version
  • Experiment tracking
  • Data snapshotting and a locked environment
  • One feature implementation, shared
  • A row-count assertion on every pipeline
  • Per-slice metrics logged from run one
  • A trivial baseline, logged as a metric
  • An inventory of what is in production (chapter 17)

Where the unit ends up

Almost everything in these eighteen chapters follows from the two facts in chapter 1.

An ML system's behaviour depends on data as well as code
Which is why there are four artefacts to version rather than one (chapter 3), why the data pipeline is where the incidents are (chapters 4 and 6), why a feature must be what was knowable at the time (chapter 5), why correctness is a metric on a distribution rather than a pass or fail (chapter 10), and why "what changed?" is the wrong first question.
The world keeps moving and the training data does not
Which is why models decay with no deployment, why monitoring has to measure quality and not just health (chapter 15), why retraining must be an operation rather than a project (chapters 8 and 9), why deployment needs a holdback to know whether anything improved (chapter 12), and why every practice here is ultimately in service of closing a loop.

The serving chapters are consequences of a third, narrower fact: that the same computation has to happen twice, once over history and once on a live request, and any difference between the two is invisible (chapter 14). Governance is those same properties written as obligations rather than as advice (chapter 17), which is why a team with good engineering practice finds compliance mostly a documentation exercise and a team without it finds it a rebuild.

What remains, when all of that is accounted for, is a discipline with an unusually poor ratio of writing to practice. The reference architectures are elaborate, the vendor landscape is enormous, and the failures that actually take models down are a short and boring list: the pipeline broke and nobody noticed, the features differed between training and serving, nobody could reproduce the model, and nobody was measuring whether it still worked. Six weeks of unglamorous work covers all four. Almost everything else is optimisation, and optimisation is worth doing after the thing exists.

The short version

  1. Ask whether a component prevents a failure you have had, or one you can name as imminent. Otherwise it is premature.
  2. An incident is the cheapest justification for platform work — and the response is the specific fix, not the whole layer.
  3. Items 1 to 10 in the ordered list are about six weeks and cover most production ML incidents.
  4. Prediction logging first, always. An afternoon, and it unlocks everything downstream.
  5. An assembled stack's risk is becoming a platform nobody owns. Keep the glue in one repo, with tests.
  6. A cloud-native stack's lock-in is often an acceptable price for not integrating six things yourself.
  7. Defer the feature store, Kubernetes, distributed training and online serving until each has a named problem.
  8. The failures that take models down are a short, boring list. Everything else is optimisation.
