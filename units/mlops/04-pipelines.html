CHAPTER 04

From notebook to pipeline

A ten-hour training job that restarts from the beginning when it fails does not take ten hours. At one failure per hundred minutes of running it takes forty thousand minutes — twenty-eight days. Twenty checkpoints bring it to 700, and the first split alone recovers most of that.

    THE MOTIVATION

    What a pipeline buys

    Four things, and only one of them is the one people usually give.

    Resumability
    A failed step restarts from its own beginning rather than the run's. The second demo shows this is not a convenience — beyond a certain job length it is the difference between finishing and not.
    Cached reuse
    An unchanged step can be skipped. The first demo shows the saving is smaller than people expect and depends entirely on where you cut.
    A dependency graph you can reason about
    "What has to rerun if this changes" becomes answerable from the structure, which is what chapter 11's lineage is built on.
    Steps that can be scheduled independently
    Different resources, different retries, different owners. A feature job on CPU and a training job on GPU are one script only by accident.
    What it does not buy

    A pipeline does not make a notebook correct, reproducible or fast. It is a structural change, and running badly-specified work on a schedule mostly produces badly-specified work more often.

    Chapter 2 and chapter 3 come first for a reason: a pipeline whose inputs are named rather than hashed automates the 45.5% from chapter 1 rather than fixing it.

    Placement beats count

    Two decompositions with one boundary each: cutting before training saves 15%, cutting before feature building saves 11%. Same effort, and the first is better because training is the single most expensive stage and 40% of changes land downstream of it.

    That one well-placed cut captures 55% of everything full decomposition would give you. The rule is simply: put a boundary immediately before your most expensive stage, and only then think about the rest.

    Note also that the total saving is modest — 27% even fully decomposed. Caching is worth having and it is not the main reason to build a pipeline. The next section is.

    Over-decomposition can be worse than none

    Read the last row. At 8 minutes per boundary, the fully decomposed pipeline expects 106.5 minutes against the monolith's 102 — the tidiest structure is now the slowest thing on the table.

    This is the failure behind "we split everything into steps and it got slower". Each boundary is a serialisation, a write to object storage, and a read back on the next step, and on a large intermediate that is not a rounding error.

    There is an optimum and it moves with your storage. The practical test is to measure how long materialising the intermediate actually takes before splitting around it.

    And none of it helps when the data changes

    Every stage is downstream of ingest, so a data refresh reruns all 102 minutes whatever the decomposition. Caching pays for iteration on code — which is what you do all day while developing — and does nothing for the scheduled retrain.

    Which means the caching argument and the resumability argument apply to different situations, and it is worth knowing which one you are making.

    A ten-hour job, at one failure per hundred minutes of running, has an expected wall clock of 40,243 minutes — twenty-eight days. Not because any individual failure is catastrophic, but because each one throws away everything and the job has to get lucky for ten straight hours.

    Six times the nominal length costs the monolithic job 234 times the wall clock. The same job with twenty checkpoints costs 6.8 times, which is about linear.

    The closed form is k·(eλT/k−1)/λ, and it matches a direct simulation at every setting tested. It also says there is a length beyond which an uncheckpointed job essentially never finishes, and that length is not far past the mean time between failures.

    Which is good news

    The first split saves 4,082 minutes and the sixth saves 28 — a ratio of 144 to one. Diminishing returns arrive immediately, so you do not need a finely-sliced pipeline to get the benefit.

    A handful of checkpoints at the boundaries you would have drawn anyway — after ingest, after features, after training — captures nearly all of it. And that is the same small set of boundaries the caching section recommended, which is convenient: the two arguments point at the same decomposition.

    THE PRECONDITION

    A step you can safely rerun

    Everything above assumes a failed step can simply be run again. That is a property you have to build.

  1. Write to a new location, then swap. A step that appends to a table produces duplicates on retry; a step that writes a new partition and atomically promotes it does not.
  2. Derive the output path from the inputs. If the output location is a function of the input hashes, a rerun overwrites its own previous attempt rather than creating a second artefact.
  3. No hidden state between steps. A step that reads a file the previous step happened to leave in /tmp works until the retry lands on a different machine.
  4. Make the step declare what it needs. Inputs as parameters, not as globals or ambient configuration, which is what lets a scheduler run it in isolation.
  5. Fail before writing, not during. Validate inputs at the top of the step so a bad run leaves nothing half-written — chapter 6's subject.
    6. The retry that makes things worse

    An automatic retry on a non-idempotent step is a way of turning one failure into several corruptions. If a step appends rows, three retries append three times, and the failure is now silent and in the data rather than loud and in the logs.

    So the order is: make it idempotent, then turn on retries. A scheduler that retries by default — most do — means this is not optional.

    IN PRACTICE

    What the decomposition usually looks like

    The boundaries that tend to be right, and why.

    A conventional training pipeline, and what each boundary is for
    StepOutputWhy the boundary
    IngestAn immutable, hashed snapshotChapter 3: the thing every downstream step is pinned to
    ValidatePass, or a failed runFails before anything expensive happens
    FeaturesA feature table, also hashedReused by training and serving alike
    TrainWeights plus a model cardThe expensive step — the cut that matters most
    EvaluateMetrics against a pinned holdoutCheap, and rerun far more often than training
    RegisterA registry entrySeparates producing a model from promoting one
    The two boundaries worth arguing about

    Evaluate must be separate from train. You will re-evaluate a model far more often than you train one — a new metric, a new slice, a new holdout — and if evaluation lives inside the training step every one of those costs a full retrain.

    Register must be separate from train. Producing a candidate and promoting it are different decisions with different approvals, and merging them means every successful training run ships. That distinction is chapter 10.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a pipeline

  6. What happens if it fails halfway? If the answer is "start again", the 40,243 applies as soon as the job gets long.
  7. Is there a boundary immediately before the most expensive step? It captured 55% of the available caching saving here.
  8. How long does materialising an intermediate take? At 8 minutes a boundary, full decomposition was slower than no decomposition.
  9. Is every step safe to run twice? Retries on a non-idempotent step turn failures into corruption.
  10. Can you run one step alone, locally? If not, debugging means rerunning the pipeline, which is the notebook problem with more infrastructure.
