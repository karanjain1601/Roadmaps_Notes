MLOps

A model that scores well in a notebook is perhaps a fifth of the work. The rest is the machinery that gets it trained reproducibly, deployed safely, watched carefully and replaced without drama — and the reason that machinery is a discipline rather than a checklist is that ML systems fail in ways ordinary software does not.

How to use this

Two prerequisites. You should have trained a model at some point, even a small one — enough to know what a train/test split is and why a metric can be misleading; the Machine Learning unit covers that ground. And you should be comfortable with the ordinary engineering tools: containers, CI, a cloud provider, some Python. No maths beyond that.

Chapters 1–3 are the framing, and chapter 3 is the one to read twice: almost every MLOps practice is downstream of being able to reproduce a result. Chapters 4–6 are data, 7–10 are training, 11–14 are serving — including chapter 14 on training/serving skew, which is the largest single cause of models that pass evaluation and fail in production. Chapters 15–18 are running it, ending with an argument about how much of this you should actually build.

Demos run real logic — you can watch point-in-time correctness prevent a leak that inflates a metric, compute drift statistics on shifting distributions, see a canary rollout catch a bad model, and price an inference workload three ways. Tools are named where naming them helps and treated as interchangeable where they are.

Two commitments

Problems before tools

MLOps has an unusually crowded vendor landscape and an unusual amount of writing that is really product marketing. Every chapter here starts from a failure that happens to real systems, then asks what property would prevent it. Tools appear as answers, and the answer is often "a convention and a script" rather than a platform.

Honest about premature platform work

A great deal of MLOps advice assumes a team running dozens of models. Most teams run one or two, and for them a feature store, a Kubeflow cluster and a service mesh are cost with no return. Chapter 18 says so directly, and the maturity ladder in chapter 1 is there so you can locate yourself before buying anything.
