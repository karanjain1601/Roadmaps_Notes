    Shadow answers a narrower question than people think

    It tells you the new model does not crash, fits the latency budget, and produces a prediction distribution you recognise. That is genuinely valuable and it is all it tells you.

    What it cannot tell you is whether the predictions are better, because nobody acted on them and so no outcome was generated. A shadow deployment of a recommendation model produces recommendations nobody saw, so there are no clicks to compare. Shadow is a safety check, not an evaluation — and treating it as one is how a model reaches production having been "validated on live traffic" without a single outcome having been observed.

    The hour-long canary everyone runs

    An hour at 5% canary detects an 8.6% relative drop and nothing smaller. A 3% drop in conversion is a serious incident, and this canary is blind to it.

    Time scales as 1/δ², so halving the regression you want to catch quadruples the canary. Catching a 3% drop at this traffic and share takes 8.2 hours; a 1% drop takes 3.1 days.

    None of that is a criticism of canaries. It is a statement of what the sample size supports, and the problem is only that canaries are usually promoted on a schedule that has nothing to do with it.

    A bigger canary is faster and exposes more people

    The sample the test needs is nearly fixed, so you are mostly choosing how fast to accumulate it — not how many people to risk. Going from 1% to 50% cuts the wait from 14.2 hours to 0.6 and doubles total exposure from 102,538 to 203,024.

    So a small canary is the more efficient way to spend exposure, at the price of taking 25 times as long to get an answer. Which of those you want depends on whether the scarce thing is patience or trust, and it is a genuine trade rather than a best practice.

    A canary catches crashes, not regressions

    A fivefold rise in errors is detectable in 16 seconds; a 5% conversion drop takes three hours. That is a factor of 648, and it is the whole design principle.

    Gate the canary on the fast signals — errors, latency, crash rate, prediction distribution — where it is genuinely decisive within minutes. Accept that the slow ones need an experiment, and run that experiment deliberately rather than pretending the canary covered it.

    The failure mode this prevents is the common one: a canary that passes on error rate, gets promoted in an hour, and ships a 4% conversion regression that takes a fortnight to notice in the weekly numbers.

    Every healthy deploy gets rolled back

    One check at the end rolls back a healthy canary 2.3% of the time, which is roughly what a two-sigma threshold should do. Checking every minute for six hours rolls it back 100% of the time.

    Nothing is wrong with the model. This is peeking arriving in the deployment pipeline as an automated rule, and it is more insidious here because the rule was written by an engineer building a safety feature rather than by anyone analysing an experiment.

    The visible symptom is a team that has quietly stopped trusting automated rollback, because it fires on deploys that turn out to be fine.

    Tightening costs time, not detection

    This is the pleasant surprise. A real 10% drop is caught 100% of the time at every threshold in the table — what tightening costs is the delay before rollback, from 6 minutes to 35.

    So the trade is minutes-of-exposure against false rollbacks, which is far easier to reason about than the sensitivity trade people assume they are making. Going from z<−2 to z<−4 takes false rollbacks from 100% to 1.1% and costs 29 extra minutes on the deploys that genuinely are broken.

    That is a good deal in almost every setting, and it means the correct response to a noisy rollback rule is to tighten it rather than to switch it off — which is not what teams usually do.

    The bottom rows are a sample-size problem, not a threshold problem

    A 30% collapse is caught in 7 minutes. A 1% drop is caught 11% of the time. And the 2% row is the one that matters: caught 63% of the time, at a median of 258 minutes — four and a quarter hours, long after any canary anyone actually runs has been promoted.

    No threshold fixes that. More traffic or a longer canary would, and neither is usually on offer. The honest response is to say the canary does not cover regressions of that size, and to catch them with a real experiment or with chapter 16's monitoring instead of pretending otherwise.

    PRACTICE

    A deployment sequence that follows the arithmetic

  1. Shadow first, for minutes. Crashes, latency, prediction distribution. Cheap, zero risk, and it catches the failures that would make a canary pointless.
  2. Canary gated on fast signals only. Errors, latency, fallback rate, prediction distribution — all decisive within minutes. Do not gate on conversion; the canary cannot see it.
  3. Use a tight threshold and let it take longer. z<−4 caught every real 10% regression here at a 1.1% false-rollback rate.
  4. Ramp rather than jump. 5% to 25% to 50% to 100%, each step re-checking the fast signals. Exposure accumulates gradually while the fast checks stay decisive at every stage.
  5. Then run an actual experiment for the business metric, at whatever share and duration the arithmetic says. Three hours for a 5% drop, and you now know that in advance.
  6. Keep the old model warm. Rollback that requires a deploy is not rollback; chapter 10's registry makes it a stage change.
    7. Blue-green for models has one specific advantage

    Rollback is instantaneous and complete, which matters when the failure mode is "the new model is catastrophically wrong" rather than "slightly worse". Since the fast signals detect exactly that class of failure in seconds, blue-green plus fast-signal monitoring is a coherent design.

    What it cannot do is give you a comparison, because there is no concurrent control. If you need to know whether the new model is better, you need overlapping traffic — and that is a canary or an experiment, not a switch.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a deployment process

  7. What is the smallest regression your canary can detect? An hour at 5% saw 8.6% and nothing smaller.
  8. Which signals does it gate on? Fast ones are decisive in seconds; business metrics are not decisive in hours.
  9. How often does the rollback rule check? Every minute for six hours rolls back a healthy deploy 100% of the time.
  10. When did automated rollback last fire, and was it right? A rule nobody trusts has already been switched off in practice.
  11. Is the previous model still warm? If rolling back means a deploy, your rollback time is a deploy time.
