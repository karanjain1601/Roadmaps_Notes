Monitoring in production

The quantity you want to monitor — is the model still right — is usually unavailable, because the labels arrive days or months later or never arrive at all. Everything else in ML monitoring is a proxy chosen to fill that gap, and choosing them deliberately is the whole skill.

Standard observability is necessary and tells you nothing about the model

Latency, error rate, throughput and saturation are all required, and a model can be catastrophically wrong while every one of them is green. That is the structural difference from monitoring a conventional service: a wrong prediction is a successful request. So ML monitoring is a second layer on top of the usual one, and it has four tiers arranged by how quickly each can tell you something — inputs, predictions, proxies, then outcomes. The first three are available immediately and none is the thing you care about; the fourth is the thing you care about and arrives late. Build all four, and be explicit about which layer an alert came from, because that determines what the alert means.

the four tiers
TIER 1 — INPUTS                                   available: immediately
  per feature: null rate, mean, std, quantiles, category set, unseen-value
  rate; PSI against the training baseline logged with the model (ch 6, 7)
  detects: upstream breakage, population shift, skew
  cannot detect: concept drift

TIER 2 — PREDICTIONS                              available: immediately
  score distribution, mean and quantiles, rate above the decision
  threshold, PSI against the validation-set distribution
  detects: almost anything wrong with inputs or plumbing, as a summary
  cannot detect: whether the shift is correct

TIER 3 — PROXIES AND BEHAVIOUR                    available: hours
  the rate at which downstream systems accept the prediction — analyst
  override rate, click-through, manual-review volume, appeal rate
  detects: quality problems, sometimes before labels
  cannot detect: anything the proxy is blind to; and the proxy itself
  can be gamed by a change in downstream behaviour

TIER 4 — OUTCOMES                                 available: label delay
  actual precision, recall, calibration, business metric, by slice
  detects: the real thing
  cannot detect: it in time to prevent it

THE PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE: alert on tiers 1 and 2, INVESTIGATE with tier 3,
and DECIDE with tier 4. A tier-1 alert is never sufficient reason to
retrain — it is reason to look, because retraining on an upstream bug bakes
it in (chapters 6 and 14).

AND ONE ARRANGEMENT THAT CHANGES THE ARITHMETIC: a permanent holdback
(chapter 12). With 5% of traffic on the previous model, when performance
degrades you can see whether the holdback degraded too. Both fell means the
world changed; only the new one fell means the model is the problem. That
distinction is otherwise very hard to establish, and it is available at
tier 4 without any extra label collection.

Working with delayed labels

four techniques, roughly in order of usefulness
# 1. JOIN LABELS BACK AS THEY ARRIVE, and compute performance on a rolling
#    window keyed by PREDICTION time, not label time. This distinction
#    matters: you want "how good were the predictions made in week 12",
#    which is only fully answerable once week 12's labels have all landed.
perf = (predictions
        .merge(labels, on="request_id", how="inner")
        .assign(week=lambda d: d.predicted_at.dt.to_period("W"))
        .groupby("week").apply(metrics))
# Plot it with a confidence interval and mark the weeks that are still
# incomplete — a partially-labelled recent week looks different for
# mechanical reasons and people read the dip as a regression.

# 2. SURVIVAL-AWARE EARLY SIGNALS. If 60% of churn labels arrive within 7
#    days, the 7-day-partial precision is a biased but usable early
#    indicator — as long as you compare it against the 7-day-partial
#    figure from previous weeks rather than against the final number.
#    Comparing like with like is what makes a biased metric useful.

# 3. PROXY LABELS. Something correlated with the outcome and available
#    sooner:
#      fraud      -> analyst override rate on flagged items (hours)
#      churn      -> logins in the following week (days)
#      recommender-> click-through (minutes), and be aware CTR and
#                    long-term retention diverge
#    Validate the proxy against real labels once, quantify the
#    relationship, and re-validate periodically. An unvalidated proxy is a
#    number nobody should act on.

# 4. UNSUPERVISED PERFORMANCE ESTIMATION. Methods that estimate accuracy
#    from the prediction distribution and the model's calibration without
#    labels — confidence-based estimation, importance weighting, and
#    NannyML's CBPE and DLE implementations.
#    Genuinely useful and genuinely approximate. Treat the output as a
#    smoke alarm, not as a measurement, and validate it against real
#    labels on historical data before trusting it.

# THE STATISTICAL POINT THAT SAVES FALSE ALARMS: a metric computed on a
# small labelled sample has a wide confidence interval. Precision on 80
# labelled examples moves several points from noise alone. Bootstrap the
# interval and alert on the interval, not the point estimate — otherwise
# you will investigate noise every week and stop trusting the alert
# (chapter 10).
Feedback loops make your monitoring lie to you

If the model's prediction affects whether you observe the outcome, the labels you collect are not a sample of reality. A fraud model that blocks a transaction never learns whether it was fraudulent; a lending model that declines an application never sees whether that person would have repaid; a recommender only receives clicks on items it chose to show. The observed precision then looks excellent and is measured on a population the model itself selected — and the effect compounds, because the next model trains on that biased sample. The mitigations are all deliberate exploration: hold back a random small fraction from the model's action entirely, or accept a band of borderline cases against policy, and treat that as the cost of knowing whether the system works. This is one of the few places where the correct engineering answer requires a business decision.

the retraining decision
WHEN TO RETRAIN — four triggers, in descending order of how good they are:

  1. MEASURED PERFORMANCE DEGRADATION on labelled data. The honest trigger.
     Requires labels, so it is the slowest.

  2. PROXY DEGRADATION, where the proxy has been validated against real
     labels. Faster, and only as good as the validation.

  3. SUSTAINED DRIFT in important features, once you have ruled out an
     upstream bug. Weaker, and often right in practice.

  4. A SCHEDULE. Weekly or monthly, unconditionally. Crude, and much better
     than nothing — a monthly retrain bounds how stale the model can get,
     costs a pipeline run, and requires no monitoring sophistication at all.

FOR MANY TEAMS THE RIGHT ANSWER IS 4 PLUS 1: retrain on a schedule so
staleness is bounded, and retrain off-schedule when measured performance
drops. Waiting for a sophisticated drift-triggered pipeline before doing
anything is how models sit unretrained for a year.

WHEN NOT TO RETRAIN, and these matter more:

  * the drift is an upstream data bug. Retraining fits the model to
    corrupted values, locks in the error and destroys the evidence
  * the drift is in a feature the model barely uses. Weight by importance
    before reacting
  * you do not have enough NEW labelled data yet, so the retrain is mostly
    the same data with a different seed — which will produce a different
    number that means nothing (chapter 3)
  * the degradation is on one slice. A targeted fix — a feature, a
    separate model, a different threshold — usually beats a global retrain
    that averages the problem away (chapter 10)

AND ALWAYS GATE THE RESULT. An automatic retrain that deploys without the
promotion gates from chapter 9 is an automatic way to ship a broken model,
and drift is exactly the situation in which the new training data is most
likely to be wrong.
One dashboard, five panels

ML monitoring tends to sprawl into hundreds of per-feature charts that nobody reads. The version that gets used is small: prediction volume and the rate above the decision threshold, which together catch most upstream and plumbing problems; the top five features' PSI, importance-weighted, rather than all two hundred; the null and unseen-category rate across features, as a single worst-case number; labelled performance by prediction week with confidence intervals and incomplete weeks marked, alongside the holdback; and which model version is actually serving, compared against the registry. Five panels, one screen, reviewed weekly. Per-feature detail belongs in a drill-down that is opened during an investigation, not on the wall — because a dashboard with two hundred sparklines is a dashboard that is never read.

The short version

  1. A wrong prediction is a successful request. Standard observability cannot see model quality at all.
  2. Four tiers: inputs, predictions, proxies, outcomes. The first three are immediate; only the fourth is the thing you want.
  3. Alert on tiers 1 and 2, investigate with 3, decide with 4. A drift alert is not a reason to retrain.
  4. Key performance by prediction time, not label time, and mark the incomplete recent weeks.
  5. Compare biased early signals against the same biased signal historically, not against final numbers.
  6. Feedback loops make observed performance a lie. Deliberate exploration is the only fix, and it costs something.
  7. Bootstrap confidence intervals on small labelled samples, or you will investigate noise weekly.
  8. A monthly schedule plus performance-triggered retraining beats waiting for a sophisticated trigger you never build.
