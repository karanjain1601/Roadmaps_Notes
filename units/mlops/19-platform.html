    PURPOSE

    What a platform is actually for

    A platform is a way of paying a cost once

    Every chapter in this unit describes something that has to be true of every model: reproducible builds, validated inputs, point-in-time correct features, a registry, monitoring, a retraining cadence. A platform is the decision to solve each of those once rather than per team.

    Which means the case for one scales with the number of teams, not the number of models. Three teams each solving feature freshness badly is the problem a platform fixes; one team with thirty models is not, because they will solve it once anyway.

  1. The failure mode is a platform nobody uses. Built ahead of demand, generalised for cases that never arrived, and worked around by every team because adopting it costs more than not.
  2. The opposite failure mode is quieter. Six teams each maintaining their own feature pipeline, none of which is anyone's job, all of which fail differently.
  3. The reliable order is to extract rather than anticipate. Let two teams solve it, take the better one, make it a service, and delete the other.
  4. Adoption is the metric, not capability. A platform component used by one team is a component that team now shares maintenance of with a platform team — strictly worse than them owning it outright.

    Maintenance is the term that never appears in the discussion

    Three engineer-months is £33,000 of build. At 30% a year, five years of maintenance is £49,500 more — 150% on top of the figure that got the decision approved.

    That is not pessimistic. Thirty per cent covers dependency upgrades, the odd breaking API change, one migration, and answering questions from whoever inherits it. Components that need less than that are usually the ones small enough that buying was never the question.

    Time to value is what actually decides it

    On bare cost the crossover is two models, which is why so many components get built. Once you count the quarter during which the models are not being served, the crossover moves past 200 models at these numbers.

    The reason is that a model earning nothing for three months costs more than several years of a licence. Whether that applies depends on whether the models are genuinely blocked or merely inconvenienced — which is the question worth arguing about, rather than the build estimate.

    The question is never asked about the portfolio

    Eight components at three months each consume exactly one engineer permanently. Each individual decision looked like three months of work, and each was probably defensible on its own.

    Nobody approved a permanent headcount for platform maintenance, and nobody will notice they have one until the team is asked why it is slow.

    The most valuable thing in this unit costs half a week

    Counting the fallback rate is worth £759,050 a year and takes half a week. It is the largest single number in the table and the cheapest item on it — which almost never happens, and is worth acting on when it does.

    Below the top the orderings diverge sharply. Model monitoring is #2 by value and #5 by value per week. Point-in-time correctness is #5 by value and last by rate — genuinely worth having, and the worst possible thing to start with.

    Cheap-first buys time, not a smaller bill

    Ordering by value per week reaches half the money in 3.5 weeks against 12.5 — more than three times faster. That is the case for doing the cheap things first.

    But it is not uniformly better. By 75% the value ordering has caught up (15.5 weeks against 17), because the cheap list runs out and you are left with the expensive items either way. Twenty-four weeks of work exists regardless of the order you do it in.

    So the honest claim is narrower than the usual one: sequencing cheap-first gets you protected sooner, and it does not reduce the total. A roadmap that promises otherwise is promising that the expensive items will not be needed.

    COST

    Attribution, and why it changes behaviour

  5. Tag everything with the model it serves. Training jobs, serving replicas, feature pipelines, storage. Without it, cost is a single number nobody owns.
  6. Report cost per prediction and cost per model per month. Chapter 12's question — what share of these predictions does anyone read — is unanswerable without the first figure and obvious with it.
  7. Include the idle share. Chapter 15 measured 618 idle replica-hours a day at a 5× peak; that is a real line item that appears nowhere by default.
  8. Attribute the platform too. The eight-components-one-engineer arithmetic above is invisible unless someone charges it somewhere.
  9. Show the owning team, not just the total. Costs that appear on a central bill do not change behaviour; costs that appear on a team's own dashboard do.
    10. The cheapest cost lever is usually a model nobody uses

    Attribution's first result is nearly always the same: a model still serving traffic that no product surface reads any more. It costs a fraction of the bill, needs no optimisation work, and cannot be found without per-model tagging.

    Governance that slows deployment usually increases risk

    Chapter 14 measured a real 10% regression caught 100% of the time at every rollback threshold, with the cost being minutes rather than detection. A process that turns a twenty-minute rollback into a two-day change-approval cycle has multiplied every incident's damage by roughly a hundred, and it did so in the name of safety.

    The governance worth having makes bad outcomes visible — slices, lineage, documented intent. The governance worth questioning makes them slower to fix.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a platform

  10. How many components have you built, and what is the maintenance total? Eight at three months each is a permanent engineer.
  11. Do you count time-to-value in build decisions? It moved the crossover from 2 models to beyond 200.
  12. Can you produce cost per model per month? If not, nobody owns the bill.
  13. Is the fallback rate counted? Half a week of work, £759,050 a year, and it is the most common thing missing.
  14. Does any governance step add days to a rollback? That step is increasing risk, not reducing it.
