    Coverage is a threshold, not a gradient

    While the cold start meets or exceeds the burst length, coverage is flat at 0%. The replicas arrive after the burst has ended, and the only visible trace is a scale-up on the graph that nobody needed and a scale-down five minutes later.

    Once the cold start drops below the burst length it rises quickly: 50% at half the length, 93% at a fifteenth. The same 300-second burst sheds 52,200 requests at a 90-second cold start and 2,900 at a five-second one.

    So cold-start time is the single parameter that decides whether autoscaling works at all. Warm pools, smaller images and lazily-loaded weights buy coverage; tuning the scaling policy does not.

    Two budgets, not one

    An hour-long swell is covered 98%; a 40-second retry storm is covered 0%. Those are not two settings of the same dial — they are different problems.

    The honest design is to autoscale the daily shape and hold static headroom for everything faster than your cold start. Trying to make the scaler handle both produces a policy that is too twitchy for the slow variation and still too slow for the fast one.

    And the fast variation has a second defence that costs nothing: chapter 13's pooling. A retry storm hitting one shared queue is absorbed by the whole fleet's spare capacity; the same storm hitting a per-tenant shard has only that shard's headroom.

    THE PARAMETER THAT MATTERS

    Where cold-start time goes

    Scheduling and image pull
    Getting a container onto a node and the image onto that node's disk. Fixable with smaller images, layer caching and pre-pulled images on the node pool — often the largest single term and the easiest to attack.
    Loading the model
    Reading weights from object storage into memory, then onto the accelerator. Scales with model size and is the term that makes ML cold starts worse than ordinary service cold starts. Memory-mapping and streaming the load help; so does keeping the weights on a local volume rather than fetching them.
    Framework initialisation and warm-up
    Graph compilation, kernel autotuning, JIT on the first few requests. A replica that is "ready" by the health check and slow for its first thirty requests is a real and commonly missed failure mode — the readiness probe should run an inference, not just return 200.
    The scaler's own delay
    Metric scrape interval, evaluation period, stabilisation window. Usually tens of seconds and usually the term people tune first, which is worth doing only once the three above are smaller than it.
    A warm pool is the direct purchase

    Keeping a few replicas started, loaded and idle converts the cold start to nearly zero for the first N scale-up events. It costs exactly what those idle replicas cost, and the demo above prices what it buys: at a 300-second burst, going from a 90-second to a 5-second cold start avoids 49,300 shed requests.

    That is an unusually clean trade for an infrastructure decision — both sides are in units you can compute.

    The hidden 25%

    Covering a 600 req/s peak needs 15 replicas at nominal capacity and 19 at usable capacity, because chapter 13 showed the tail explodes past about 80% utilisation.

    That is a 25% surcharge on every capacity calculation, and it is the term most often left out — capacity plans get built from throughput benchmarks that were run at saturation, which is precisely the operating point you cannot use.

    The optimum here is 24 replicas: 1.28× headroom over the usable-capacity peak, 0.3% of the day under capacity. Note that the cheapest option is neither "cover everything" nor "cover the average" — shedding a small amount at the very peak is genuinely cheaper than provisioning for it.

    Static provisioning prices the peak and you pay for it all day

    At a 5× peak you buy 32 replicas and use 16% of them at the average moment — 618 idle replica-hours a day. At a 1.5× peak the same fleet runs at 50%.

    Which is exactly the case autoscaling exists for, and exactly why the first demo matters: the daily shape is slow enough for a 90-second cold start to follow easily, so this is the variation you genuinely can scale away. The fast bursts sitting on top of it are the ones you must buy headroom for.

    ACCELERATORS

    What changes on a GPU

  1. Cold starts get much worse. Weights must reach device memory, and a large model turns a 20-second cold start into minutes. Every conclusion in the first demo shifts against autoscaling.
  2. Utilisation is measured wrong by default. GPU utilisation reports whether a kernel is running, not whether the device is doing useful work. A model at "90% utilisation" running batch size 1 may be using a small fraction of the available compute — chapter 13's batching is what closes that gap.
  3. The unit of scaling is coarse. One replica is one GPU, so the granularity is expensive: going from four to five replicas is a 25% cost step rather than a rounding error.
  4. Sharing is possible and awkward. Time-slicing or partitioning lets several models share a device, which helps utilisation and reintroduces noisy-neighbour effects on the tail.
  5. The right first move is usually not more GPUs. Quantisation, distillation and a smaller model change the arithmetic more than the fleet size does, and they reduce cold-start time at the same time.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a serving fleet

  6. How long is your cold start? Compare it with your typical burst length; below that ratio autoscaling covers nothing.
  7. Does the readiness probe run an inference? A replica that is ready and slow for thirty requests is a silent tail problem.
  8. Was the capacity plan built from usable or nominal throughput? The gap is 25%.
  9. What utilisation do you run at the daily mean? 16% at a 5× peak means most of the fleet is idle most of the time.
  10. Is shedding at the very peak actually cheaper? Here it was — 24 replicas beat 30, with 0.3% of the day over capacity.
