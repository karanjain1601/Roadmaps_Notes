    Streaming is the interesting middle

    It looks like online — predictions are fresh — but serves like batch, because the caller reads a stored value rather than waiting for inference. That combination is why it is the right answer more often than its reputation for complexity suggests.

    The cost is that "whatever changed" requires knowing what changed, which means the event stream has to carry the feature updates. If you already have that stream, streaming inference is cheap; if you do not, building it is the whole project.

    FRESHNESS

    What staleness costs

    A batch job scores everyone every T hours, so a prediction is up to T hours old when it is used. What that costs depends entirely on how fast the underlying state moves.

    One degrades and the other stops working

    The slow signal loses 0.0246 over a day — real, about a tenth of its skill above chance — and is still clearly useful at 0.7308. After a full week it still reads 0.6278.

    The fast signal loses 0.2343 over the same day and reads 0.5161, which is essentially random. And it has been there since the first day: by three days it is at 0.5017, because there is nothing left for more staleness to take.

    That is not a difference of degree. Daily batch scoring is a perfectly sound architecture for the first column and a way of shipping a broken model for the second — and the model report looks identical either way, because chapter 7's point applies: offline evaluation scores fresh features.

    Which is the argument for a hybrid

    The loss is seven times larger among the users whose state actually moved, and they are a minority. Everyone else is served perfectly well by a day-old prediction.

    So the choice is not batch or online for the whole population. It is: batch for everyone, plus fresh scoring for the ones whose state changed since the batch ran — which is exactly what an event stream tells you. That is the streaming architecture, arrived at from the cost side rather than the architecture side.

    And measuring this is cheap. Score a sample fresh, score them again with a day-old feature vector, and compare. You will get both the aggregate loss and the split, and the split tells you how much of the population actually needs freshness.

    The break-even is at 9.4% of entities requested per day

    Below that, batch is paying to score people nobody asked about. Above it, online is paying a premium per prediction on volume high enough that precomputation amortises.

    The counterintuitive part: the dearer online inference is, the lower the utilisation at which batch wins — 29.4% break-even at a 2× unit-cost ratio, 2.9% at 20×. A large model with expensive inference pushes you toward precomputation even at very low utilisation, which is the opposite of the usual instinct that big models are too expensive to run over everyone.

    The hybrid beats both, and the optimum is small

    Precomputing just 5% of entities absorbs 37% of requests and costs 27% less than the better pure strategy. The optimum is interior — 40% is already worse than 5%, and 100% is 88% worse.

    This works because requests are concentrated. A small precomputed head takes most of the traffic and the long tail is served online, which is what most real systems converge on after someone runs this arithmetic. It is also why the answer to "batch or online" is usually "both, and mostly online by entity count".

    FAILURE

    Three different things going wrong

    The architectures fail in ways that need different detection.

    Batch fails quietly
    The job does not run and yesterday's predictions are still sitting there, being served. Nothing errors, nothing is slow, and the only symptom is age. This is chapter 6's stale-data problem: schema and distribution checks both pass at 0%, and only a freshness check sees it. Alert on the age of the newest prediction, not on the job's exit code.
    Online fails loudly
    Errors and timeouts the caller sees, which is unpleasant and at least self-reporting. The real risk is the tail: a model that is fast on average and slow on 1% of inputs breaks a request budget in a way the mean latency conceals, which is chapter 13's subject.
    Streaming fails as lag
    The consumer falls behind and predictions get older without anything failing. Detectable, and only if you are watching consumer lag rather than throughput — a stalled consumer and a quiet stream look identical on a throughput graph.
    All three need a fallback
    What is served when the prediction is missing or the model is down? A default, the population average, the previous value, or a rules-based path. Deciding this in advance is a five-minute conversation; deciding it during an incident is not.
    The fallback that hides the outage

    A serving layer that silently returns the population average when the model is unavailable will pass every health check while delivering no personalisation at all. The system is up, latency is excellent, error rate is zero, and the model has been effectively switched off for a fortnight.

    Fallbacks are correct. Fallbacks that are not counted are how a model quietly stops contributing. Emit a metric for the fallback rate and alert on it, because it is the one number that distinguishes a healthy system from a convincingly healthy one.

    DECIDING

    Choosing, in order

  1. Measure the staleness cost first. Score a sample fresh and again with a day-old feature vector. If the loss is 0.0246 you have a much wider choice than if it is 0.2343.
  2. Then measure utilisation. What share of entities is actually requested per interval? The break-even was 9.4%, and most teams guess far higher than the truth.
  3. Prefer batch when both allow it. It is the simplest failure surface, retries are free, and latency is a lookup.
  4. Reach for streaming when freshness matters for a minority. The 47%-of-users-carrying-the-loss split is the signature of a case streaming handles well and pure online overserves.
  5. Reach for online when the input arrives with the request. If the features describe the request itself — a basket, a search query, a submitted form — there is nothing to precompute and the decision is already made.
  6. Then consider the hybrid, which beat both pure options by 27% here and is where most systems end up anyway.

    7. JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a serving setup

  7. How old is a prediction when it is used? Not the batch interval — the age at the moment of use, which is up to the interval.
  8. What does that cost in AUC? Measurable in an afternoon, and the answer ranges from 0.0246 to 0.2343 on the same architecture.
  9. What share of entities is requested per interval? Break-even was 9.4%; guesses tend to be high.
  10. What is served when the model is unavailable, and how often does that happen? An uncounted fallback is a model switched off.
  11. What alerts on prediction age? A batch failure produces no errors, no latency and no failed checks — only staleness.

    12. Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Batch, online and streaming differ in prediction age, what they score, and how they fail.
    2. Streaming is fresh like online and serves like batch, if you already have the event stream.
    3. A week-half-life signal loses 0.0246 over a day and still reads 0.7308.
    4. A six-hour signal loses 0.2343 and reads 0.5161 — essentially random.
    5. One degrades and the other stops working. The model report looks identical either way.
    6. By three days the fast signal is at 0.5017; there is nothing left to take.
    7. The loss is seven times larger among users whose state moved, and they are 47%.
    8. So the choice is not batch or online for everyone — it is batch plus freshness for the movers.
    9. Measure it by scoring a sample twice, fresh and with a day-old vector.
    10. Break-even utilisation is 9.4% of entities requested per day.
    11. Below it, batch pays to score people nobody asked about.
    12. The dearer online inference is, the LOWER the break-even — 29.4% at 2×, 2.9% at 20×.
    13. Precomputing 5% of entities absorbs 37% of requests and costs 27% less than the best pure option.
    14. The optimum is interior: 40% is worse than 5%, and 100% is 88% worse.
    15. Batch fails as staleness, online as errors, streaming as lag — three different alerts.
    16. Count your fallback rate. An uncounted fallback is a model switched off that passes every health check.
