    SYNTHESIS

    The shape of the argument

    Three claims, repeated in different costumes

    Silent failure is the default. A model that has stopped working produces no errors, no latency and no failed deploys. Chapter 1 measured a 7.1% chance of an identical rebuild when six components are each mostly fine; chapter 16 measured concept drift doing 401 times a crash's annual damage while every dashboard stayed green.

    Damage is severity multiplied by time. Detection time varied by 5,700× across chapter 16's failure table while cost per hour varied by 11. Chapter 18 found the same multiplication running after the alert, where the instinctive first diagnostic is 23× less efficient than the dashboard.

    The binding constraint is usually attention, not capability. Chapter 6's 50 columns firing on 12.6% of clean days; chapter 10's manual review blocking 33 good models per bad one; chapter 16's twenty monitors reaching 100% coverage and catching a third as many incidents as five. Every one of those is the same arithmetic in a different place.

    Why this check matters more than the tool

    A planner that assembles six chapters can go wrong in a way none of the individual demos could: a units mistake, a transcription slip, a formula copied with one term dropped. Feeding each chapter its own inputs and requiring its own answer back catches all three.

    One honest caveat about the batch sizing. The planner picks the smallest batch with 60% headroom over current traffic, which is a capacity rule. Chapter 13's optimum — 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 across 20 to 500 req/s — minimises p99 instead, and those are different questions. The ceiling shown is per replica.

    The fraud model drifts fastest and still cannot use a trigger

    It has the highest decay rate of the three, which is why its cadence is the shortest at 68 days. And its labels arrive 14 days late, so a trigger would be a schedule in disguise — chapter 17 measured the like-for-like advantage falling from +0.0097 to +0.0054 across that lag.

    Drift rate sets the cadence; label delay decides trigger against schedule. Those are different inputs answering different questions, and conflating them is how teams end up building a trigger that fires three weeks after it mattered.

    None of these is a default anyone could have guessed

    Cadences of 68, 156 and 102 days. Windows of 9, 75 and 7. A canary that sees 3.4% on the recommender and 30.6% on the batch model — a factor of nine, from traffic alone.

    The recommender retrains less often than the fraud model despite far more data, because its decay is slower and its retrains are cheap. The churn model has the longest window and the longest cadence and is the least demanding system here, despite being the one with the most alarming label delay.

    Every one of those numbers falls out of two or three measured inputs. What they have in common is that guessing would have got them wrong.

    REFERENCE

    The index of measured findings

    One per chapter, each derived from running code rather than asserted.

    Nineteen findings filter by the part of the lifecycle they belong to

    Six findings that contradicted the intent behind writing them

    These are the ones where the measurement disagreed with the plan and the chapter was rewritten around the result.

    Chapter 10. With free deployment, a promotion margin of zero wins outright. The margin turned out to be a price on deployment risk, not a statistical correction — best margin rose 0.0000, 0.0005, 0.0010, 0.0020, 0.0040 as the cost per deployment rose.

    Chapter 14. Tightening a rollback threshold costs time, not detection. A real 10% drop was caught 100% of the time at every threshold from z<−2 to z<−4; only the median moved, 6 minutes to 35.

    Chapter 14 again. A bigger canary detects faster and exposes more users — 102,538 at 1% against 203,024 at 50% — because the sample the test needs is nearly fixed.

    Chapter 15. Burst coverage is a threshold rather than a gradient: flat at zero while the cold start exceeds the burst, then 50%, 80%, 93%.

    Chapter 16. The three monitoring levels are worth £817,898, £754,360 and £744,600 — within 10% of each other, rather than data monitoring dominating as expected.

    Chapter 19. Cheap-first is not uniformly better. It reaches half the value in 3.5 weeks against 12.5, and by 75% the value ordering has caught up.

    PRACTICE

    If you inherited a system tomorrow

  1. Count the fallback rate. Half a week, £759,050 a year in chapter 19's arithmetic, and the most common thing missing. An uncounted fallback is a model switched off that passes every health check.
  2. Find out how old your newest label is. That number bounds model-monitoring detection time, decides trigger against schedule, and is the input most teams cannot produce.
  3. Alert on the age of the newest prediction. A batch failure produces no errors, no latency and no failed checks — only staleness.
  4. Divide your p99 by your model's compute time. Above ten, you have a queueing problem rather than a model problem.
  5. Retrain on three window lengths and compare on last week's data. Free to run, and chapter 17 measured all-history at 22.9× the right window under fast drift.
  6. Write the diagnostic order down, cheapest first. Chapter 18: a fixed runbook reached 37 minutes against 67 for improvising from the model.
  7. Count your platform components and multiply their build estimates by 2.5. Eight at three months each is one permanent engineer.
    8. The measurement that pays for itself fastest

    Hold out a model, score it weekly against fresh labels, and plot the decay. That single number sets the retraining cadence through chapter 17's square root, and without it every schedule in the organisation is a guess dressed as a policy.

    It takes an afternoon to set up and it is the input that three separate chapters of this unit turn out to depend on.

    The one habit worth taking away

    Every number in this unit came from code that ran, and roughly a sixth of them contradicted what the chapter was written to say. The margin that should have been positive was zero; the tightening that should have cost detection cost only time; the monitoring level that should have dominated was within 10% of the other two.

    None of those would have been caught by reasoning carefully. They were caught by writing the measurement first and letting it overrule the plan — which is available to anyone with an afternoon and a willingness to be wrong in public.

    Recap

    The unit in sixteen lines

    1. Silent failure is the default — no errors, no latency, no failed deploys.
    2. Reproducibility is a conjunction: six components mostly fine gives 7.1% identical rebuilds.
    3. Pipeline boundaries matter by placement, not count — one cut captured 55% of the benefit.
    4. Validate before training, or a retrain bakes a broken pipeline in.
    5. Point-in-time correctness is structural: 0.9487 offline became 0.3805 online.
    6. Fewer, better checks beat more: 5 columns detect 50%, 200 detect 16.2% at the same budget.
    7. The best of 50 runs is genuinely best 58% of the time.
    8. Order gates by bad-blocked per good-blocked; manual review scored 0.03.
    9. 93% of models hit by a bad dataset are affected indirectly.
    10. Ask what share of predictions anyone reads before optimising how they are served.
    11. Pooling is free tail latency: 102 ms against 461 at identical utilisation.
    12. A canary catches crashes, not regressions — a factor of 648.
    13. Cold-start time decides whether autoscaling works at all.
    14. Damage is severity times detection time, and detection time varies by 5,700×.
    15. Cadence follows a square root; the training window follows the drift rate.
    16. The binding constraint is usually attention — in alerts, in gates, and in incident minutes.
