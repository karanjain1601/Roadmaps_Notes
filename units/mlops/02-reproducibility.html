CHAPTER 02

Reproducibility

Retrain the same converged model with the examples in a different order and the test AUC moves by 0.00158 — nothing. Meanwhile 5.90% of individual decisions flip, and among borderline cases it is 41.1%. The metric says the model is unchanged; the people it decides about would disagree.

    DEFINITIONS

    Three different things called reproducible

    They cost wildly different amounts, and most arguments about reproducibility are people wanting different ones.

    Bit-identical
    The same weights, byte for byte. Achievable, and the demo below shows what it takes: a fixed seed and no source of ordering nondeterminism. Expensive on a GPU, and often not the thing you actually need.
    Statistically equivalent
    A different model whose aggregate behaviour is indistinguishable. Cheap, and what most teams mean. The first demo is about why this is a weaker guarantee than it sounds.
    Reconstructible
    You can identify every input that produced the artefact, whether or not rerunning gives the same answer. This is the one that matters for audit, debugging and rollback, and it is the cheapest of the three — it is bookkeeping rather than determinism.
    The order to want them in

    Reconstructible first, always. It is a few lines of metadata and it answers "what produced this", which is the question you will actually be asked.

    Bit-identical where it is cheap — which is most CPU training and most classical models. Do not spend a fortnight forcing determinism onto a distributed GPU job.

    Statistically equivalent as the fallback, but knowing what the second demo shows: aggregate equivalence permits a lot of per-case disagreement.

    The metric is stable and the decisions are not

    Shuffling the examples moves the test AUC by 0.00158 — a range of 0.00516 across eight rebuilds, which no report would flag and no acceptance test would fail. The model is, by any aggregate measure, the same model.

    And 5.90% of individual test cases receive a different decision. On a hundred thousand applications that is roughly six thousand people whose outcome depended on which run of the training job happened to be the one deployed.

    The flips concentrate near the boundary — 41.1% of cases within 0.05 of the threshold flip, against 2.88% of confident ones. That is reassuring for an average and not at all reassuring for the borderline applicant, who is precisely the person a review process will ask about.

    Two things follow

    "The metrics match" is not evidence that a rebuild reproduced. It is evidence that the aggregate behaviour is similar, which is a much weaker claim and one that a comparison of AUCs cannot distinguish from a genuinely identical model.

    Convergence is a reproducibility property. The undertrained row flips 12.20% of decisions and has three times the AUC spread. A model stopped early is not just less accurate, it is less stable, and the two are usually discussed separately.

    If per-case consistency matters — credit, moderation, medical triage, anything a person can appeal — then the thing to test is not the metric. It is the fraction of decisions that change, which takes one line and almost nobody computes.

    MECHANISM

    Where nondeterminism enters

    In rough order of how often it is the culprit.

    Sources, and what each one costs to remove
    SourceWhy it happensCost to pin
    Data orderingShuffling, and parallel readers finishing out of orderLow — seed the shuffle, sort the shards
    Unseeded initialisationWeight init, dropout, augmentation, samplingLow — one global seed, set early
    ConcurrencyThread count changes the reduction order of a sumMedium — fix threads, lose some speed
    GPU kernelsAtomics accumulate in nondeterministic orderHigh — deterministic kernels are slower and not always available
    Library versionsA default changed, or an algorithm was replacedLow — a lockfile
    The data itselfA late-arriving row, a backfill, a mutable tableMedium — snapshot or content-address it
    The last row is the one that catches people

    Everything above it is a property of your code. "The data itself" is a property of a system you probably do not control, and a query against a live table returns different rows depending on when you ran it.

    Chapter 1 measured what that does: with a silently-refreshing data source, 35.5% of days ran a combination nobody had tested. Pinning the training data is usually the highest-value single change, and it is chapter 3's subject.

    A seed is not one seed

    Python's random, NumPy's global state, the framework's own generator, the data loader's workers, and the CUDA generator are separate. Setting one and assuming the rest followed is the most common way a "seeded" run turns out not to be.

    Frameworks provide a single call that sets all of them, and a determinism flag that raises an error when a nondeterministic operation is used. That flag is worth turning on once even if you then turn it off — the list of things it complains about is the list of things making your training irreproducible.

    THE BETTER GOAL

    Do not eliminate divergence — detect it

    Hashing the inputs does not make a rebuild match. It changes who finds out that it did not.

    The first column never moves

    7.1% throughout. Hashing does not improve reproducibility at all — the rebuild is exactly as likely to differ as it was before.

    What changes is the third column: silent divergence falls from 92.9% to 0.0%. The failure mode goes from "a different model is now in production and nobody knows" to "the job stopped and printed which input moved". Those are not the same incident.

    And the third row is the practical one. Hashing the four cheap inputs — code, data, libraries, seed — gets silent divergence to 7.7% without touching hardware or upstream feature logic, which are the expensive things to pin. Doing it the other way round, hashing only hardware and upstream logic, leaves 40.9% silent: 5.3 times worse, for considerably more effort.

    What a hash of each input looks like in practice

    Code: the git commit, plus a flag for a dirty working tree. Data: a content hash of the snapshot, or the table version if the store provides one. Libraries: a hash of the lockfile, not of the requirements file. Seed: the value itself. Environment: the container image digest, which is already a hash.

    Store all five with the model. A rebuild recomputes them and refuses to proceed on a mismatch unless overridden explicitly. That is perhaps thirty lines, and it converts the most confusing class of ML incident into a failed job with a clear message.

    DOING IT

    The checklist that gets you most of the way

    In the order that pays.

  1. Snapshot or content-address the training data. The highest-value single change, and the one that stops a rerun quietly training on a different week.
  2. Lockfile, not a requirements list. Exact versions of everything including transitive dependencies, and hash the lockfile rather than the top-level file.
  3. One seed, set once, covering every generator. Use the framework's combined helper rather than setting them individually.
  4. Run in a container, and record the image digest. The digest is a hash you already have.
  5. Record all of it with the model artefact. Not in a wiki page — alongside the weights, where a rollback can read it.
  6. Test the rebuild on a schedule. A monthly job that retrains last month's model and compares. This is the only thing that tells you whether any of the above is actually working.
    7. Where to stop

    Forcing bit-identical results out of distributed GPU training is expensive, sometimes slower by a large factor, and occasionally impossible with the kernels available. It is rarely worth it.

    What is worth it is knowing you did not get it. A recorded set of input hashes plus an accepted tolerance on the metric gives you a defensible position: this model was built from these exact inputs, and rebuilding produces a model within this stated tolerance. That is an honest claim, and unlike "it is reproducible" it is one you can check.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a training pipeline

  7. Which of the three meanings are you claiming? Bit-identical, statistically equivalent, or reconstructible. Most disagreements dissolve here.
  8. If you reran it now, would it use the same rows? A query against a live table says no.
  9. What fraction of decisions would change? Not the metric — the decisions. 5.90% here with a metric that moved 0.00158.
  10. Would a mismatch be loud or silent? Silent is the 92.9% default.
  11. When did a rebuild last actually run? An untested rebuild procedure is a hypothesis, and this is the cheapest possible test of everything above.

    12. Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Three meanings: bit-identical, statistically equivalent, reconstructible — and the cheapest one matters most.
    2. Want reconstructible first, bit-identical where cheap, statistical equivalence as a fallback.
    3. A fixed seed with no shuffling gives a bit-identical rebuild and zero flipped decisions.
    4. Shuffling the examples moves the AUC by 0.00158 — a range of 0.00516 across eight rebuilds.
    5. And flips 5.90% of individual decisions. The metric says the model is unchanged.
    6. Among borderline cases it is 41.1%, against 2.88% of confident ones.
    7. So "the metrics match" is not evidence a rebuild reproduced.
    8. Convergence is a reproducibility property: stopped early, 12.20% of decisions flip.
    9. If per-case consistency matters, measure decision flips, not the metric.
    10. Data ordering and unseeded initialisation are the usual culprits, and both are cheap to fix.
    11. The data itself is the expensive one, because it belongs to a system you do not control.
    12. A seed is not one seed — several generators, and the determinism flag lists what is breaking.
    13. Hashing does not change the 7.1%. It changes who finds out.
    14. Silent divergence falls from 92.9% to 0.0% as you hash more inputs.
    15. The four cheap inputs get it to 7.7%; hashing only the expensive ones leaves 40.9%.
    16. Test the rebuild on a schedule. It is the only check that the rest of this is working.
