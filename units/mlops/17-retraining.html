    FIRST

    Retraining fixes one thing

    It is the default response to a model behaving badly, and it is the right response to a specific and narrow cause.

    What retraining does and does not address
    ProblemDoes retraining fix it?
    The world has moved and the old relationship no longer holdsYes — this is what it is for
    New categories, new products, new user behaviourYes
    A feature pipeline is brokenNo — it trains on the broken feature
    Training-serving skewNo — and it may deepen it
    A point-in-time leakNo — it relearns the leak
    The metric was never rightNo
    Retraining on a broken pipeline bakes the break in

    The rows that say no are the reason chapter 6's validation runs before training rather than after. A retrain triggered by a quality drop that was actually caused by a stalled feature job produces a model fitted to the stalled feature — and now the problem survives fixing the pipeline.

    So the first question on any automated retrain is not "how often" but "what does it check before it starts".

    The square-root rule

    The optimum is √(2 · cost · 365 / (decay · value)), and the simulation matches it within 15% at every combination tested — 156 days against 163, 102 against 105, 68 against 70, 59 against 61, 186 against 183.

    Two numbers decide it: how fast your model decays and what a retrain costs all-in. Both are measurable — decay from holding out a model and scoring it weekly, cost from your own billing plus chapter 10's deployment price.

    And because it is a square root, it is forgiving. Retraining twice as often as optimal costs 13%; half as often costs 19%. Being roughly right is enough, which is worth knowing before anyone spends a fortnight tuning a schedule.

    Note the asymmetry though: going long hurts more than going short. When in doubt, err short.

    The comparison people make is not like for like

    A laggy trigger has a lower total cost than an instant one, which looks like good news. It is not — a lazy trigger simply retrains less often, and that saving was available from a schedule without the degraded weeks in between.

    Matched on retrain frequency, the trigger's real advantage is +0.0097 of AUC with instant detection and +0.0054 at a 21-day lag. And the quality it actually delivers falls monotonically: 0.8596 → 0.8585 → 0.8560 → 0.8532.

    Chapter 16 established that model monitoring detects in days, bounded below by the label delay. So the label delay is the number that decides whether a trigger is worth building at all — and for a churn model with a month-long label, the honest answer is usually no.

    What to trigger on instead

    If the slow signal cannot fire a useful trigger, fire on the fast ones. A retrain triggered by a new category appearing, an input distribution shift, or a step change in prediction distribution arrives in hours rather than weeks, and those are exactly the events a scheduled retrain handles worst — it either catches them immediately by luck or three months late.

    The practical arrangement is both: a schedule at roughly the square-root cadence as the floor, plus fast-signal triggers for the discrete events. That is one policy, two mechanisms, and it needs no label delay to work.

    Using all the data is right only when nothing changes

    With no drift, the optimal window is the entire history — that is the case the instinct is built for. Every day of drift moves it shorter, and the window falls from 75 days to 5 as drift rises fifty-fold.

    Under fast drift, training on all 365 days gives 22.9 times the error of the right window. That is a larger effect than most model changes anyone spends a quarter on, and unlike those it costs nothing to test: retrain on several window lengths and compare on a recent holdout.

    It also interacts with cadence. Retraining more often does not help if the window still reaches back a year — the new model is fitted to mostly the same stale data, which is why "we retrain weekly" and "our model is stale" are perfectly compatible statements.

    Weighting is the softer version

    A hard cutoff throws away everything older than the window. Exponential time-weighting keeps old rows at reduced influence, which is usually better and has one parameter instead of one threshold.

    The half-life plays the role the window did, and the same test finds it: fit at several half-lives, compare on a recent holdout. For seasonal effects the hybrid is a short recent window plus the same period last year at full weight, which is a small amount of code and often the largest single improvement available to a retraining pipeline.

    BEFORE IT SHIPS

    Validating the candidate

  1. Re-measure the incumbent on the same fresh data. Chapter 10: comparing against the recorded metric promoted 6.0 times in twenty cycles against re-measurement's 10.0, because the recorded figure is inflated by exactly what got it promoted.
  2. Evaluate on a recent holdout, not a random split. A random split over a drifting year measures the wrong thing — chapter 7's point-in-time argument applied to evaluation.
  3. Check the same slices every time. A retrain that improves the aggregate while collapsing on a segment is common, and the aggregate will not show it.
  4. Compare prediction distributions, not just metrics. A large distribution shift with an unchanged metric means something structural changed, and it is worth understanding before it ships.
  5. Run the automated gates. Chapter 10's arithmetic applies unchanged: order them by bad-blocked per good-blocked, and be suspicious of anything below 1.
  6. Keep the deployment margin honest. The margin is a price on deployment risk, not a statistical correction, and it should reflect what a deployment actually costs you.

    7. JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a retraining setup

  7. What is your decay rate? Without it the cadence is a guess; with it the square-root rule gives an answer in one line.
  8. What does a retrain cost all-in? Compute plus deployment risk, not just the GPU bill.
  9. What does the retrain check before it starts? Retraining on a stalled feature bakes the stall in.
  10. How long is your training window, and when was that chosen? Under fast drift, all-history was 22.9× worse than the right window.
  11. If you retrain on a trigger, what is the detection lag? The trigger's advantage fell from +0.0097 to +0.0054 across a three-week lag.
