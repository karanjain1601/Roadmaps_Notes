Governance and compliance

Almost every regulatory requirement for ML turns out to be an engineering requirement you should have met anyway: know what data trained the model, know what it does, know who decided to deploy it, and be able to explain a specific decision. The compliance framing just makes it non-optional.

Governance is mostly recording things at the moment they happen

Look at the fields in that demo: a model version in the prediction log, a data snapshot on the training run, an approver on the promotion, a timestamped history of which version was live. Each is one extra recorded value, each costs essentially nothing at the time, and none of them can be added retroactively. That asymmetry is the entire practical content of ML governance. The organisations that struggle with an audit are not the ones with bad processes — they are the ones who did not write down the four or five facts that make their process demonstrable, and by the time the question arrives the information has been overwritten by a backfill, a privacy deletion or a redeployment.

The EU AI Act, as engineering requirements

the timeline, and what it currently asks for
THE REGULATION: (EU) 2024/1689, in force 1 August 2024, applying in stages.
It is risk-tiered: prohibited practices, high-risk systems, limited-risk
(transparency only), and minimal risk.

  Feb 2025   prohibited practices apply; Article 4 AI LITERACY duty applies
             — every provider and deployer must ensure staff operating AI
             on their behalf are sufficiently AI-literate
  Aug 2025   general-purpose AI model obligations apply
  Aug 2026   Article 50 transparency obligations apply
  Dec 2027   HIGH-RISK obligations for stand-alone Annex III systems
  Aug 2028   high-risk obligations for AI embedded in regulated products

THOSE LAST TWO DATES MOVED. The original text set August 2026 and August
2027. The "Digital Omnibus on AI" — Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, published
24 July 2026 and in force from 27 July 2026, six days before the original
deadline — deferred them to 2 December 2027 (Annex III) and 2 August 2028
(Annex I). It also delayed the Article 50(2) watermarking requirement to
2 December 2026 for systems already on the market, and added a new
prohibited practice covering AI-generated non-consensual intimate imagery
and CSAM.

WHAT DID NOT MOVE, and this is the part most often misread: the Article 4
AI literacy duty and the Article 50 transparency rules stay on their
original timeline. "The EU delayed the AI Act" is half true and misleading.

ALSO WORTH KNOWING: there is GRANDFATHERING. Systems placed on the market
before the new deadlines can avoid the full high-risk regime until
substantially modified — so what counts as a substantial modification, and
whether a retrain counts, is a question worth asking your legal team about
early. And the Omnibus added a lawful basis for processing special-category
data specifically for bias detection and correction, which removes a real
obstacle to fairness testing.

THIS IS A MOVING TARGET. Verify dates against the current Official Journal
text rather than trusting any summary, including this one.

"Human oversight" is a design requirement, not a checkbox

The failure mode is a person nominally in the loop who cannot actually exercise judgement: a queue of a thousand decisions a day with a score and an approve button, reviewed at four seconds each. That is rubber-stamping, it satisfies nobody, and under a high-risk regime it is unlikely to count as meaningful oversight. Making it real has engineering consequences — the reviewer needs the feature values that drove the prediction (chapter 14's feature log), an explanation of the contribution of each, comparable past cases, and the ability to override with the override recorded and fed back as a label. It also needs the volume to be reviewable, which usually means the model routes only genuinely uncertain cases to humans rather than everything above a threshold. Design the review interface as part of the system, not as an afterthought bolted on for compliance.

Fairness, practically

and the trade-off nobody can engineer away
# THE METRICS, and they conflict:
#
#   DEMOGRAPHIC PARITY      equal selection rate across groups
#                           P(ŷ=1 | A=a) equal for all a
#   EQUAL OPPORTUNITY       equal true positive rate across groups
#                           P(ŷ=1 | y=1, A=a) equal for all a
#   EQUALISED ODDS          equal TPR and FPR across groups
#   CALIBRATION BY GROUP    a score of 0.7 means 70% in every group
#   PREDICTIVE PARITY       equal precision across groups
#
# THE IMPOSSIBILITY RESULT: when base rates genuinely differ between
# groups, calibration and equalised odds cannot both hold, except in
# degenerate cases. This is a theorem (Kleinberg, Chouldechova, 2016-17),
# not a limitation of current methods, and no technique resolves it.
#
# So the question "is this model fair" has no purely technical answer. What
# has an answer is "which definition are we adopting, why, and who decided"
# — and that decision belongs to the people accountable for the product,
# recorded in the model card, not made implicitly by whoever wrote the
# evaluation script.

from fairlearn.metrics import MetricFrame, selection_rate, equalized_odds_difference

mf = MetricFrame(
    metrics={"selection_rate": selection_rate, "recall": recall_score,
             "precision": precision_score, "count": lambda y, p: len(y)},
    y_true=y_test, y_pred=y_pred, sensitive_features=A_test,
)
print(mf.by_group)              # per group, with counts — always show counts
print(mf.difference())          # the largest gap
print(equalized_odds_difference(y_test, y_pred, sensitive_features=A_test))

# THREE PRACTICAL POINTS THAT MATTER MORE THAN THE METRIC CHOICE:
#
#   PROXIES. Removing a protected attribute does not remove the disparity;
#   postcode, device, name and browsing time are all proxies. "We do not
#   use race as a feature" is not a fairness argument and never was.
#
#   YOU USUALLY CANNOT MEASURE WHAT YOU DO NOT COLLECT. Fairness testing
#   needs the attribute, and collecting it can itself be restricted. The
#   Digital Omnibus added an explicit lawful basis for processing
#   special-category data for bias detection and correction, which is a
#   genuine change — if data protection was your blocker, revisit it.
#
#   THE INTERVENTION POINT MATTERS. Pre-processing (reweight the data),
#   in-processing (constrain the objective), or post-processing (per-group
#   thresholds). Post-processing is the most transparent and the most
#   legally fraught, since it means explicitly treating groups differently.
#   Take advice.
An inventory is the first governance artefact, and most teams lack one

Before risk classifications, model cards or fairness reviews: a list of every model in production, with an owner, what it decides, what it decides it about, whether a human reviews the output, and when it was last retrained. A spreadsheet is fine. It is the thing every subsequent conversation depends on — you cannot classify systems by risk tier, plan a compliance programme, or answer "what AI are you running" without it. And building it reliably surfaces things nobody expected: a model still serving that was replaced last year, a notebook someone runs monthly that makes real decisions, a vendor feature that is a model nobody registered as one. That last category is the one that catches organisations out, because the obligations attach to the deployer regardless of who trained it.

The short version

  1. Governance is recording four or five facts at the moment they happen, and none of them is retrofittable.
  2. The AI Act's high-risk obligations moved to December 2027 (Annex III) and August 2028 (Annex I) via Regulation (EU) 2026/1744.
  3. AI literacy and transparency duties did not move. "The EU delayed the AI Act" is misleading.
  4. The high-risk requirements are this unit's chapters — provenance, data governance, logging, monitoring — written as obligations.
  5. Human oversight is a design requirement. A thousand decisions a day with an approve button is not oversight.
  6. Generate the model card from the registry. Only intended use, out-of-scope use and limitations are hand-written.
  7. Fairness definitions conflict provably. Choose one deliberately, record who chose it, and show group counts.
  8. Start with an inventory. Every model, its owner, what it decides — and it will surface models nobody registered.
