How to use this

The subject has a reputation for being interview trivia. It is not. It is the study of how the way you store something determines what you can afford to do with it — and once you see a problem in those terms, a surprising number of hard problems become easy ones.

What the subject is actually about

A data structure is a decision about which operations will be cheap. An algorithm is a decision about how to spend those operations. That is the whole field.

Consider a phone book. As an unsorted list, adding a name is instant and finding one means reading every entry. Sorted alphabetically, finding a name takes a handful of comparisons and inserting one means shifting everything after it. Indexed by first letter into 26 buckets, both get faster and you have spent memory to do it. Nothing was added or removed from the data — only the arrangement changed, and the arrangement decided what was affordable.

The one question

For any structure, ask: which operations does this make cheap, and what did it cost to make them cheap? Every table in this unit is an answer to that question. Nothing is universally best — a hash table gives you constant-time lookup and destroys ordering; a sorted array gives you range queries and makes insertion expensive; a linked list gives you cheap insertion and takes away indexing.

And the counterweight

Most code does not need this. A loop over forty configuration entries can be O(n³) and nobody will ever notice. Reaching for a segment tree when a list would do is a real failure mode, and it costs readability, correctness and time. The skill is knowing when the cost of an operation is about to matter — which requires knowing what the costs are, which is why the material is worth learning even when you rarely deploy the exotic parts of it.

The trade-offs that recur

Time against space
The most common exchange in the field. A hash table spends memory to buy constant-time lookup. Memoisation spends memory to avoid recomputation. Prefix sums spend an array to make range queries instant. When something is too slow, "what could I precompute?" is usually the first question.
Read speed against write speed
Sorted structures and indexes make lookups fast and modifications slow. Append-only structures do the reverse. Databases live entirely inside this trade-off — every index you add speeds up reads and slows down writes.
Generality against speed
Comparison sorting cannot beat O(n log n), but counting sort does the job in O(n) if your values are small integers. Specialised structures win by assuming something about the data.
Worst case against average case
Quicksort's worst case is O(n²) and it still beats mergesort in practice. Hash tables are O(n) in the worst case and effectively O(1) always. Knowing which case you are exposed to is a judgement, not a lookup.
Simplicity against optimality
An O(n²) solution that is obviously correct and ships today often beats an O(n log n) one that takes a week and has an edge case. Optimise when there is evidence, not on instinct.

How to practise so it sticks

This is the part most people get wrong, and the reason so many give up believing they are bad at it. Reading solutions produces a warm feeling of understanding that is almost entirely illusory.

  1. Understand the structure before the problems. Know what a heap is and what it costs before attempting problems that need one. Pattern-matching without the underlying model produces memorised solutions that do not transfer.
  2. Implement it once from scratch. Write your own linked list, your own hash table, your own binary heap — badly, and without looking. The bugs you hit are the mechanism teaching you itself. Then throw it away and use the library version forever after.
  3. Struggle before looking. Twenty to thirty minutes of genuine effort on a problem. Retrieval effort is what builds durable memory; reading a worked solution builds recognition, which feels identical and disappears within days.
  4. Ask for a hint, not a solution. "Which structure would make this cheap?" is enough to unblock most problems while leaving the actual work to you.
  5. Debrief every problem. After solving — or after giving up and reading — write two lines: what the signal was that identified the pattern, and what you would look for next time. This step is where the transferable knowledge actually forms.
  6. Revisit cold after three days. Re-solve from scratch without notes. If you cannot, you did not learn it, you recognised it. This single habit is worth more than doubling the number of problems attempted.
Four ways people waste months
  • Grinding problem count. Two hundred problems solved by pattern-matching teach less than forty solved slowly with debriefs. The number is not the skill.
  • Watching solution videos. Fluent explanation creates the strong impression of understanding without any retrieval practice. Watch after attempting, never before.
  • Skipping the complexity analysis. If you cannot say why your solution is O(n log n), you do not yet understand your own solution. Chapter 2 exists so this is not hand-waving.
  • Starting with dynamic programming. It is the hardest topic and the one people jump to first because it is famous. It requires comfortable recursion, which requires comfort with the call stack. Chapter 7, then chapter 20.

Choosing a language

Almost any language works. You need arrays, dictionaries, a sort, and ideally a heap — everything else you will build. The examples here are Python, chosen because the syntax stays out of the way, but each one names the operation clearly enough to translate.

Learn your language's built-ins first

Before implementing a heap, know that heapq exists. Before writing a hash table, know exactly what dict guarantees. In real work you will use the library version every time — and interviewers expect you to. Implementing from scratch is a learning exercise, done once, to understand what the library is doing on your behalf.

A note on notation

Chapter 2 defines Big-O properly, but you will meet it immediately, so: O(f(n)) means the number of operations grows no faster than f(n) as the input gets large, ignoring constant factors. O(1) is constant regardless of input size, O(n) is proportional, O(n²) quadruples when the input doubles, O(log n) barely grows at all. Constants are dropped because they depend on the machine, and the point of the notation is to compare algorithms independently of the machine.

Self-check

  1. Why is there no universally best data structure?
    Because every structure buys cheap operations by making others expensive. Hash tables give constant lookup and lose ordering and range queries. Sorted arrays give range queries and make insertion O(n). Linked lists give cheap insertion and lose indexing. The right choice depends entirely on which operations you actually perform and how often — which means the first step of choosing is listing those operations.
  2. Your nested-loop solution takes two seconds on 10,000 records. Roughly what happens at 100,000?
    About 200 seconds, not 20. A nested loop is quadratic, so multiplying the input by 10 multiplies the work by 100. This is the reasoning that matters more than any specific number: ask how the cost scales, because that is what decides whether a solution survives growth. Buying a machine twice as fast buys you a 41% larger input.
  3. Why is reading solutions before attempting a problem counterproductive?
    Because understanding an explanation and being able to produce it are different skills, and the first feels exactly like the second. Durable memory is built by retrieval effort — the struggle to generate an answer — not by exposure to a correct one. Reading first removes the only part of the process that teaches. Attempt for twenty to thirty minutes, then read, then re-solve cold three days later.
  4. When is an O(n²) algorithm the right choice?
    When n is small and bounded, when it is dramatically simpler and therefore more likely to be correct, or when the constant factor is so much lower that it wins at realistic sizes — insertion sort beats mergesort below about fifty elements, which is why real sort implementations switch to it. "Optimal" is a property of a solution plus a workload, not of an algorithm alone.
