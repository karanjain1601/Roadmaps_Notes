Neural networks

A neural network is a stack of linear transformations with a bend between each pair. That is genuinely all it is — but the bends are what let it represent anything, and the training procedure that makes it work has enough sharp edges to fill a chapter.

— words— min read2 interactivesNeeds: chapters 02–04, 10

From one neuron to a network

A single neuron is logistic regression: weight the inputs, add a bias, squash. Chapter 12 already covered it.

$$a = \phi\big(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b\big)$$

Stack neurons into a layer and layers into a network, and the forward pass becomes a chain of matrix products:

$$\mathbf{a}^{(1)} = \phi\big(\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)}\big), \qquad \mathbf{a}^{(2)} = \phi\big(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\mathbf{a}^{(1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(2)}\big), \qquad \hat{y} = \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}^{(3)}\mathbf{a}^{(2)} + b^{(3)}\big)$$
LayerWeight shapeOutput shape (batch of B)
Input—(B, 20)
Hidden 1, 64 units(64, 20)(B, 64)
Hidden 2, 32 units(32, 64)(B, 32)
Output, 1 unit(1, 32)(B, 1)
Why the nonlinearity is not optional

Remove φ and the network collapses: \(\mathbf{W}^{(3)}\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}\) is just \(\mathbf{W}_{\text{combined}}\mathbf{x}\), a single linear layer with extra steps. A hundred layers would have exactly the expressive power of one. The bend between layers is the entire source of a network's capacity, which is why so much attention goes into choosing it.

The universal approximation theorem says a network with one hidden layer can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary accuracy, given enough units. It is reassuring and almost useless in practice: it says nothing about how many units, whether gradient descent can find them, or whether the result generalises. Depth turns out to matter enormously in practice even though the theorem does not require it — deep networks reuse features hierarchically and reach the same accuracy with exponentially fewer units.

Dying ReLU

A ReLU unit whose input is negative for every training example outputs zero, so its gradient is zero, so its weights never update — it is dead for the rest of training. Usually caused by too high a learning rate driving the bias sharply negative. Symptoms: a chunk of units with permanently zero activation and a network that has silently lost capacity. Fixes: lower the learning rate, use leaky ReLU or GELU, and check He initialisation is being used.

Apply the activation inside the loss, not before it

Use BCEWithLogitsLoss on raw logits rather than Sigmoid followed by BCELoss. The fused version uses the log-sum-exp trick to stay numerically stable; the separate version can produce log(0) and an instant NaN once the model becomes confident. The same applies to CrossEntropyLoss, which expects logits and applies softmax internally — passing it softmax output is a common bug that trains, badly, without erroring.

Backpropagation

Chapter 3 did one neuron. Here is the general form, which is the same rule applied layer by layer with the intermediate results cached.

flowchart LR X["x"] --> Z1["z¹ = W¹x + b¹"] Z1 --> A1["a¹ = φ(z¹)"] A1 --> Z2["z² = W²a¹ + b²"] Z2 --> P["ŷ = σ(z²)"] P --> L["L(y, ŷ)"] L -.->|"δ² = ŷ − y"| Z2 Z2 -.->|"δ¹ = (W²)ᵀδ² ⊙ φ′(z¹)"| Z1 Z2 -.->|"∂L/∂W² = δ²(a¹)ᵀ"| W2["W²"] Z1 -.->|"∂L/∂W¹ = δ¹xᵀ"| W1["W¹"]
$$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)} = \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} = \big(\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\big)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)} \odot \phi'\big(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\big), \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(l)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}\big(\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}\big)^{\top}$$

δ is the error signal at a layer. ⊙ is elementwise multiplication. The first expression assumes the sigmoid-plus-cross-entropy pairing from chapter 3, where the messy factors cancel.

Every gradient above came from multiplying local derivatives along a path. Nothing in the procedure changes when the network has a hundred layers and ten million parameters; only the bookkeeping grows, and that is what autodiff handles.

Optimisers

Chapter 3 introduced these geometrically. Here are the update rules you will see in papers.

$$\begin{aligned} \text{SGD:}\quad & \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, g \\[3pt] \text{Momentum:}\quad & v \leftarrow \beta v + g, \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, v \\[3pt] \text{RMSProp:}\quad & s \leftarrow \rho s + (1-\rho)g^2, \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{s}+\epsilon}\,g \\[3pt] \text{Adam:}\quad & m \leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1-\beta_1)g, \quad s \leftarrow \beta_2 s + (1-\beta_2)g^2, \quad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \frac{\eta\,\hat{m}}{\sqrt{\hat{s}}+\epsilon} \end{aligned}$$

g is the gradient. Adam combines a momentum term m with a per-parameter scale s, and the hats denote bias correction, which matters only in the first few dozen steps when both averages start at zero.

SGD + momentum 0.90.01 – 0.1Vision models with a good schedule. Often the best final accuracy
Adam0.001The safe default. Works nearly everywhere with little tuning
AdamW0.001 – 0.0003Adam with weight decay applied correctly. The standard for transformers

Why AdamW rather than Adam plus weight decay

In plain Adam, an L2 penalty is added to the gradient and then divided by \(\sqrt{s}\) along with everything else — so parameters with large historical gradients get less regularisation, which is the opposite of the intent. AdamW decouples the two: the decay is applied directly to the weights, outside the adaptive scaling. The difference is small in code and consistently measurable in results.

Initialisation and normalisation

Why you cannot start at zero

Initialise every weight to zero and every unit in a layer computes the same thing, receives the same gradient, and updates identically — forever. The layer has one effective unit no matter how wide it is. Symmetry has to be broken randomly.

But the scale of that randomness decides whether a deep network trains at all. Too small and the signal shrinks toward zero as it passes through layers; too large and it explodes. Both are visible in the right-hand panel of the activation demo above.

$$\text{Xavier/Glorot:}\;\; \text{Var}(W) = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}} \qquad\qquad \text{He:}\;\; \text{Var}(W) = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}$$

Xavier suits symmetric activations like tanh. He suits ReLU, whose zeroing of negative inputs halves the variance and needs the extra factor of two to compensate. Frameworks default to sensible choices, but if you build layers by hand, set this explicitly.

Batch norm's two modes

During training it normalises using the current batch's statistics; at inference it uses running averages accumulated during training. Forgetting model.eval() means your predictions depend on whatever else happens to be in the batch — a bug that produces plausible-looking but subtly wrong output, and one of the most common mistakes in hand-written PyTorch inference code. Dropout has the same train/eval split.

The training loop

python — PyTorch, with the things people forget
import torch, torch.nn as nn
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader, TensorDataset

torch.manual_seed(42)
device = "cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu"

model = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Linear(n_features, 128), nn.BatchNorm1d(128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Dropout(0.2),
    nn.Linear(128, 64),          nn.BatchNorm1d(64),  nn.ReLU(), nn.Dropout(0.2),
    nn.Linear(64, 1),                                   # logits — no sigmoid here
).to(device)

criterion = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()                          # fused, numerically stable
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight_decay=1e-2)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt, T_max=50)

train_dl = DataLoader(TensorDataset(Xtr, ytr), batch_size=64, shuffle=True)
val_dl   = DataLoader(TensorDataset(Xva, yva), batch_size=256)

best, patience, wait = float("inf"), 10, 0

for epoch in range(200):
    model.train()                                           # dropout ON, batchnorm uses batch stats
    for xb, yb in train_dl:
        xb, yb = xb.to(device), yb.to(device)
        opt.zero_grad()                                     # gradients accumulate otherwise
        loss = criterion(model(xb).squeeze(1), yb)
        loss.backward()
        nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)  # cheap insurance
        opt.step()
    sched.step()

    model.eval()                                            # dropout OFF, batchnorm uses running stats
    with torch.no_grad():                                   # no graph, less memory, faster
        val = sum(criterion(model(xb.to(device)).squeeze(1), yb.to(device)).item() * len(xb)
                  for xb, yb in val_dl) / len(Xva)

    if val < best - 1e-4:
        best, wait = val, 0
        torch.save(model.state_dict(), "best.pt")          # keep the best, not the last
    else:
        wait += 1
        if wait >= patience:
            print(f"early stop at epoch {epoch}"); break

model.load_state_dict(torch.load("best.pt"))

The overfit-a-tiny-batch test

Before any real training run, take twenty examples and train until the loss reaches near zero. If a model cannot memorise twenty examples, no amount of tuning will help — there is a bug in the data pipeline, the loss, the shapes or the gradient flow. It takes a minute and catches the majority of silent failures.

Do not reach for a neural network on tabular data by default

On tabular problems with fewer than a few hundred thousand rows, gradient boosting usually wins — and wins with less tuning, faster training and easier interpretation. Neural networks earn their place where the input has structure a network can exploit: spatial (images), sequential (text, audio, time series), or relational (graphs). Chapter 12's advice still stands: start with boosting, and switch only when you have a reason.

Self-check

  1. What happens if you initialise every weight to zero?
    Every unit in a layer computes the same output and receives the same gradient, so they update identically and remain identical forever. A 128-unit layer has the expressive power of one unit. Random initialisation exists to break that symmetry, and the scale of the randomness — Xavier or He — controls whether the signal survives the depth of the network.
  2. Your loss goes to NaN at epoch 3. Give three checks in order.
    (1) Divide the learning rate by ten and rerun — the cause most of the time. (2) Check for a separate sigmoid or softmax before a loss that applies it internally; switch to BCEWithLogitsLoss or CrossEntropyLoss on raw logits. (3) Check preprocessing for a division by a zero standard deviation, or a log of zero. If it is a recurrent model, add gradient clipping.
  3. Why does ReLU work better than sigmoid in deep networks?
    Sigmoid's derivative peaks at 0.25 and approaches zero at both extremes, so backpropagating through ten layers multiplies gradients by numbers well below one ten times — the signal vanishes. ReLU's derivative is exactly 1 for positive inputs, so gradients pass through unattenuated. It is also cheaper to compute and produces sparse activations. The price is the dying-ReLU failure mode; the demo's right-hand panel shows both effects directly.
  4. What does model.eval() actually change?
    It switches dropout off and makes batch norm use its accumulated running statistics rather than the current batch's. Forgetting it means predictions are randomly perturbed by dropout and depend on which other samples share the batch. It is also worth pairing with torch.no_grad(), which is a separate thing — it stops building the autograd graph, saving memory and time.
  5. A network cannot get below 0.5 loss on twenty training examples. What does that tell you?
    That something is broken rather than badly tuned. Twenty examples are trivially memorisable by any working network. Look for: labels not aligned with inputs, a shape error being silently broadcast, gradients not flowing because a tensor was detached, a learning rate near zero, or the loss being computed on the wrong axis. Fix this before touching any hyperparameter.
  6. Why is AdamW preferred over Adam with an L2 term added to the loss?
    In Adam, adding L2 to the gradient means the penalty also passes through the per-parameter division by \(\sqrt{s}\). Parameters with a history of large gradients get a smaller effective decay — the reverse of what regularisation should do. AdamW applies the decay directly to the weights, outside the adaptive scaling, so every parameter is regularised equally. It is a few characters of difference and a consistently measurable improvement.
