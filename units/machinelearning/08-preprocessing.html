Cleaning and preprocessing

This is where the thirty per cent of your time goes. Missing values, absurd outliers, categories the model cannot read, features on incompatible scales, a target class that appears once in five hundred rows — and running through all of it, the risk of accidentally telling the model the answer.

— words— min read3 interactivesNeeds: chapters 06–07

The order of operations

Sequence matters here more than in most engineering. Do these steps in the wrong order and you will produce a model that validates beautifully and fails in production.

flowchart TD A["Audit — understand what you have"] --> B["Fix structural problems
types, duplicates, units, encodings"] B --> C["SPLIT into train / validation / test"] C --> D["Fit transformations on TRAIN ONLY
imputers, scalers, encoders"] D --> E["Apply the fitted transformations
to validation and test"] E --> F["Train the model"] style C fill:#FBEAF2,stroke:#E0699F,stroke-width:3px

The reason is subtle and worth stating precisely. If you compute a column mean over the whole dataset and use it to impute, the imputed values in your training rows contain information from your test rows. Your validation estimate is then measuring performance on data the model has partially seen. The effect is small for a mean and catastrophic for target encoding, but the principle is the same: any quantity learned from data must be learned from training data only.

The structural exception

Fixes that involve no learned statistic can happen before the split — parsing dates, casting types, stripping whitespace, deduplicating exact rows, correcting a known unit error. These are deterministic transformations of individual rows, so they cannot leak. Anything computed across rows — a mean, a median, a category frequency, a min and max — goes after.

Missing values

First, work out why they are missing

MechanismMeaningExampleConsequence
MCAR
missing completely at random		Missingness unrelated to anythingA sensor dropped packets at randomSafe to drop or impute; no bias introduced
MAR
missing at random		Missingness depends on observed variablesIncome missing more often for younger usersImputation using the other columns works well
MNAR
missing not at random		Missingness depends on the unobserved value itselfHigh earners decline to state incomeAny imputation is biased. The missingness is itself a feature

You cannot test for MNAR directly — that is what makes it dangerous — but you can look for evidence. Cross-tabulate missingness against the target and against other columns:

python
# does the fact of being missing predict the target?
for col in df.columns[df.isna().any()]:
    rates = df.groupby(df[col].isna())[target].mean()
    print(f"{col:24s} present={rates[False]:.3f}  missing={rates[True]:.3f}")

# do columns go missing together? (usually means one upstream failure)
df.isna().corr(numeric_only=False)

# is missingness related to time? (a pipeline broke on a specific date)
df.set_index("date")["income"].isna().resample("W").mean().plot()
Missingness is information

If income is missing for 40% of rows and those rows default at twice the rate, that pattern is one of your strongest features. Add a binary income_was_missing indicator as well as imputing, and let the model decide what to do with it. Imputing silently throws this away.

Outliers

The question is never "is this an outlier" but "is this an error or a real extreme value", and the two demand opposite responses.

DetectionRuleNotes
Z-score\(|z| > 3\)Assumes normality; the outliers themselves inflate σ and hide each other
IQROutside \(Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}\) to \(Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}\)Robust, distribution-free. The rule behind box plot whiskers
Modified z-scoreUses median and MAD instead of mean and σRobust version of the z-score; the sensible default
Isolation ForestPoints that are easy to isolate by random splitsMultivariate; catches combinations that look normal one column at a time
Local Outlier FactorDensity relative to neighboursFinds points anomalous for their local region
Domain rulesAge > 120, negative price, future timestampThe most valuable check, and the one most often skipped
A decision procedure
  1. Is it physically impossible? Negative age, blood pressure of 0, a transaction dated 2087. This is an error — treat it as missing, do not "fix" it to a plausible value.
  2. Is it a sentinel value? −999, 9999, 0 meaning "not recorded". Extremely common in legacy systems. Convert to NaN, then handle as missing.
  3. Is it a unit or scale error? One row in grams among kilograms. Correct it if you can establish the rule; otherwise drop.
  4. Is it genuinely extreme but real? A £2 million order from a corporate account. Keep it. Consider a log transform, a robust loss, or a model that does not care — and check whether it belongs to a segment you should model separately.
Do not routinely delete outliers

In fraud detection, anomaly detection and risk modelling, the outliers are the signal. Deleting the tail of the distribution because a rule flagged it is how you build a model that works perfectly on the 99% of cases nobody cared about. Winsorising — clipping at the 1st and 99th percentiles — is usually a gentler option than deletion, and keeps the row.

Encoding categorical features

Most models need numbers. How you turn categories into numbers is a modelling decision, not a formatting one.

EncodingProducesGood forWatch out for
One-hotOne binary column per levelLow cardinality (< ~15), linear models, neural netsExplodes with cardinality; the dummy variable trap
OrdinalIntegers 0, 1, 2 …Genuinely ordered categories: small < medium < largeImplies order and equal spacing where none exists
LabelArbitrary integersTree models, which can split anywhereMeaningless for linear models — implies fake ordering
Target / meanMean of y for that levelHigh cardinality with real signalLeaks badly unless computed inside CV folds with smoothing
Frequency / countHow often the level occursHigh cardinality; cheap and leak-freeTwo different levels with equal counts collide
HashingFixed-width vector via a hashVery high or unbounded cardinality, streamingCollisions; not interpretable
EmbeddingLearned dense vectorVery high cardinality inside a neural networkNeeds a lot of data per level
NativeModel handles it directlyLightGBM, CatBoostUsually the best result with the least code
python
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder, OrdinalEncoder

# handle_unknown is not optional — production will show you categories your
# training data never contained, and the default behaviour is to raise.
ohe = OneHotEncoder(
    handle_unknown="infrequent_if_exist",
    min_frequency=0.01,        # rare levels are grouped into one "infrequent" column
    sparse_output=False,
    drop="first",             # avoids perfect collinearity for LINEAR models
)

# ordinal only where an order genuinely exists
sizes = OrdinalEncoder(categories=[["small", "medium", "large"]])
The dummy variable trap

One-hot encoding k levels into k columns makes them sum to 1 in every row, which is perfectly collinear with the intercept. For linear regression this makes \(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) singular — the rank deficiency from chapter 2 — and coefficients become unstable or undefined. Dropping one level fixes it. Tree models and regularised linear models do not care, so drop="first" is for unregularised linear models specifically; using it elsewhere just makes coefficients harder to read.

Target encoding, and why it is a trap

Replacing each category with the mean target for that category is seductive: it compresses a thousand levels into one informative column. It is also the most reliable way to fabricate a model that scores brilliantly and predicts nothing.

The problem: a row's own target contributes to the mean used as its own feature. With enough categories that each contains only a handful of rows, the encoded value essentially is the label. The demo below runs this on data with no relationship at all between feature and target.

Target encoding on pure noise
There is no real signal in this data

python — target encoding done safely
# scikit-learn ≥ 1.3 has a built-in that cross-fits internally
from sklearn.preprocessing import TargetEncoder

enc = TargetEncoder(smooth="auto", cv=5)     # cv= is what prevents the leak

# the smoothing, written out, so you can see what it does:
#   encoded(level) = (n_level · mean_level + m · global_mean) / (n_level + m)
# a level with 2 rows is pulled almost entirely toward the global mean;
# a level with 5,000 rows keeps its own mean. m controls how fast that happens.
The rule

Any encoding that uses the target must be fitted inside the cross-validation loop, on training folds only, with smoothing for rare levels. If it lives in your preprocessing script and runs once over the whole dataframe, it is leaking. When in doubt, use frequency encoding or let LightGBM handle the categorical natively — you give up a little and remove a whole class of silent failure.

Scaling and transforming

Chapter 3 showed geometrically why this matters: features on different scales produce an elongated loss surface that gradient descent crawls across. Some models care enormously; others not at all.

Fit on train, transform everything

A scaler stores μ and σ (or min and max, or quantiles). Those are statistics learned from data, so they must come from the training set alone. scaler.fit_transform(X_train) then scaler.transform(X_test) — never fit_transform on the test set, and never on the full dataframe before splitting. The bias this introduces is small, which is exactly why it survives code review.

Class imbalance

Fraud at 0.2%, disease at 1%, churn at 5%. The model can score 99.8% accuracy by predicting "no" every time, and gradient descent will happily find that solution because it minimises the loss.

TacticHowVerdict
Change the metricPrecision, recall, F1, PR-AUC, cost-weighted errorDo this first, always. Often it is the whole fix
Class weightsclass_weight="balanced"Cheap, no data duplication, supported nearly everywhere
Threshold tuningMove the decision boundary off 0.5Underrated. Costs nothing and directly targets your operating point
Random undersamplingDrop majority rowsFast; throws away real data. Reasonable when the majority is huge
Random oversamplingDuplicate minority rowsEncourages overfitting to the duplicated points
SMOTESynthesise minority points between neighboursPopular; mixed evidence. Can invent implausible examples in high dimensions
Collect more positivesTargeted labellingThe real fix, when it is available
Resample after splitting, never before

Oversampling before the split copies minority rows into both train and test, so the model is tested on rows it trained on. Reported recall goes through the roof. Apply resampling inside the training fold only — imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline exists precisely to make this automatic. And never resample the test set at all: it must reflect the real class balance, or your precision estimate is meaningless.

Leakage, catalogued

Leakage is any situation where information that would not be available at prediction time finds its way into training. It is the most common cause of models that work in the notebook and fail in production, and it is worth learning to recognise by shape.

TypeExampleSymptomPrevention
Target leakageA cancellation_date column when predicting churnNear-perfect accuracy; one feature dominates importanceFor every feature ask: would this exist, with this value, before the event?
Train–test contaminationScaling or imputing before the splitValidation slightly too optimistic; production slightly worsePipelines; split first
Temporal leakageRandom split on time-ordered data; a centred rolling averageOffline good, live bad, degrades over timeTime-based splits; only backward-looking windows
Group leakageThe same patient in train and testHuge gap between offline and live performanceGroupKFold
Target encoding leakageCategory means computed over all rowsHigh-cardinality feature tops the importance chartCross-fitted encoding with smoothing
Duplicate leakageThe same record present twice, split across sidesSuspiciously high scores on near-duplicate-heavy dataDeduplicate before splitting, including near-duplicates
Feature-selection leakageChoosing features by correlation with y on the full dataCV score much better than the final test scoreSelection inside the CV loop
Proxy leakageA case_id that increments with outcome; a filename encoding the classAn ID column has predictive powerDrop identifiers; be suspicious of anything ordinal and unexplained
Three questions that catch most of it
  1. Is the score too good? 0.99 AUC on a hard business problem is evidence of a bug, not of skill. Investigate before celebrating.
  2. Which feature is doing the work? Look at feature importances. If one feature carries the model, ask exactly when its value is populated in the source system.
  3. Could I compute this feature at prediction time? Walk through the serving path concretely: at 09:00 on the day of scoring, does this value exist yet, and does it hold the same value it holds in your training table?

Pipelines

Everything in this chapter should live inside a scikit-learn Pipeline. Not for elegance — because a pipeline makes the fit-on-train rule structurally impossible to break, and because it gives you one object to serialise and serve.

python — a complete, honest preprocessing pipeline
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder, FunctionTransformer
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, cross_val_score, StratifiedKFold

numeric  = ["age", "tenure_months", "logins_30d"]
skewed   = ["income", "total_spend"]
nominal  = ["country", "plan", "channel"]

numeric_pipe = Pipeline([
    ("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="median", add_indicator=True)),   # keeps the missingness flag
    ("scale",  StandardScaler()),
])

skewed_pipe = Pipeline([
    ("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
    ("log",    FunctionTransformer(np.log1p, feature_names_out="one-to-one")),
    ("scale",  StandardScaler()),
])

nominal_pipe = Pipeline([
    ("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="constant", fill_value="missing")),
    ("ohe",    OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="infrequent_if_exist", min_frequency=0.01)),
])

pre = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", numeric_pipe, numeric),
    ("skew", skewed_pipe,  skewed),
    ("cat", nominal_pipe, nominal),
], remainder="drop")          # explicit: anything unlisted is dropped, not silently passed

model = Pipeline([
    ("pre", pre),
    ("clf", HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=42)),
])

# split FIRST
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=42)

# every fold refits the whole preprocessing chain on its own training rows.
# this is the property that makes the estimate trustworthy.
cv = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=cv, scoring="average_precision")
print(f"PR-AUC {scores.mean():.3f} ± {scores.std():.3f}")

model.fit(X_train, y_train)          # one object holds preprocessing + model
import joblib; joblib.dump(model, "model.joblib")   # and one object gets deployed
Why the single object matters at serving time

The commonest production bug in classical ML is training–serving skew: the preprocessing code in the training notebook and the preprocessing code in the API drift apart, so the model receives differently-shaped inputs from the ones it learned on. Serialising the fitted pipeline eliminates the possibility — the exact fitted imputers, scalers and encoders travel with the model.

Self-check

  1. You standardise your features and then split into train and test. What did you leak, and how much does it matter?
    The μ and σ used for scaling were computed using the test rows, so the training data has been shaped by information from the test set. In practice the bias is small for a mean and standard deviation over a decent-sized dataset — but it is a habit that scales to catastrophic when the same pattern is applied to target encoding or feature selection. Split first and put the scaler in a pipeline; then the question never arises.
  2. A column is 45% missing. Do you drop it?
    Not without checking two things. First, does the missingness itself relate to the target? Group the target by col.isna() — if the rates differ, the missingness is a feature and you should keep an indicator regardless of what you do with the values. Second, does the observed 55% carry signal? If both answers are no, dropping is reasonable. If either is yes, impute plus indicator, or use a model that handles NaN natively.
  3. Your gradient boosting model reaches 0.98 AUC on a churn problem where the business expects 0.75. What do you do?
    Assume leakage until proven otherwise. Print feature importances and look at the top one or two. Then trace those columns back to the source system and establish when their values are written — a status field, a cancellation timestamp, a flag set by the retention team after a customer calls to leave. Also verify the split respects both grouping and time. It is nearly always one column, and it is nearly always obvious once you look.
  4. Why is target encoding especially dangerous compared with one-hot encoding?
    Because it uses the target to build the feature. Each row's own label contributes to the category mean that becomes its own feature value, so with small categories the feature approaches being a copy of the label. One-hot encoding never touches y, so it cannot leak. The demo in this chapter produces above-chance cross-validation accuracy on data with literally no signal, purely from this mechanism.
  5. When is scaling a waste of time?
    For tree-based models — decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting. They split on thresholds within one feature at a time, and any monotonic transform of a feature produces the same splits in a different coordinate. Scaling changes nothing about the model, so it only costs you time and a little interpretability. Anything distance-based, gradient-based or regularised does need it.
  6. You oversample the minority class to balance your dataset and recall jumps from 0.4 to 0.9. What should you check?
    Whether you oversampled before splitting. If duplicated minority rows landed in both train and test, the model is being scored on rows it memorised. Redo it with resampling inside the training fold only — imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline handles this — and leave the test set at its natural class balance. Also check precision, which oversampling usually damages while flattering recall.
