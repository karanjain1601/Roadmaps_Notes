Linear algebra

Data arrives as a table, and a table is a matrix. That single fact is why linear algebra is the native language of machine learning: it gives you a compact notation for "do this to every row at once", and a geometric picture for what your models are doing to the space your data lives in.

— words— min read3 interactivesNeeds: school algebra

Why this chapter exists

You can train a model without linear algebra. You cannot debug one, read a paper, understand why your GPU is idle, or grasp what PCA is doing to your features. This chapter covers the parts that earn their place, and skips the parts that do not.

Three things become possible once the notation clicks:

Compactness

The prediction of a linear model over an entire dataset is ŷ = Xw. Four characters replace three nested loops, and the notation is identical whether you have 10 rows or 10 million.

Speed

Expressing computation as matrix operations lets BLAS, SIMD instructions and GPUs do the work. Vectorised NumPy is routinely 50–200× faster than the equivalent Python loop.

Geometry

Every model becomes a shape. Linear regression is a projection; PCA is a rotation; a neural layer is a transformation followed by a bend. Geometry is how you build intuition that survives new architectures.

What you can skip

Determinant cofactor expansion by hand, Cramer's rule, Gram–Schmidt by hand, hand-computing inverses of anything larger than 2×2. You will never do these in practice, and libraries do them better. Understanding what they mean matters; performing them does not.

Scalars

A single number: a learning rate η = 0.01, a bias term, a loss value. Written in lowercase italic, \(x \in \mathbb{R}\). The notation \(\mathbb{R}\) means "the real numbers", and \(\in\) means "is a member of" — so that reads "x is a real number".

Vectors

An ordered list of numbers, written bold lowercase. A vector with d entries lives in \(\mathbb{R}^d\):

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_d \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$$

In machine learning a vector is almost always one sample: one customer described by 20 numbers, one word described by a 300-dimensional embedding, one image flattened into 784 pixel intensities. By convention vectors are columns; a row is written \(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\) (x transpose).

Two ways to see a vector

As an arrow from the origin to a point — useful in 2D and 3D for intuition. As a point in a d-dimensional space — the only picture that survives past three dimensions. When people say "the model puts similar documents close together", they mean the points are close under some distance. Both views are the same object; use whichever helps.

Matrices

A rectangular grid, bold uppercase, with the row count first: \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) has m rows and n columns. The entry in row i, column j is \(A_{ij}\).

The single most important matrix in this unit is the design matrix \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\): one row per sample, one column per feature.

Tensors

Rank 3 and above. The word carries mathematical baggage elsewhere, but in ML it just means a multi-dimensional array. Where they show up:

Vector operations

Addition and scaling

Both are elementwise, and both require matching shapes:

$$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix}u_1 + v_1 \\ \vdots \\ u_d + v_d\end{bmatrix}, \qquad c\,\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix}c\,v_1 \\ \vdots \\ c\,v_d\end{bmatrix}$$

Geometrically, addition places one arrow's tail at the other's head; scaling stretches an arrow (and flips it if c is negative). A linear combination — sums of scaled vectors, \(c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots\) — is the fundamental operation of the whole subject. Every layer of a neural network computes linear combinations of its inputs before applying a nonlinearity.

The dot product

The single most important operation in machine learning. Two definitions, both true:

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{d} u_i v_i \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta$$

θ is the angle between the two vectors; ‖u‖ is the length of u, defined just below. The first form is how you compute it; the second is what it means.

The result is a scalar, and its sign tells you about alignment:

Dot productAngleMeaningML reading
Large positivenear 0°Pointing the same wayStrong match; high similarity score
Zero90°OrthogonalUnrelated; independent directions
Large negativenear 180°Pointing opposite waysStrong anti-match

Where you meet it, over and over:

  • Linear model prediction: \(\hat{y} = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b\) — one dot product of weights and features.
  • A neuron: exactly the same thing, followed by an activation function.
  • Similarity search: retrieval systems rank documents by the dot product of query and document embeddings.
  • Attention: a transformer scores how much token i should attend to token j with \(\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{k}_j\). Chapter 19.
  • Projection: the building block of PCA and least squares.

Norms: measuring length

A norm turns a vector into a single non-negative number representing its size. Three matter:

$$\|\mathbf{v}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i v_i^2}\quad(\text{L2, Euclidean}) \qquad \|\mathbf{v}\|_1 = \sum_i |v_i|\quad(\text{L1, Manhattan}) \qquad \|\mathbf{v}\|_\infty = \max_i |v_i|$$

L2 is ordinary straight-line distance and is the default everywhere. L1 is the distance a taxi drives on a grid. The choice is not cosmetic — it shapes what your model learns:

NormAs a penalty on weightsEffectName of the method
L2\(\lambda\sum w_j^2\)Shrinks all weights smoothly toward zero, rarely to exactly zeroRidge regression, weight decay
L1\(\lambda\sum |w_j|\)Drives many weights to exactly zero — automatic feature selectionLasso
Bothmix of the twoSparsity plus stability with correlated featuresElastic net

Chapter 11 shows geometrically why the L1 ball's corners produce exact zeros while the L2 sphere does not. For now, note that "regularisation" is mostly just "add a norm to the loss".

Cosine similarity

Normalise the dot product by both lengths and you measure pure direction, ignoring magnitude:

$$\text{cos\_sim}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_2} \in [-1, 1]$$

This is the standard similarity measure for embeddings. For text it matters because a long document and a short one about the same topic have similar directions but very different magnitudes; cosine ignores the length difference. Note that if all vectors are unit length — which many embedding models guarantee — cosine similarity and the plain dot product are identical, which is why vector databases can use either.

Projection, and why it is the whole of least squares

The projection of \(\mathbf{v}\) onto \(\mathbf{u}\) is the shadow \(\mathbf{v}\) casts on the line through \(\mathbf{u}\):

$$\text{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{u}$$

The leftover part, \(\mathbf{v} - \text{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{v})\), is orthogonal to \(\mathbf{u}\) — drag the vectors above and watch the dashed residual stay at right angles. That orthogonality is the least-squares solution: fitting a linear model means projecting the target vector \(\mathbf{y}\) onto the space spanned by your feature columns, and the residuals come out perpendicular to every feature. Chapter 11 makes this concrete.

Matrix operations

Transpose

Flip rows and columns: \((\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{ij} = A_{ji}\), so an \(m \times n\) matrix becomes \(n \times m\). Useful identities, worth memorising because they appear in every derivation:

$$(\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{\top} = \mathbf{A} \qquad (\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})^{\top} = \mathbf{A}^{\top}+\mathbf{B}^{\top} \qquad (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^{\top} = \mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}$$

Note the reversal in the last one. It catches everyone once.

Matrix multiplication

The operation that everything reduces to. For \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) and \(\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\), the product \(\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{AB}\) is \(m \times p\) with

$$C_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} A_{ik}B_{kj}$$

Entry (i,j) of the result is the dot product of row i of A with column j of B.

Three ways to read a matrix product

All three are used in papers and all three are worth holding:

  1. Many dot products at once. \(\mathbf{Xw}\) computes the prediction for every row of X in a single operation. This is the vectorisation view.
  2. Composition of transformations. If B rotates and A stretches, then AB is "rotate, then stretch" applied as one matrix. Chaining layers in a network without activations collapses to exactly this — which is why activations are necessary.
  3. A weighted sum of columns. \(\mathbf{Av}\) is \(v_1(\text{col}_1) + v_2(\text{col}_2) + \dots\) — the output lives in the space spanned by the columns of A. This is the view that makes rank and column space obvious.
Not commutative

\(\mathbf{AB} \neq \mathbf{BA}\) in general — often the shapes do not even permit both. Order encodes meaning: "rotate then stretch" is a different transformation from "stretch then rotate". Matrix multiplication is associative, \((\mathbf{AB})\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{BC})\), and choosing the cheaper grouping is a real optimisation — multiplying a \(1000\times2\) by a \(2\times1000\) by a \(1000\times1\) costs 4,000 operations one way and 2,000,000 the other.

Elementwise (Hadamard) product

Written \(\mathbf{A} \odot \mathbf{B}\), multiplying matching entries. Completely different from matrix multiplication, and appears in LSTM gates (chapter 18), attention masks, and dropout. In NumPy, A * B is elementwise and A @ B is matrix multiplication — one of the most consequential single characters in the language.

Matrices as transformations

A matrix does not have to be data. Multiply every point in a space by a fixed matrix and the space itself deforms — stretched, rotated, sheared, flattened. This is the picture that makes determinants, rank and eigenvectors feel inevitable rather than arbitrary.

Determinant

For a 2×2 matrix, \(\det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix} = ad - bc\). It is the factor by which areas are scaled (volumes in 3D, hypervolumes above that). Push the sliders above and watch the shaded unit square change size.

det = 1
Area preserved — a rotation or a shear.
det = 3
Everything triples in area.
det < 0
Space has been flipped over as well as scaled.
det = 0
The transformation squashes space into a lower dimension. Information is destroyed and cannot be recovered — the matrix is singular and has no inverse.

Inverse

\(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) undoes \(\mathbf{A}\), so \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I}\). It exists only when \(\det(\mathbf{A}) \neq 0\). For 2×2 there is a formula worth knowing:

$$\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}$$
Never actually invert a matrix

To solve \(\mathbf{Ax}=\mathbf{b}\), do not compute \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\). Use a solver: np.linalg.solve(A, b). It is faster and dramatically more accurate numerically. The inverse appears constantly in textbook formulas — including the normal equation in chapter 11 — and is almost never computed literally in production code. When a matrix is nearly singular, inverting it amplifies floating-point noise catastrophically; the measure of this is the condition number, and a large one is the usual explanation for "my linear model's coefficients are absurd".

Rank

The number of linearly independent columns — the true dimensionality of the output space. A \(1000 \times 50\) matrix whose rank is 47 tells you three of your fifty features are exact linear combinations of the others, carrying no new information.

This is not academic. Rank deficiency in your design matrix means:

  • \(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) is singular, so the normal equation has no unique solution and linear regression coefficients become unstable or arbitrary.
  • Duplicated or perfectly correlated features are present — often accidentally, via one-hot encoding all categories instead of dropping one (the dummy variable trap, chapter 8).
  • Regularisation fixes it: adding \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) to \(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) makes it invertible again. That is literally what ridge regression does, and it is a large part of why ridge is more numerically reliable than plain OLS.

Trace

The sum of the diagonal, \(\text{tr}(\mathbf{A}) = \sum_i A_{ii}\). It equals the sum of eigenvalues, and it has the cyclic property \(\text{tr}(\mathbf{ABC}) = \text{tr}(\mathbf{BCA})\), which is the trick behind half the matrix-calculus derivations you will read.

Eigenvectors and eigenvalues

Most vectors change direction when a matrix acts on them. A few special ones do not — they only get longer or shorter. Those are the eigenvectors, and they reveal what a transformation fundamentally does.

$$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}$$

v is an eigenvector (a direction unchanged by A) and λ is its eigenvalue (how much it stretches). Turn on "show eigenvectors" in the demo above and watch them stay on their own lines while everything else swings around.

Interpreting the eigenvalue:

λ > 1 — stretched along that direction 0 < λ < 1 — squashed λ < 0 — flipped and scaled λ = 0 — collapsed to nothing; matrix is singular complex λ — pure rotation, no real invariant direction

The covariance connection, in one paragraph

Centre your data, then \(\mathbf{C} = \frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) is the covariance matrix: entry \((i,j)\) says how features i and j vary together. Because it is symmetric, its eigenvectors are orthogonal and its eigenvalues are real and non-negative. The top eigenvector points along the direction your data is most spread out in; its eigenvalue is that spread. Keep the top k and you have PCA — a rotation into a coordinate system where the axes are ordered by how much they matter.

Singular value decomposition

SVD is the most useful factorisation in applied mathematics, and unlike eigen-decomposition it works on any matrix — rectangular, singular, whatever you have.

$$\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}$$

U (m×m) and V (n×n) are orthogonal — pure rotations. Σ (m×n) is diagonal with non-negative entries \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0\), the singular values.

Geometrically the statement is remarkable: every linear transformation, no matter how complicated it looks, is a rotation, then an axis-aligned stretch, then another rotation. Nothing else is possible.

Low-rank approximation, and why it is everywhere

Because the singular values are sorted, truncating after k terms gives the best possible rank-k approximation of the matrix in a precise sense (the Eckart–Young theorem):

$$\mathbf{A} \approx \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_i\,\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^{\top}$$

Storage drops from \(m \times n\) numbers to \(k(m+n+1)\). If the singular values decay quickly — and for real data they almost always do — a small k loses almost nothing. Drag the slider below to see how few components a structured image actually needs.

Where SVD shows up

PCA
Running SVD on the centred data matrix is the numerically preferred way to do PCA — you never form the covariance matrix at all.
Recommender systems
The classic matrix-factorisation approach decomposes a sparse user × item rating matrix into low-rank user and item factors.
Latent semantic analysis
SVD of a term × document matrix produces topic-like directions; the ancestor of modern embeddings.
Pseudo-inverse
SVD defines \(\mathbf{A}^{+}\) even when \(\mathbf{A}\) is singular, giving the minimum-norm least-squares solution. This is what np.linalg.lstsq uses.
Model compression
LoRA fine-tuning updates a large weight matrix with a low-rank product \(\mathbf{BA}\) — the same idea, applied to adapting large models cheaply.
Noise reduction
Signal often lives in the top components and noise in the tail, so truncating denoises. Watch the "noisy" preset above.

Doing it in NumPy

Everything above, in the syntax you will actually type. Chapter 6 goes deeper on NumPy itself; this is the linear-algebra subset.

python
import numpy as np

# ---- creating ----
v = np.array([3.0, 4.0])                 # shape (2,)  — a vector
A = np.array([[1., 2.], [3., 4.]])         # shape (2, 2)
np.zeros((3, 4)); np.ones(5); np.eye(3)   # zeros, ones, identity
np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=(100, 5))  # seeded random data

# ---- shapes: check these constantly ----
A.shape      # (2, 2)
A.ndim       # 2      — the rank
A.T          # transpose

# ---- the crucial distinction ----
A * A        # elementwise (Hadamard)
A @ A        # matrix multiplication  ← almost always what you want
np.dot(v, v) # 25.0  — dot product of two 1-D arrays

# ---- norms and similarity ----
np.linalg.norm(v)            # 5.0     L2 by default
np.linalg.norm(v, ord=1)     # 7.0     L1
np.linalg.norm(v, ord=np.inf)# 4.0     max

def cosine(u, w):
    return u @ w / (np.linalg.norm(u) * np.linalg.norm(w))

# ---- solving, not inverting ----
b = np.array([5., 6.])
x = np.linalg.solve(A, b)    # do this
x = np.linalg.inv(A) @ b     # not this: slower and less accurate

# ---- decompositions ----
det  = np.linalg.det(A)
rank = np.linalg.matrix_rank(A)
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A)        # general square matrix
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(A)       # use for symmetric: faster, sorted, real
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)

# rank-k reconstruction
k = 10
A_k = (U[:, :k] * s[:k]) @ Vt[:k]

# ---- the ML one-liner ----
X = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=(1000, 20))   # 1000 samples, 20 features
w = np.random.default_rng(1).normal(size=20)
y_hat = X @ w + 0.5          # every prediction, one operation, shape (1000,)

Habit worth forming

Write the expected shape as a comment beside every line that changes one. When something breaks three functions later, the comments turn a thirty-minute hunt into a ten-second scan. Serious codebases go further and assert shapes explicitly.

Why any of this is fast

Multiplying an \(n \times n\) by an \(n \times n\) matrix costs roughly \(2n^3\) floating-point operations. For n = 1000 that is two billion — and a laptop does it in well under a second. Three reasons:

  • The operations are independent. Every output entry can be computed in parallel, which suits SIMD units and GPU cores exactly.
  • Memory access is predictable. Optimised BLAS libraries tile the computation so data stays in cache. This is worth more than raw arithmetic speed on modern hardware.
  • Decades of tuning. NumPy calls into OpenBLAS or MKL, which are hand-optimised per CPU architecture. You will not beat them in Python, and should not try.

This is the real reason deep learning happened when it did. GPUs were built to multiply matrices for graphics; a neural network's forward pass is a chain of matrix multiplications. The hardware arrived before the demand for it, and training times fell by two orders of magnitude when people noticed.

Self-check

  1. X has shape (500, 12) and w has shape (12,). What is the shape of X @ w, and what does each entry mean?
    (500,) — one number per sample, namely the dot product of that sample's features with the weights. That is the prediction vector of a linear model before adding the bias.
  2. Two embedding vectors have a dot product of 0. What does that tell you, and would cosine similarity tell you anything different?
    They are orthogonal — at 90°, sharing no directional component. Cosine similarity would also be 0, since it is the dot product divided by two positive lengths. Cosine differs from the dot product only in ignoring magnitude, so on unit-length vectors the two are identical.
  3. Your linear regression returns coefficients in the millions with opposite signs on two similar features. What is the likely cause and the fix?
    Near-collinearity: the two features are almost linear combinations of each other, so \(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) is nearly singular and the coefficients can grow enormous while cancelling out. High condition number. Fix by dropping one feature, combining them, or adding L2 regularisation — ridge adds \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\), which restores invertibility and shrinks the wild coefficients.
  4. Why does a determinant of zero mean no inverse exists?
    Zero determinant means the transformation collapses space onto a lower-dimensional subspace — a plane onto a line, say. Many different inputs map to the same output, so there is no way to run the map backwards; the information distinguishing them is gone. An inverse would have to pick one, and cannot.
  5. A colleague computes np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X) @ X.T @ y. It works but they report unstable results on some datasets. What would you suggest?
    That is the normal equation implemented literally. Suggest np.linalg.lstsq(X, y), which uses SVD and handles rank-deficient or ill-conditioned X gracefully, or scipy.linalg.solve with a regularisation term. The instability is exactly the condition-number problem: forming X.T @ X squares the condition number, doubling the number of digits of precision you lose.
  6. An image is 1000×800. You keep 20 SVD components. How much storage do you use, as a fraction of the original?
    Original: 1000 × 800 = 800,000 values. Rank-20: 20 × (1000 + 800 + 1) = 36,020 values, about 4.5%. Whether it still looks like the photograph depends entirely on how fast the singular values decay — try the "noisy" preset in the demo to see a case where they barely decay at all.
  7. Why are activation functions necessary between neural network layers?
    Because composing linear transformations gives another linear transformation: \(\mathbf{W}_2(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{W}_1)\mathbf{x}\), a single equivalent matrix. Without a nonlinearity in between, a hundred-layer network has exactly the expressive power of one layer. Chapter 16 makes this concrete.
