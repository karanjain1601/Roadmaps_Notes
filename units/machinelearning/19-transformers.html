Transformers

Chapter 18 ended with attention bolted onto a recurrent model. The 2017 paper asked what happens if you remove the recurrence and keep only the attention. The answer turned out to be: everything gets better, and it parallelises.

The problem with recurrence

RNN / LSTMTransformer
TrainingSequential — step t needs step t−1Fully parallel across positions
Path length between two positionsO(n) — information passes through every step betweenO(1) — any position reaches any other directly
Cost per layerO(n·d²)O(n²·d) — quadratic in sequence length
Long-range dependenciesPossible with gating, still hardDirect

The middle row is the conceptual win and the third is the price. An RNN passing information from word 1 to word 200 must carry it through 199 intermediate states, each of which can dilute it. Attention connects them in a single operation. In exchange, every position attends to every other, so cost grows with the square of sequence length — which is why context windows were small for years and why so much engineering has gone into making attention cheaper.

The parallelism matters just as much commercially. An RNN cannot start step 500 until step 499 finishes, so a GPU sits mostly idle. A transformer computes all positions simultaneously as one large matrix product — exactly the operation hardware is built for. That is what made training on internet-scale corpora practical.

Self-attention, derived

Every token asks a question, every token advertises what it offers, and every token carries content. Those three roles are the query, the key and the value.

  1. Project each token three ways. From its embedding \(\mathbf{x}_i\), compute \(\mathbf{q}_i = \mathbf{W}_Q\mathbf{x}_i\), \(\mathbf{k}_i = \mathbf{W}_K\mathbf{x}_i\), \(\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{W}_V\mathbf{x}_i\). These three matrices are the only learned parameters in the mechanism.
  2. Score every pair. The relevance of token j to token i is the dot product \(\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{k}_j\) — chapter 2's alignment measure, doing exactly the job it was introduced for.
  3. Scale and normalise. Divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\), then softmax across j so the weights for each query sum to 1.
  4. Mix the values. The output for token i is the weighted average \(\sum_j \alpha_{ij}\mathbf{v}_j\).
$$\text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V}$$
Why divide by √dk

A dot product of two random d-dimensional vectors with unit-variance components has variance d, so it grows as \(\sqrt{d}\). With d = 64 the raw scores are large, and softmax of large numbers is nearly one-hot — one weight close to 1, the rest near 0. That saturated softmax has almost zero gradient, so the layer stops learning. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) keeps the scores at a sensible scale. Turn off the scaling in the demo below and watch the distribution collapse onto a single token.

Multi-head attention

One attention operation produces one weighted average, which forces a single notion of relevance. Multi-head attention runs several in parallel with separate projections, each of a smaller dimension, then concatenates the results and projects back:

$$\text{MultiHead} = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1,\dots,\text{head}_h)\mathbf{W}_O, \qquad \text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_Q^i,\, \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_K^i,\, \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_V^i)$$

With \(d_{\text{model}} = 512\) and 8 heads, each head works in 64 dimensions, so the total cost is unchanged. Different heads reliably specialise — some track syntactic dependencies, some attend to the previous token, some to rare content words. Switch heads in the demo to see three such patterns.

Attention weights are not explanations

It is tempting to read a heatmap as "the model used this word to decide". Several papers have shown attention weights can be substantially altered without changing the output, and that different weight patterns produce identical predictions. They show where information was gathered from, not what drove the decision — especially after a dozen layers of mixing. Treat them as a debugging aid, not evidence. Chapter 22 covers attribution methods with firmer foundations.

Positional encoding

Attention is a weighted sum, and a sum does not care about order — shuffle the input tokens and the outputs are the same, merely permuted. That is a serious problem for language, so position must be injected explicitly.

Attention sub-layer
Mixes information across positions. This is the only place tokens communicate.
Feed-forward sub-layer
Two linear layers with a nonlinearity, applied to each position independently. Typically 4× the model width, and it holds roughly two-thirds of the parameters.
Residuals
Around both sub-layers. Without them, deep transformers do not train.
Layer norm, not batch norm
Normalising across features per token is batch-size independent and behaves identically in training and inference — which matters when sequence lengths vary. Pre-norm (before the sub-layer) trains more stably than the original post-norm and needs less warmup.

Three families

FamilyAttentionTrained byGood forExamples
Encoder-onlyBidirectional — every token sees all othersMasked language modelling: hide 15% of tokens, predict themClassification, retrieval, embeddings, token labellingBERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa
Decoder-onlyCausal — each token sees only what precedes itNext-token predictionGeneration, and increasingly everything elseGPT, Llama, Claude, Mistral
Encoder–decoderBoth, plus cross-attention from decoder to encoderSpan corruption or translation pairsTranslation, summarisation, structured transductionT5, BART, original transformer

The causal mask is what separates the first two: set the attention scores for all future positions to −∞ before the softmax, so their weights become zero. This lets a decoder be trained on every position of a sequence simultaneously while still only ever seeing the past — the trick that makes next-token prediction efficient enough to run on trillions of tokens. Scale and its costs

Transformer performance improves with model size, dataset size and compute in a relationship smooth enough to extrapolate — the scaling laws. The Chinchilla work refined the recipe: for a fixed compute budget, most large models had been too big and undertrained, and roughly 20 training tokens per parameter is closer to optimal.

CostScales asConsequence
Attention compute and memoryO(n²) in sequence lengthDoubling context quadruples the cost. The binding constraint on long context
ParametersO(d²) per layerMemory for weights, gradients and optimiser state — roughly 16 bytes per parameter when training with Adam
KV cache at inferenceO(n · layers · d)Often the real limit on how many users you can serve at once
FlashAttention
Computes exact attention without ever materialising the n × n matrix, by tiling the computation to stay in fast on-chip memory. Same result, several times faster, far less memory. Now the default implementation.
Sliding window
Each token attends only to a local window, with global tokens for long-range links. Linear in n, at the cost of exactness.
Grouped-query attention
Share key and value projections across several query heads, shrinking the KV cache substantially at little quality cost.
Mixture of experts
Replace the feed-forward layer with many experts and route each token to a couple of them. Parameter count grows while compute per token stays roughly fixed.
State-space models
Mamba and similar architectures revisit recurrence with modern design, achieving linear scaling in sequence length. An active alternative rather than a settled replacement.

Using them

from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSequenceClassification, Trainer, TrainingArguments

name = "distilbert-base-uncased"
tok  = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(name)
model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(name, num_labels=3)

def prep(batch):
    return tok(batch["text"], truncation=True, max_length=256)   # pad dynamically per batch

args = TrainingArguments(
    output_dir="out",
    learning_rate=2e-5,               # two orders below a from-scratch rate
    per_device_train_batch_size=16,
    num_train_epochs=3,               # fine-tuning needs very few epochs
    warmup_ratio=0.1,                 # transformers want warmup
    weight_decay=0.01,
    eval_strategy="epoch",
    load_best_model_at_end=True,
    fp16=True,
)

# ---- parameter-efficient fine-tuning: train ~0.5% of the weights ----
from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model
model = get_peft_model(model, LoraConfig(
    r=16, lora_alpha=32, lora_dropout=0.05,
    target_modules=["q_lin", "v_lin"],      # attention projections only
))
model.print_trainable_parameters()
LoRA, and why it is chapter 2 again

Full fine-tuning updates every weight, which for a large model means storing a full copy per task. LoRA freezes the original matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) and learns a low-rank update \(\mathbf{BA}\) with rank 8 or 16 — the low-rank approximation from chapter 2, applied to weight updates. For a 4096 × 4096 matrix that is 130,000 trainable parameters instead of 16.7 million, and the adapter is a few megabytes you can swap per task. Quality is usually close to full fine-tuning, and the technique is now the default way to adapt large models.

How the largest models are trained

  1. Pretraining. Next-token prediction over trillions of tokens. This is where almost all the capability comes from, and almost all the compute goes.
  2. Supervised fine-tuning. Train on curated instruction–response pairs so the model answers questions rather than continuing text.
  3. Preference optimisation. Collect human comparisons between responses, train a reward model, and optimise the policy against it with PPO — chapter 14's actor-critic machinery — or skip the reward model with a direct method such as DPO.

Beyond text: vision transformers cut an image into 16 × 16 patches, embed each as a token, and apply the identical architecture — they beat convolutional networks given enough data, and lose below that because they lack the built-in assumption that signal is local. Multimodal models project images, audio and text into one token stream so a single transformer handles all of them.

Self-check

  1. Why divide attention scores by √dk?
    The dot product of two d-dimensional vectors with unit-variance entries has standard deviation about √d. Large scores make the softmax nearly one-hot, and a saturated softmax has almost no gradient, so the layer stops learning. Scaling keeps the score distribution in a range where softmax stays soft and differentiable. Untick the scaling box in the demo and the weights collapse onto a single token.
  2. What exactly does the causal mask do, and why is it needed?
    It sets the attention scores for all future positions to −∞ before the softmax, making their weights exactly zero. Without it, a model trained to predict the next token could simply read that token from the input — trivially perfect training loss and no ability to generate. With it, every position can be trained in parallel while each one still only sees its own past, which is what makes next-token pretraining computationally feasible at scale.
  3. Why does a transformer need positional encoding when an RNN does not?
    An RNN consumes tokens one at a time, so order is built into the computation. Attention is a weighted sum over a set, and a sum is permutation-invariant — shuffle the inputs and you get the same outputs in shuffled order. Position must therefore be added to the representation explicitly, either as an additive encoding or, as in RoPE, by rotating the query and key vectors by an angle proportional to position.
  4. Your context window doubles from 4k to 8k tokens. What happens to the cost?
    Attention compute and memory roughly quadruple, since both scale with n². The feed-forward layers only double, as they are per-position. The KV cache at inference also doubles. This is why long context was hard for so long, and why FlashAttention, sliding-window attention and grouped-query attention exist — each attacks one part of that scaling.
  5. You have 2,000 labelled examples and want a model that outputs a specific JSON format. What do you try, in order?
    Prompting with a few examples first — it costs nothing and frequently works, especially with a constrained decoding or schema-enforcement option. If the format is still inconsistent, LoRA fine-tuning on the 2,000 examples is the natural next step: it trains a fraction of a percent of the weights, runs on modest hardware, and produces a small swappable adapter. Full fine-tuning would be overkill at this data size and risks degrading the model's general ability.
  6. Can you use attention weights to explain a prediction to a regulator?
    Not safely. Research has shown that attention weights can often be changed substantially without altering the output, and that different distributions produce identical predictions — so they are not faithful attributions. After many layers of mixing, a weight tells you where information was gathered from, not what caused the decision. Use them for debugging, and use gradient- or perturbation-based attribution from chapter 22 when the explanation has to hold up.
