Probability

Every prediction is a claim under uncertainty, and probability is the arithmetic of uncertainty. It also answers a question the previous chapter dodged: where do loss functions come from? Not from taste — from assuming a distribution and asking which parameters make your data most likely.

— words— min read4 interactivesNeeds: chapters 02–03

Why probability runs through everything

Four distinct jobs, and you meet all of them before chapter 16.

Expressing uncertainty

A classifier that says "spam" is less useful than one that says "0.93 spam". Probabilities let you set thresholds, rank by risk, and abstain when unsure.

Deriving loss functions

Squared error and cross-entropy are not arbitrary. Each is the negative log-likelihood of a specific assumption about how your data was generated.

Modelling the data itself

Naive Bayes, mixture models, variational autoencoders and diffusion models are all statements about probability distributions.

Reasoning about evidence

Bayes' rule tells you how to update a belief when new data arrives — and why a 99%-accurate test can still be wrong most of the time.

The basic machinery

Sample spaces and events

The sample space \(\Omega\) is everything that could happen: for one die roll, \(\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\). An event is any subset — "rolled even" is \(\{2,4,6\}\). A probability assigns each event a number obeying three rules:

Non-negative
\(P(A) \ge 0\) for every event.
Normalised
\(P(\Omega) = 1\) — something happens.
Additive
For mutually exclusive events, \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)\).

Everything else follows, including the one people misremember: for events that can overlap,

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

Subtract the overlap, or you count it twice. ∪ is "or", ∩ is "and".

Joint, marginal, conditional

Three views of the same table. Suppose you record whether an email is spam and whether it contains the word "invoice":

has "invoice"no "invoice"row total
spam0.120.180.30
not spam0.070.630.70
column total0.190.811.00
Joint
\(P(\text{spam}, \text{invoice}) = 0.12\) — a single cell. Both things together.
Marginal
\(P(\text{spam}) = 0.30\) — a row total. Sum out the variable you do not care about. The name comes from writing these sums in the margin of the table.
Conditional
\(P(\text{spam} \mid \text{invoice}) = 0.12 / 0.19 = 0.63\) — restrict to one column and renormalise. "Given that."

The conditional definition is worth writing out, because Bayes' rule falls straight out of it:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A, B)}{P(B)} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad P(A,B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)$$
The direction matters enormously

\(P(A \mid B)\) and \(P(B \mid A)\) are different numbers, and confusing them is the single most common probability error in the wild. \(P(\text{positive test} \mid \text{disease})\) is a property of the test and is usually high. \(P(\text{disease} \mid \text{positive test})\) is what the patient wants to know and can be low. The demo further down makes the gap visible.

Independence

Two events are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other:

$$P(A, B) = P(A)\,P(B) \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$

In the table above, \(P(\text{spam})P(\text{invoice}) = 0.30 \times 0.19 = 0.057\), but the joint is 0.12 — more than double. Not independent, which is precisely why the word is a useful feature.

Conditional independence is the weaker and more useful cousin: A and B may be dependent overall yet independent once you know C. Naive Bayes (chapter 12) assumes all features are conditionally independent given the class. That assumption is almost always false, and the classifier works well anyway — one of the field's more instructive embarrassments.

The chain rule of probability

$$P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = P(x_1)\,P(x_2 \mid x_1)\,P(x_3 \mid x_1,x_2)\cdots P(x_n \mid x_1,\dots,x_{n-1})$$

This is how a language model works. The probability of a sentence is decomposed into the probability of each token given everything before it, and the model's only job is estimating those conditionals. Chapter 19 returns to it.

Bayes' theorem

Rearranging the conditional definition twice gives the rule for turning \(P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{hypothesis})\) into \(P(\text{hypothesis} \mid \text{evidence})\) — which is nearly always the direction you actually need.

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\;P(H)}{P(E)}$$
TermNameReading
\(P(H)\)PriorBelief in the hypothesis before seeing this evidence
\(P(E \mid H)\)LikelihoodHow well the hypothesis explains the evidence
\(P(E)\)Evidence / marginalHow likely the evidence is overall, across all hypotheses
\(P(H \mid E)\)PosteriorUpdated belief after seeing the evidence

The denominator is usually computed by summing over every hypothesis, which for two outcomes is:

$$P(E) = P(E \mid H)P(H) + P(E \mid \neg H)P(\neg H)$$

The example everyone should work through once

A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test detects it in 99% of people who have it (sensitivity), and correctly clears 95% of people who do not (specificity). You test positive. What is the chance you have the disease?

Most people answer around 95%. The correct answer:

worked out
P(disease)            = 0.001
P(positive | disease) = 0.99
P(positive | healthy) = 0.05          # 1 − specificity

P(positive) = 0.99 × 0.001  +  0.05 × 0.999
            = 0.00099       +  0.04995
            = 0.05094

P(disease | positive) = 0.00099 / 0.05094 = 0.0194   # about 1.9%

Under 2%. The test is good; the disease is rare. Because there are a thousand times more healthy people, the 5% of them that test positive falsely swamp the 99% of the tiny sick group that test positive correctly. This is the base rate fallacy, and it is not a curiosity — it is the daily reality of fraud detection, rare-disease screening, security alerting and any other problem with an extreme class imbalance.

The ML translation

Swap "disease" for "fraudulent transaction" and "test" for "your classifier". Sensitivity is recall; \(P(\text{disease} \mid \text{positive})\) is precision. The lesson transfers exactly: on a rare positive class, even a small false-positive rate produces an alert queue that is mostly noise. Chapter 15 turns this into the precision–recall trade-off, and chapter 8 covers what to do about imbalance.

Random variables and distributions

A random variable maps outcomes to numbers — the number of clicks, the height of a person, whether an email is spam encoded as 0 or 1. Two kinds:

Discrete
Countable values. Described by a probability mass function \(P(X = x)\), which gives the probability of each value. Masses sum to 1.
Continuous
Values on a continuum. Described by a probability density function \(f(x)\). Density is not probability — \(P(X = 3.7) = 0\) exactly. Only intervals have probability, found by integrating: \(P(a < X < b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\). The total area under the curve is 1, and the density itself can exceed 1.

The cumulative distribution function \(F(x) = P(X \le x)\) works for both and is what percentiles are built on.

Expectation and variance

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\,P(x) \quad\text{or}\quad \int x f(x)\,dx, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\big]$$

Expectation is the long-run average; variance is the average squared distance from it. The standard deviation \(\sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}}\) is preferred for reporting because it carries the same units as the data.

Two properties do most of the work in derivations:

$$\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y] \quad \text{(always)}, \qquad \text{Var}(aX) = a^2\text{Var}(X)$$

Linearity of expectation holds even when X and Y are dependent, which is surprising and extremely convenient. Variance does not add unless the variables are uncorrelated.

A computational identity worth remembering, since it is how variance is calculated in one pass:

$$\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$$

Covariance and correlation

$$\text{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big], \qquad \rho = \frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} \in [-1,1]$$

Covariance has awkward units; correlation normalises it into a comparable number. The covariance matrix of your features — the object PCA decomposes in chapter 9 — has variances on the diagonal and covariances off it.

Correlation only measures straight lines

A correlation of 0 means no linear relationship, not no relationship. Points on a perfect parabola \(y = x^2\) centred at zero have correlation exactly 0 while being perfectly determined. Always plot before trusting a correlation coefficient — Anscombe's quartet and the Datasaurus dataset exist precisely to make this point.

Why the normal distribution is everywhere

Not because nature is fond of it, but because of the central limit theorem below: anything that is the sum of many independent small contributions ends up approximately normal regardless of what those contributions look like individually. Heights, measurement errors and aggregated noise qualify. Incomes and city sizes do not — they are products rather than sums, which is why their logarithms are normal and their raw values have a long right tail.

The central limit theorem

The result that makes statistics possible: averages become normal even when the thing being averaged is not.

$$\bar{X}_n \;\xrightarrow{\;n \to \infty\;}\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$$

The sample mean of n independent draws is approximately normal, centred on the true mean, with variance shrinking as 1/n — so the standard error shrinks as 1/√n.

Two consequences you will use constantly:

  • Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests work even when you know nothing about the underlying distribution, provided the sample is large enough. Chapter 5 builds on this.
  • Precision improves with the square root of effort. Quadrupling your sample size halves the error bars. This is why A/B tests need so many users, and why the last decimal place of an accuracy estimate is expensive.

The conditions people forget

The CLT needs independent observations and a finite variance. Correlated data — repeated measurements on the same user, time series, clustered samples — breaks the independence requirement, and the resulting confidence intervals are far too narrow. This is the technical reason A/B tests should randomise by user rather than by page view.

Where loss functions come from

This section is the payoff. Squared error and cross-entropy look like arbitrary engineering choices. They are not: each is what you get when you assume a distribution for your data and ask which parameters make the observations most likely.

Maximum likelihood

Given data \(\mathcal{D}\) and a model with parameters \(\theta\), the likelihood is the probability of the data under those parameters. Choose the parameters that maximise it:

$$\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_{\theta} \; P(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) = \arg\max_{\theta} \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(y^{(i)} \mid \mathbf{x}^{(i)}, \theta)$$

Products of many small numbers underflow to zero, and products are awkward to differentiate. Taking the logarithm fixes both — it turns products into sums and is monotonic, so it does not move the maximum. Flipping the sign turns maximisation into minimisation, which is the convention optimisers expect:

$$\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\min_{\theta} \; \underbrace{-\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log P(y^{(i)} \mid \mathbf{x}^{(i)}, \theta)}_{\textstyle \text{negative log-likelihood}}$$

Assume Gaussian noise, get squared error

Suppose the target is the model's output plus normal noise: \(y = f(\mathbf{x};\theta) + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\). Then

$$P(y \mid \mathbf{x},\theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(y - f(\mathbf{x};\theta))^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

Take the negative log. The constant out front and the \(2\sigma^2\) do not depend on \(\theta\), so they drop out of the arg min, leaving:

$$-\log P \;\propto\; \big(y - f(\mathbf{x};\theta)\big)^2$$

Mean squared error is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise. Which also tells you when MSE is the wrong choice: if your errors are heavy-tailed, the Gaussian assumption is false, outliers get squared into dominance, and something like Huber loss or mean absolute error fits the actual noise better.

Assume Bernoulli outcomes, get cross-entropy

For binary labels, model \(P(y=1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = p\). One observation has probability \(p^{y}(1-p)^{1-y}\) — which equals p when y = 1 and 1−p when y = 0. Negative log:

$$-\log P = -\big[\,y\log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)\,\big]$$

That is binary cross-entropy, arrived at without anyone choosing it. The multiclass version follows the same way from the categorical distribution.

Maximum a posteriori, and why regularisation is a prior

MLE asks which parameters make the data likeliest. MAP asks which parameters are likeliest given the data, which by Bayes' rule brings in a prior:

$$\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\theta} P(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) = \arg\max_{\theta} \;\underbrace{P(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)}_{\text{likelihood}}\;\underbrace{P(\theta)}_{\text{prior}}$$

Take the negative log again and the product becomes a sum: the usual loss, plus a term coming from the prior. Now put a zero-mean Gaussian prior on the weights, \(w_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)\). Its negative log is proportional to \(\sum_j w_j^2\), so:

$$\underbrace{-\log P(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)}_{\text{your loss}} \;+\; \underbrace{\lambda\sum_j w_j^2}_{\text{L2 penalty}}$$

Ridge regression is MAP estimation with a Gaussian prior on the weights. Swap in a Laplace prior and the penalty becomes \(\sum_j |w_j|\) — that is lasso. Regularisation is not a hack bolted on to prevent overfitting; it is the formal statement "I believe the weights are probably small", and λ controls how strongly you believe it.

Worth pausing on

Three things you might have learned as separate tricks — squared error, cross-entropy, and weight regularisation — turn out to be one idea seen from different angles. When you meet a new loss function, the useful question is no longer "why this formula" but "what distribution is this assuming, and is that assumption true for my data?"

Information theory

Cross-entropy has a second origin story, from information theory, and it explains the name.

Entropy

$$H(p) = -\sum_{x} p(x)\log p(x)$$

Entropy is the average surprise of a distribution, measured in bits when the log is base 2. A fair coin has 1 bit of entropy; a coin that always lands heads has 0 — no surprise, nothing to learn. Maximum entropy means maximum uncertainty, which for a discrete variable means the uniform distribution.

Decision trees use exactly this: a split is good if it reduces the entropy of the class labels in the resulting groups. That reduction is called information gain, and chapter 12 uses it to grow trees.

Cross-entropy and KL divergence

$$H(p,q) = -\sum_x p(x)\log q(x), \qquad D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) = \sum_x p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$$

Cross-entropy is the average surprise when reality is p but you are predicting with q. KL divergence is the excess surprise caused by being wrong. They are related by an identity worth internalising:

$$H(p,q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q)$$

Since \(H(p)\) is a fixed property of the data and does not depend on your model, minimising cross-entropy is exactly minimising KL divergence — you are making your predicted distribution as close as possible to the true one. Cross-entropy hits its floor, \(H(p)\), when \(q = p\); KL is zero then and positive otherwise.

Entropy, cross-entropy and KL
Move the predicted bars toward the true ones

KL divergence is not symmetric: \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) \neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)\), so it is not a distance. The asymmetry has real consequences in generative modelling — one direction punishes assigning zero probability to things that happen, the other punishes spreading probability where nothing happens, and the choice shapes whether your model produces blurry averages or sharp but incomplete samples. Chapter 20 revisits this for VAEs and GANs.

Mutual information

$$I(X;Y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(P(X,Y) \,\|\, P(X)P(Y)\big)$$

How much knowing one variable tells you about another — zero exactly when they are independent. Unlike correlation it detects nonlinear relationships, which makes it a useful feature-selection filter. Chapter 9 uses it.

from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)          # always seed; reproducibility is not optional

# ---- sampling ----
rng.normal(loc=0, scale=1, size=1000)

rng.choice(["a", "b", "c"], size=10, p=[0.5, 0.3, 0.2])

d.ppf(0.975)        # the 97.5th percentile = 129.4
1 - d.cdf(130)      # P(X > 130), the upper tail

# ---- summary statistics ----
x = rng.normal(size=(1000, 3))
x.mean(axis=0); x.std(axis=0, ddof=1)   # ddof=1 for a sample, not a population
np.cov(x, rowvar=False)                # 3×3 covariance matrix
np.corrcoef(x, rowvar=False)            # correlation matrix

# ---- Bayes, written out ----
def posterior(prior, sens, spec):
    """P(disease | positive test)."""
    tp = sens * prior                     # true positives
    fp = (1 - spec) * (1 - prior)          # false positives
    return tp / (tp + fp)

posterior(0.001, 0.99, 0.95)              # 0.0194

# ---- entropy and cross-entropy, numerically safe ----
def cross_entropy(p, q, eps=1e-12):
    q = np.clip(q, eps, 1)               # never log(0)
    return -np.sum(p * np.log(q))

p = np.array([0.7, 0.2, 0.1])
q = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2])
H  = cross_entropy(p, p)                  # entropy of p          = 0.802
CE = cross_entropy(p, q)                  # cross-entropy         = 0.856
KL = CE - H                               # divergence            = 0.054

# ---- MLE by hand: fit a normal ----
data = rng.normal(loc=5, scale=2, size=500)
mu_hat    = data.mean()                   # the MLE for μ is just the sample mean
sigma_hat = data.std(ddof=0)             # the MLE for σ uses ddof=0 — it is biased low
ddof, and the divide-by-n−1 question

NumPy defaults to ddof=0 (divide by n), pandas defaults to ddof=1 (divide by n−1). They disagree, silently, and the gap matters on small samples. Use n−1 when estimating the variance of a population from a sample — it corrects a downward bias, because the sample mean sits closer to your data than the true mean does. Use n when you literally have the whole population, or when you want the maximum likelihood estimate.

Self-check

  1. 1% of transactions are fraud. Your detector flags 90% of fraud and falsely flags 2% of legitimate transactions. A transaction is flagged — what is the probability it is fraud?
    True positives 0.9 × 0.01 = 0.009. False positives 0.02 × 0.99 = 0.0198. Total flagged 0.0288. So P(fraud | flagged) = 0.009 / 0.0288 ≈ 31%. Roughly two out of three alerts are false. Whether that is acceptable depends on the cost of reviewing an alert versus the cost of missing fraud — which is a business question, not a modelling one.
  2. Why do we minimise the negative log likelihood rather than maximising the likelihood directly?
    Three reasons. Products of thousands of probabilities underflow to zero in floating point, while sums of logs do not. Logs turn products into sums, which differentiate far more easily. And optimisers are written to minimise, so the sign is flipped. The log is monotonic, so none of this changes where the optimum is.
  3. Your regression residuals have several extreme outliers. What does that tell you about your loss function?
    MSE assumes Gaussian noise, which has thin tails — under that assumption large residuals are so improbable that squaring them lets a few points dominate the fit. Heavy tails mean the assumption is wrong. Switch to mean absolute error (a Laplace assumption) or Huber loss, which is quadratic near zero and linear far out. Alternatively investigate whether the outliers are data errors rather than genuine observations.
  4. What is the difference between \(P(A \mid B)\) and \(P(B \mid A)\), in words, with an example where they differ wildly?
    The first fixes B and asks about A; the second does the reverse. P(speaks Welsh | lives in Wales) is perhaps 0.2; P(lives in Wales | speaks Welsh) is close to 0.9. They are linked by Bayes' rule, and the ratio between them is governed by the base rates of the two conditions.
  5. Why does the standard error of a mean shrink as 1/√n rather than 1/n?
    Variance of a sum of n independent variables adds, so Var(ΣX) = nσ². The mean divides by n, and variance scales by the square of a constant, giving Var(X̄) = nσ²/n² = σ²/n. Taking the square root for the standard deviation gives σ/√n. Practically: to halve your error bars you need four times the data.
  6. Cross-entropy loss on your validation set is 0.35. What is the lowest it could possibly go, and why is it not zero?
    The floor is H(p), the entropy of the true conditional distribution of the labels. If identical inputs sometimes carry different labels — irreducible noise, ambiguous cases, mislabelled data — then even a perfect model cannot drive cross-entropy below that. Chapter 10 calls this the Bayes error. A loss stuck well above zero may mean your model is weak, or may simply mean the problem has genuine uncertainty in it.
  7. You add an L2 penalty and someone asks what assumption you have made. What do you say?
    That the weights are drawn from a zero-mean Gaussian prior — you believe, before seeing data, that coefficients are probably small and near zero. λ encodes the strength of that belief: it is inversely proportional to the prior's variance. An L1 penalty corresponds instead to a Laplace prior, which is sharply peaked at zero and produces exact zeros in the solution.
