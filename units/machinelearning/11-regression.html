Regression

Predicting a number. Linear regression is the model everyone learns first and then underrates — it is the only common model with a closed-form solution, the only one whose coefficients mean something directly, and the baseline that surprisingly often survives contact with the alternatives.

— words— min read3 interactivesNeeds: chapters 02–04, 10

The model

$$\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + \dots + w_dx_d \;=\; \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b$$

w are the coefficients (weights), b the intercept. Over a whole dataset this is one matrix product: ŷ = Xw + b.

"Linear" refers to linearity in the parameters, not in the features. \(\hat{y} = w_1x + w_2x^2 + w_3\log x\) is still a linear model — you can throw any transformation of the inputs at it, and the fitting machinery is unchanged. That flexibility is easy to miss and is what makes the linear family far more useful than it first appears.

The loss

Chapter 4 derived it rather than choosing it: assume the target is the model's output plus Gaussian noise, and maximum likelihood gives you squared error.

$$J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y^{(i)} - \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\big)^2$$

Squaring does three things: it makes errors positive, it is smoothly differentiable everywhere (unlike absolute error), and it punishes large errors disproportionately. That last property is a genuine choice — it is why one outlier can drag the whole line, as the demo below shows.

Two ways to solve it

The normal equation

Set the gradient to zero and solve. Writing the cost in matrix form, \(J = \|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{Xw}\|^2\), the gradient is \(-2\mathbf{X}^{\top}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{Xw})\). Setting it to zero:

$$\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{w} = (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}$$

One shot, no iterations, no learning rate. Almost no other model in this unit has that.

The geometry: it is a projection

Chapter 2 hinted at this. The columns of X span a subspace; \(\mathbf{Xw}\) can only ever land inside it. Your target vector \(\mathbf{y}\) almost certainly lies outside. The closest reachable point is the perpendicular projection of \(\mathbf{y}\) onto that subspace — and "perpendicular" is exactly what \(\mathbf{X}^{\top}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{Xw}) = 0\) says: the residual is orthogonal to every feature column. Least squares is not an arbitrary criterion; it is the unique choice that makes the leftover error carry no remaining linear information about any feature.

Do not implement it literally

Forming \(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) squares the condition number of your data, which halves the number of reliable digits, and inverting it is both slower and less stable than solving. Use np.linalg.lstsq or scikit-learn, which go through QR or SVD. Cost is \(O(nd^2)\), so the normal equation is fine to a few thousand features and impractical beyond that — which is where gradient descent takes over.

Gradient descent

The general-purpose alternative from chapter 3, and the only option once d is large or the data does not fit in memory:

$$\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} J = -\frac{2}{n}\mathbf{X}^{\top}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{Xw}), \qquad \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} J$$

Reading the coefficients

This is linear regression's real advantage. Each coefficient is a sentence in English, provided you state it carefully.

The sentence
"A one-unit increase in \(x_j\), holding all other features fixed, is associated with a \(w_j\)-unit change in y."
The two hedges that matter
Holding others fixed is often physically impossible — you cannot increase floor area while holding room count constant. Associated with, not "causes": chapter 5's confounding applies in full force.
FormCoefficient means
\(y = w x\)A one-unit rise in x changes y by w units
\(y = w \log x\)A 1% rise in x changes y by roughly \(w/100\) units
\(\log y = w x\)A one-unit rise in x changes y by roughly \(100w\) per cent
\(\log y = w \log x\)A 1% rise in x changes y by \(w\) per cent — an elasticity
Standardised inputsA one-standard-deviation rise in x changes y by w. The only form in which coefficient magnitudes are comparable across features
When you want p-values and intervals

scikit-learn gives point estimates only. For inference — standard errors, t-statistics, confidence intervals — use statsmodels:

python
import statsmodels.api as sm
model = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(X)).fit()

model.get_robust_cov_results(cov_type="HC3")   # heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors

Which of these you actually need

If you only want predictions, the assumptions matter far less than textbooks suggest — you can evaluate predictive accuracy directly on held-out data, and a model that predicts well while violating normality is still predicting well. If you want to interpret coefficients or quote p-values, the assumptions matter enormously, because the standard errors those quantities depend on are computed under them. Know which mode you are in.

Multicollinearity

When features are strongly correlated, \(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) approaches singular and the coefficient estimates become wild — chapter 2's condition-number problem in its most common practical form. Symptoms: enormous coefficients with opposite signs on similar features, wide confidence intervals, and estimates that swing when a single row is removed. The model may still predict perfectly well; it is the individual coefficients that have become meaningless.

$$\text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1-R_j^2}$$

Regress feature j on all the other features; R²ⱼ is how well they explain it. VIF above 5 warrants a look, above 10 is a problem.

Ridge, lasso and elastic net

Add a penalty on the size of the coefficients. Chapter 4 showed this is MAP estimation with a prior; here is what it does geometrically and practically.

$$\text{Ridge:}\;\; J = \|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{Xw}\|^2 + \lambda\sum_j w_j^2 \qquad\qquad \text{Lasso:}\;\; J = \|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{Xw}\|^2 + \lambda\sum_j |w_j|$$

Ridge has a closed form — \(\mathbf{w} = (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\) — and notice that adding \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) makes the matrix invertible even when \(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) is singular. That is why ridge is numerically better behaved than plain OLS on collinear data, quite apart from its statistical benefits.

Why lasso produces exact zeros

Both penalties can be viewed as constraining the coefficients to lie inside a region of fixed size. The shape of that region is the whole story.

Three things people get wrong
  1. Not scaling first. The penalty acts on coefficient magnitude, and magnitude depends on the feature's units. An unscaled feature measured in thousands gets a tiny coefficient and is barely penalised; the same feature in units gets a large one and is crushed. Always scale before regularising.
  2. Penalising the intercept. Shrinking \(w_0\) toward zero says you believe the target is near zero, which is rarely intended. Libraries exclude it by default; hand-rolled implementations often do not.
  3. Reading lasso's selection as truth. Among a group of correlated features, lasso keeps one and zeroes the rest, and which one it keeps can flip with a small change in the data. It is a prediction tool, not a reliable feature-importance ranking.

R² is easier to misread than it looks
  • Adding any feature — even pure noise — never decreases training R². Use adjusted R², or measure on held-out data.
  • R² on the test set can be negative. That means your model is worse than predicting the mean, which is worth knowing.
  • What counts as "good" is entirely domain-dependent. R² = 0.3 is impressive when predicting individual human behaviour and alarming when predicting a physical process.
  • A high R² says nothing about whether the model is right. Anscombe's quartet from chapter 5 had identical R² across four completely different datasets.

A note on extrapolation

Linear models extrapolate — sometimes absurdly, but they do it, and the direction is at least predictable. Tree-based models cannot: outside the range of the training data, every tree returns the value of its nearest leaf, so predictions flatten into a constant. If your model will meet inputs beyond its training range — forecasting growth, pricing something larger than anything you have sold — that difference is decisive, and it is a common reason to keep a linear component in the mix.

Self-check

  1. Why does adding \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) to \(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) fix numerical problems as well as statistical ones?
    It shifts every eigenvalue up by λ. If the matrix was singular or nearly so — because two features are collinear — the smallest eigenvalues were at or near zero, making the condition number enormous. Adding λ pushes them away from zero, so the matrix becomes invertible and well-conditioned. The statistical effect (shrinking coefficients toward zero) and the numerical effect (stabilising the solve) are the same operation seen from two angles.
  2. Your coefficient on bedrooms is negative in a house-price model. What is the likely explanation?
    Almost certainly collinearity with floor area. The coefficient means "holding area fixed", and for a fixed area, more bedrooms means smaller rooms — which genuinely can reduce value. The coefficient is not wrong; the naive reading of it is. Check the VIF between the two features, and consider modelling area and bedrooms_per_100sqm instead so each coefficient answers a question someone would actually ask.
  3. Ridge and lasso give very different feature sets on your data. Which do you trust?
    Neither, as a statement about which features matter. Lasso arbitrarily picks one member of each correlated group and zeroes the others; ridge spreads weight across the group. If your goal is prediction, choose by cross-validated error. If your goal is understanding which features matter, use elastic net for stability, run it on bootstrap resamples to see which selections are consistent, and corroborate with permutation importance or SHAP from chapter 22.
  4. Test R² comes out at −0.15. What does that mean?
    Your model performs worse on the test set than simply predicting the training mean for every row. Common causes: severe overfitting; a distribution shift between train and test; or a bug such as unscaled test features or a target transformation applied to one set and not the other. R² can go negative out of sample because the residual sum of squares is no longer bounded by the total sum of squares once the model was fitted elsewhere.
  5. You have a heavily right-skewed target with occasional huge values. What do you change?
    Consider modelling log1p(y) — the squared-error assumption of Gaussian noise fits far better on the log scale, and it usually resolves heteroscedasticity at the same time. Remember to invert the transform when reporting, and note that exp(mean of logs) gives a median-like quantity rather than a mean, which matters if someone is summing your predictions. The alternatives are a gamma GLM, which handles this natively, or MAE/Huber if you would rather keep the original scale.
  6. Why can a tree ensemble not extrapolate, and when does that matter?
    A tree partitions the feature space into regions seen in training and predicts the average target within each. A new input beyond the training range falls into whichever boundary region is nearest, and receives that region's constant. So predictions saturate rather than continuing a trend. It matters whenever the deployment distribution extends past the training range: forecasting into a growth period, pricing unusually large orders, or any feature that drifts upward over time.
