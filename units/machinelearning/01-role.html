What machine learning is

Ordinary programming is a machine for turning rules into answers. You write the rules, the computer applies them to data, answers come out. Machine learning inverts the arrows: you supply data and the answers you want, and the computer produces the rules.

flowchart LR subgraph P["Traditional programming"] direction LR R1["Rules you wrote"] --> E1["Program"] D1["Data"] --> E1 E1 --> A1["Answers"] end subgraph M["Machine learning"] direction LR D2["Data"] --> E2["Learning algorithm"] A2["Answers (labels)"] --> E2 E2 --> R2["Rules (a model)"] end

That inversion matters because of a class of problems where nobody can write the rules down. You can describe in words how to tell a cat from a dog, but you cannot write the if statements. You can say a transaction "looks fraudulent", but the pattern shifts every quarter as fraudsters adapt. You can recognise a badly written support ticket instantly and still fail to specify the criteria. Machine learning is the engineering answer to problems where the rules exist but are not writable by hand — either because they are too intricate, too numerous, or because they change faster than you can maintain them.

A definition worth memorising

Tom Mitchell's 1997 formulation is still the cleanest, and it is genuinely useful because it forces you to name three things before you start:

Definition

A program learns from experience E with respect to task T and performance measure P, if its performance at T, as measured by P, improves with E.

Applied to a spam filter: T is classifying an email as spam or not; E is the archive of emails users have already marked; P might be the fraction of legitimate mail wrongly binned. If you cannot fill in all three slots for your project, you do not yet have a machine learning problem — you have an ambition. Most failed ML projects fail at P: nobody agreed in advance what "working" would mean, so the model ships and the argument starts.

What "learning" really consists of

Strip away the vocabulary and every supervised learning algorithm in this unit does the same four things:

  1. Choose a family of possible rules. Straight lines. Decision trees up to depth 6. Neural networks with this exact wiring. This family is the hypothesis space, and choosing it is your most consequential decision — the algorithm can only ever return something from inside it.
  2. Define what "wrong" costs. A loss function turns a prediction and a truth into a single number, larger when the prediction is worse. Squared error for continuous outputs, cross-entropy for probabilities.
  3. Search the family for the rule with the lowest total loss on your data. Sometimes by solving an equation exactly, usually by taking small downhill steps.
  4. Check the winner on data it never saw. Without this, you have memorisation, not learning. Chapter 15 is devoted entirely to doing this honestly.

Every algorithm you meet — logistic regression, gradient boosting, a 70-billion-parameter transformer — is a different answer to steps 1 to 3. Step 4 never changes.

When not to use it

Machine learning trades certainty for coverage. A hand-written rule is auditable, testable and correct by construction; a model is statistical and will be wrong sometimes, in ways you cannot always predict. If the rules are writable, write them. A regex that catches 95% of malformed postcodes beats a classifier that catches 96% and cannot explain itself, costs a pipeline to retrain, and degrades silently when the input format changes.

What the job is

The title "machine learning engineer" covers a spread of jobs. At a nine-person startup it means you own the whole pipeline from scraping to serving. At a large bank it might mean you spend six months making one model auditable enough for a regulator. What follows is the common core.

The two bars that dominate — understanding and preparing data — are not a failure of tooling that better libraries will one day fix. They are the job. A model is a function of its training data; if that data encodes a subtly wrong view of the world, a better optimiser only helps you reach the wrong answer faster. This is why chapters 7, 8 and 9 are as long as the modelling chapters.

Responsibilities, concretely

Frame the problem
Turn "we want to reduce churn" into a supervised learning task with a defined label, a prediction window, a population, and a metric a non-technical stakeholder has signed off on.
Get and shape the data
Write the SQL, hit the APIs, join the sources, and discover the three reasons the join silently drops 8% of rows.
Build features
Encode domain knowledge into columns a model can use. Frequently worth more than any algorithm change.
Train and select
Establish a baseline, try a small number of well-chosen models, tune the ones that show promise, and stop when the returns flatten.
Evaluate against reality
Cross-validation, error analysis on the cases that matter, fairness checks across subgroups, and a straight answer about whether this beats the current process.
Ship it
Package the model with its preprocessing, expose it behind an interface, meet the latency budget, and make it reproducible.
Watch it
Monitor inputs and outputs for drift, decide the retraining trigger, and be the person who notices the model has quietly become useless.
Explain it
To a product manager, a customer, and possibly a regulator — three different explanations of the same model.

The skill stack

Programming

Python fluency, not scripting-level. Data structures, testing, packaging, version control, enough software engineering that your work survives contact with a codebase. Chapter 6.

Maths

Linear algebra and calculus to read papers and debug training; probability and statistics to avoid fooling yourself. Chapters 2 – 5.

Data handling

SQL that is not embarrassing, pandas at speed, an understanding of file formats and of how data arrives late, duplicated and mistyped. Chapters 6 – 8.

Modelling

Knowing perhaps a dozen algorithms deeply enough to predict how each will fail on your data. Chapters 10 – 20.

Evaluation

The single highest-leverage skill. Most bad ML is bad because it was measured wrong. Chapter 15.

Engineering

Containers, CI, cloud basics, latency budgets, cost awareness. Enough to hand over cleanly. Chapter 23.

Communication

Explaining uncertainty to people who want a yes. Writing the document that says why the project should stop.

Domain sense

Knowing that a blood pressure of 0 means "not recorded", not "dead". Borrowed from colleagues if you lack it — but borrow it deliberately.

The practical read

The ML engineer sits at the intersection of data scientist and software engineer, and the split shifts with company size. Small company: you are all of the above and the on-call rota. Large company: pick the column you want and expect to hand off at the boundaries. If you are choosing a direction, the boundary that has moved most in the last few years is the AI engineer column — it barely existed before hosted foundation models made it possible to build serious products without training anything.

Step 1 deserves its own section

Problem framing is where projects are won and lost, and it is almost never taught. Four decisions get made here, and all four are hard to reverse later:

The label

What exactly are you predicting? "Churn" is not a label. "The customer has no active subscription 30 days after their renewal date, having had one on that date" is a label. Every ambiguity you leave here becomes noise in the target, and noise in the target caps your achievable accuracy no matter how good your model is.

The prediction window

Predicting churn tomorrow is easy and useless — you cannot intervene in a day. Predicting it in 90 days is useful and much harder. The window must match the length of the action you intend to take, and it determines what data you are allowed to use: only features available at prediction time, never anything from inside the window itself. Violating this is the most common form of data leakage, covered in chapter 8.

The population

Who is scored? All customers, or only those past their trial? Get this wrong and your training distribution differs from your serving distribution, which is the silent killer of otherwise sound models.

The metric, and the baseline it must beat

Two numbers, agreed in writing, before modelling starts. The metric is the technical one (chapter 15 will help you choose). The baseline is what happens if you do nothing clever: the current rules-based system, the majority class, last week's value, or a human. A model with 92% accuracy is meaningless until you know the majority class is 91%.

The most expensive mistake in the field

Building a technically excellent model for a problem the business cannot act on. If nobody has decided what happens when the model says "0.87", the model has no value. Ask "what will we do differently?" in the first meeting, and if there is no clean answer, that is the project's real problem — fix it before touching a notebook.

Notation used throughout this unit

Lowercase italic for scalars, bold lowercase for vectors, bold uppercase for matrices. Concretely:

  • n — number of samples; d — number of features
  • x⁽ⁱ⁾ — the feature vector of sample i; xj — the j-th feature
  • X — the full design matrix, shape n × d; y — the target vector, length n
  • ŷ — a prediction (the hat always means "estimated")
  • θ or w — parameters; b — a bias/intercept term
  • L — loss; J — the cost averaged over a dataset; η — learning rate

How to use this unit

Twenty-four chapters is a lot. A few notes on getting through them without the common failure mode, which is reading everything, understanding it in the moment, and retaining none of it.

Read with something running

Every code block is runnable and self-contained where it can be. Keep a notebook open with numpy, pandas, matplotlib and scikit-learn installed, and paste as you go. Reading about broadcasting teaches nothing; getting a shape error and fixing it teaches permanently.

Break the demos on purpose

Each interactive demo is wired to the real algorithm, not a canned animation. Push every slider to its extreme. When gradient descent diverges in chapter 3 because you set the learning rate to 1.9, you will never again wonder why your loss went to NaN.

Use the self-checks properly

At the end of each chapter there are questions with hidden answers. Attempt an answer out loud or on paper before revealing. The struggle is what forms the memory; recognising a correct answer feels like knowing and is not.

Self-check

  1. A colleague says their fraud model is 99.4% accurate. What is the first question you ask?
    What fraction of transactions are fraudulent? If it is 0.6%, a model that predicts "never fraud" scores 99.4% and catches nothing. Always demand the baseline, then a metric that is not accuracy — precision, recall, or better still the cost of each error type. Chapter 15 covers the right choices.
  2. Fill in Mitchell's T, E and P for "recommend the next video to watch".
    T: rank candidate videos for a given user and context. E: historical logs of impressions, clicks and watch time. P: something like watch time in the next session, or click-through on the top-3 — and note that choosing P here has real consequences, since optimising raw watch time is exactly how recommenders end up promoting content nobody is glad to have watched.
  3. Why is "predict churn in the next 24 hours" usually a bad framing even though it will score well?
    Because the prediction window is shorter than the time needed to act on it. A customer about to cancel tomorrow has already decided; there is no intervention left. It also invites leakage — features from the last day (cancelled the auto-renew, downloaded their data) essentially contain the answer, so the model looks brilliant offline and is worthless in production.
  4. You have a hyperparameter and a parameter. Which one does the training loop change, and which do you tune with a validation set?
    The training loop changes parameters (weights) to reduce loss on the training set. Hyperparameters are fixed during a training run and are chosen by comparing runs on the validation set. Confusing the two is how test sets get contaminated.
  5. Name two situations where you should recommend against machine learning.
    Any of: the rules are known and stable (write them); you have almost no labelled data and no way to get it; each error is catastrophic and unrecoverable; the decision must be fully explainable by law and the accuracy gain over a transparent model is small; the base rate is so low that even excellent precision produces mostly false positives; or nobody can say what action follows a prediction.
