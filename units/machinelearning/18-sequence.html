Chapter 18 · Deep learning

Sequence models

Text, audio, sensor streams and time series share two properties a dense network cannot express: order matters, and length varies. Recurrence solved both, ran into a gradient problem that took a decade to fix properly, and was then largely replaced by attention — but the ideas along the way are what make chapter 19 comprehensible.

The recurrent network

Process the sequence one element at a time, carrying a hidden state that summarises everything seen so far:

$$\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh\big(\mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}\big), \qquad \mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{h}_t$$

The same weight matrices are used at every timestep. That sharing is what makes the model work on sequences of any length, and it is the direct analogue of a CNN sharing kernels across positions.

Backpropagation through time, and why it fails

Unrolled, an RNN is a very deep feedforward network — one layer per timestep — with the crucial difference that every layer shares the same weights. Backpropagation works the same way, but the gradient reaching step \(t-k\) has passed through the recurrent matrix k times:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t-k}} = \prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}\,\text{diag}\big(\phi'(\cdot)\big)$$

A product of k similar factors either shrinks toward zero or grows without bound. The tipping point is roughly whether the largest eigenvalue of \(\mathbf{W}_{hh}\) is below or above 1 — chapter 2's eigenvalues, deciding whether a network can learn.

The asymmetry

Exploding gradients are loud and easy to fix: clip_grad_norm_(params, 1.0) caps the update and training continues. Vanishing gradients are silent — the model trains, the loss falls, and it simply never learns any dependency longer than a few steps. You discover it by noticing the model gets the local grammar right and the long-range agreement wrong. That silence is why the problem took so long to be properly addressed.

LSTM

The fix, from 1997: add a separate memory channel that information can travel along without being multiplied by a weight matrix at every step, and put learned gates in charge of what enters, leaves and is read from it.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{f}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}_f[\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_f\big) && \text{forget gate — what to discard} \\ \mathbf{i}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}_i[\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_i\big) && \text{input gate — what to admit} \\ \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t &= \tanh\big(\mathbf{W}_c[\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_c\big) && \text{candidate values} \\ \mathbf{c}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t && \text{the cell state update} \\ \mathbf{o}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}_o[\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_o\big) && \text{output gate — what to expose} \\ \mathbf{h}_t &= \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}_t) && \text{the visible hidden state} \end{aligned}$$

The fourth line is the important one. The cell state is updated by addition, and the only thing multiplying the old value is \(\mathbf{f}_t\), a number between 0 and 1 that the network learns. When the forget gate sits near 1, information flows forward — and gradients flow backward — almost unchanged. This is the "constant error carousel", and it is why LSTMs can learn dependencies hundreds of steps long.

GRU: the same idea with fewer parts

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{z}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}_z[\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_t]\big) && \text{update gate} \\ \mathbf{r}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}_r[\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_t]\big) && \text{reset gate} \\ \mathbf{h}_t &= (1-\mathbf{z}_t)\odot\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{z}_t\odot\tanh\big(\mathbf{W}[\mathbf{r}_t\odot\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_t]\big) \end{aligned}$$

No separate cell state, and one gate does the job of the LSTM's forget and input pair — what you keep and what you add are forced to sum to 1. Around 25% fewer parameters and correspondingly faster. Empirically the two perform very similarly; GRU is often the better default on smaller datasets, LSTM occasionally wins on the longest dependencies.

Sequence to sequence, and the bottleneck

Translation needs an output whose length and alignment differ from the input. The encoder–decoder answer: one RNN reads the whole input into a fixed-size vector, and a second RNN generates the output from it.

one fixed-size summary"] subgraph DEC["Decoder"] D1["the"] --> D2["cat"] --> D3["sleeps"] end C --> D1

The problem is stated in the caption. A single vector of, say, 512 numbers must hold an entire paragraph, and performance falls off sharply as input length grows. Empirically, translation quality for a 50-word sentence was far worse than for a 10-word one, and it was not a capacity problem you could solve by widening the vector.

Attention

The 2014 fix: rather than compressing everything into one vector, keep all the encoder's hidden states and let the decoder, at each output step, compute a weighted average over them — with the weights decided by relevance.

$$e_{tj} = \text{score}(\mathbf{s}_{t-1}, \mathbf{h}_j), \qquad \alpha_{tj} = \frac{\exp(e_{tj})}{\sum_k \exp(e_{tk})}, \qquad \mathbf{c}_t = \sum_j \alpha_{tj}\mathbf{h}_j$$

A softmax over relevance scores produces weights summing to 1; the context vector is that weighted combination. When generating the word "cat", the weights concentrate on "chat".

Three things followed. Translation quality stopped degrading with length. The attention weights turned out to be inspectable — plot them as a matrix and you can see a rough word alignment. And, most consequentially, someone asked whether the recurrence was needed at all if attention was doing the work. That question is chapter 19.

Working with sequences in practice

Padding and masking
Batching requires equal lengths, so short sequences get padded. The padding must then be masked everywhere — in the loss, in attention, in pooling — or the model learns from meaningless zeros. Forgetting the mask is the most common sequence-model bug.
Packed sequences
PyTorch's pack_padded_sequence lets the RNN skip padded steps entirely, which is both faster and correct.
Teacher forcing
During training, feed the decoder the true previous token rather than its own prediction. Much faster convergence, but it creates exposure bias: at inference the model must consume its own output, which it never practised, so one error can cascade.
Truncated BPTT
Backpropagate only k steps rather than the whole sequence. Bounds memory and compute, at the cost of not learning dependencies longer than k.
Gradient clipping
Not optional for recurrent models. clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0).
python — PyTorch, with masking done properly
import torch, torch.nn as nn
from torch.nn.utils.rnn import pad_sequence, pack_padded_sequence, pad_packed_sequence

class SeqClassifier(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vocab, emb=128, hid=256, n_classes=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.emb  = nn.Embedding(vocab, emb, padding_idx=0)   # index 0 stays a zero vector
        self.rnn  = nn.LSTM(emb, hid, num_layers=2, batch_first=True,
                            bidirectional=True, dropout=0.2)
        self.head = nn.Linear(hid * 2, n_classes)             # ×2 for the two directions

    def forward(self, x, lengths):
        e = self.emb(x)
        packed = pack_padded_sequence(e, lengths.cpu(),          # skips padded steps entirely
                                      batch_first=True, enforce_sorted=False)
        out, (h, c) = self.rnn(packed)
        out, _ = pad_packed_sequence(out, batch_first=True)

        # masked mean pooling — never average over the padding
        mask = (x != 0).unsqueeze(-1).float()
        pooled = (out * mask).sum(1) / mask.sum(1).clamp(min=1)
        return self.head(pooled)

Time series: the lag-feature baseline

Before building a recurrent forecaster, try gradient boosting on engineered lag features — the value 1, 7 and 28 days ago, rolling means over several windows, day-of-week, holiday flags. It trains in seconds, handles multiple related series naturally, and wins a surprising share of forecasting competitions. Chapter 9's aggregation features are the whole technique, and the backward-looking window rule matters more here than anywhere else.

Self-check

  1. Why do vanishing gradients affect long dependencies but not short ones?
    The gradient reaching a step k positions back has been multiplied by roughly k similar factors. With factors below 1, the product decays geometrically — at 0.9 per step, after 50 steps you are at 0.005, and after 100 steps effectively zero. Nearby steps involve only a few multiplications, so their gradients survive. The model therefore learns local structure fine and is simply blind to anything distant.
  2. Which line of the LSTM equations does the real work, and why?
    \(\mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t\). The cell state is updated additively, and the only factor multiplying the previous value is the forget gate, a learned number in (0,1). With f near 1 both the value and its gradient pass through almost unchanged, so information can survive hundreds of steps. Compare the plain RNN, where every step multiplies by a full weight matrix and a tanh derivative.
  3. Your loss suddenly spikes to NaN at epoch 4 of an LSTM. What is the fix?
    Almost certainly exploding gradients — add clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0), which is standard practice for recurrent models rather than a special measure. If it persists, lower the learning rate and check for sequences far longer than the rest of the batch, which produce disproportionately large gradients.
  4. Why can you not use a bidirectional RNN for real-time generation?
    The backward pass requires the entire sequence, including elements that have not happened yet. For classification of a complete document that is fine and usually helps considerably. For streaming or generation it is impossible, and using one accidentally on a forecasting task is a form of temporal leakage — the model can see the future it is supposed to predict.
  5. What problem did attention solve in sequence-to-sequence models?
    The fixed-size context bottleneck. The encoder had to compress an entire input sequence into one vector, and quality degraded sharply with length. Attention keeps all the encoder states and lets the decoder compute a fresh weighted average at each output step, so the relevant source position is always directly available. As a side effect, the weights are inspectable and roughly correspond to alignment.
  6. You are forecasting daily demand for 500 products. Where would you start?
    Gradient boosting on lag features, not a recurrent network. Build lags at 1, 7 and 28 days, rolling means over several windows, day-of-week and holiday indicators, plus product and category identifiers so one model serves all 500 series. It trains in seconds, extrapolates poorly but forecasts well within range, and gives you a strong baseline. Reach for a sequence model only once that baseline is beaten by something with a clear reason to be better.
