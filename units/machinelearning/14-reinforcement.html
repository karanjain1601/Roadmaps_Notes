Reinforcement learning

No dataset, no labels. An agent acts, the world responds with a number, and the agent must work out which of its past actions deserve the credit. Everything difficult about RL follows from that last clause.

— words— min read2 interactivesNeeds: chapters 04, 10

What makes it different

Supervised learningReinforcement learning
SignalThe correct answer, for every exampleA scalar reward, sometimes long after the action
DataFixed, given in advanceGenerated by the agent's own behaviour
IndependenceExamples assumed independentActions change what you see next — deeply dependent
FeedbackImmediate and completeDelayed and partial: you learn the value of what you did, never of what you did not
ObjectiveMinimise error on a fixed distributionMaximise cumulative reward over time

Three consequences make RL genuinely hard. Credit assignment: you win a chess game after forty moves — which of them mattered? Exploration: the only way to learn whether an untried action is good is to try it, and trying it costs something. Non-stationarity: as the policy improves, the distribution of states it visits changes, so the learning target moves underneath you.

Markov decision processes

The formal setting: a tuple \((\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)\).

States \(\mathcal{S}\)
What the agent can observe. The Markov property assumes the current state contains everything relevant — the past adds nothing given the present. Real problems violate this constantly, which is why observations often bundle several recent frames together.
Actions \(\mathcal{A}\)
What the agent can do. Discrete (move left) or continuous (apply 0.3 N of torque), and that distinction determines which algorithms are available.
Transitions \(P(s' \mid s,a)\)
How the world responds. Usually unknown — which is what "model-free" means.
Reward \(R(s,a)\)
The scalar signal. Designing it is the hardest and least discussed part of applied RL.
Discount \(\gamma \in [0,1)\)
How much future reward is worth now. γ = 0 is myopic; γ = 0.99 plans far ahead. It also keeps the sum finite in continuing tasks.

The agent's goal is to maximise the return — the discounted sum of all future rewards:

$$G_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma r_{t+2} + \gamma^2 r_{t+3} + \dots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}r_{t+k+1}$$

Policies and value functions

$$V^{\pi}(s) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[G_t \mid s_t = s], \qquad Q^{\pi}(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[G_t \mid s_t = s,\, a_t = a]$$

V is how good a state is; Q is how good taking a specific action in that state is. Q is the more useful of the two, because once you have it, acting well is trivial: take the action with the highest Q.

The Bellman equation

Value functions satisfy a recursive consistency condition — the value of now equals the immediate reward plus the discounted value of next:

$$Q^{*}(s,a) = \mathbb{E}\Big[r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q^{*}(s',a')\Big]$$

That single equation is the engine of value-based RL. Turn it into an update rule that nudges your current estimate toward the right-hand side, and you have Q-learning.

Exploration and exploitation

The defining dilemma. Exploit what you know and you may never discover something better; explore too much and you waste your budget on options you already know are poor.

The cleanest place to see the difference is the multi-armed bandit: several slot machines with unknown payout rates, one pull at a time, no states to worry about. It is RL with the sequential part removed, and it is a real production problem in its own right — ad selection, layout tests, treatment allocation.

Q-learning

Maintain a table of Q(s, a) estimates. After every transition, nudge the entry you just used toward the Bellman target:

$$Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\Big[\underbrace{r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')}_{\text{target}} - \underbrace{Q(s,a)}_{\text{current estimate}}\Big]$$

α is the learning rate. The bracketed quantity is the temporal-difference error — how surprised you were. Update toward the surprise.

Two properties make this remarkable. It is model-free: no knowledge of transition probabilities is needed, only experience. And it is off-policy: the max means it learns the value of the optimal policy while behaving according to some other, more exploratory one. That is what lets an ε-greedy agent, which sometimes acts randomly, still converge on the greedy optimum.

SARSA, and the difference on-policy makes

$$\text{SARSA:}\quad Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\big[r + \gamma Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\big]$$

One symbol changes: \(\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\) becomes \(Q(s',a')\), the value of the action actually taken next. SARSA therefore learns the value of the policy it is following, exploration and all.

The practical consequence is worth knowing. On a cliff-edge problem, Q-learning learns the optimal path — right along the edge — and then falls off periodically because ε-greedy exploration occasionally steps sideways. SARSA learns a longer, safer path away from the edge, because its value estimates account for the fact that it sometimes acts randomly. Q-learning optimises for the policy you intend to deploy; SARSA optimises for the policy you are actually running. If exploration continues in production, SARSA's caution is the correct behaviour.

When the table will not fit

A Q-table needs one entry per state-action pair. Chess has more states than atoms in the observable universe; a camera image has effectively infinite states. The fix is to replace the table with a function approximator — a neural network.

Deep Q-networks

Feed the state into a network that outputs one Q-value per action, and train it to minimise the squared temporal-difference error. Done naively, this diverges. Two additions from the 2015 Atari work made it stable, and both are worth understanding because the same problems recur:

Experience replay
Store transitions in a buffer and train on random batches from it. Consecutive frames are highly correlated, which violates the independence that gradient descent expects; sampling randomly from a large buffer breaks that correlation and lets each experience be reused many times.
Target network
Compute the update target using a frozen copy of the network, refreshed every few thousand steps. Without it you are chasing a target that moves every time you update — a feedback loop that reliably diverges.

Later refinements: Double DQN (decouple action selection from evaluation to cut the systematic overestimation the max introduces), duelling architectures (separate state value from action advantage), and prioritised replay (sample surprising transitions more often).

Policy gradient methods

Rather than learning values and deriving a policy, parameterise the policy directly and push its parameters up the reward gradient:

$$\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)\,G_t\big]$$

In words: increase the log-probability of actions that led to high return, decrease it for actions that led to low return, weighted by how good the outcome was. This is REINFORCE.

It works with continuous action spaces, which value-based methods handle awkwardly, and it can learn stochastic policies — necessary whenever the optimal behaviour is genuinely random, as in games with bluffing. Its weakness is variance: the return of a whole episode is a very noisy estimate of one action's quality.

Actor-critic

Combine the two. An actor chooses actions; a critic estimates values and supplies a lower-variance learning signal, replacing the raw return with an advantage — how much better this action was than average from that state. A2C, DDPG, SAC and PPO are all variations on this.

PPO is the practical default. Its contribution is a clipped objective that prevents any single update from changing the policy too much, which turns out to be most of what stands between policy gradients and stability. It is also the algorithm used in the reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback stage of language model training, where the reward comes from a model trained on human preference comparisons.

Reward design

The reward function is the specification of what you want. Agents optimise exactly what you wrote, which is rarely exactly what you meant.

Reward hacking

Documented cases, all real:

  • A boat-racing agent rewarded for score found a lagoon where it could loop forever collecting respawning pickups, never finishing the race.
  • A simulated robot rewarded for forward velocity learned to grow very tall and fall over.
  • A cleaning agent penalised for visible mess learned to cover the mess rather than remove it.
  • Recommenders optimised for engagement reliably learn that outrage is engaging.
Sparse rewards
+1 only on success is honest but often unlearnable — random exploration may never reach the goal once. Remedies: reward shaping, curriculum learning, curiosity bonuses, or demonstrations to bootstrap from.
Reward shaping
Add intermediate rewards to guide the agent. Done carelessly it changes the optimal policy; potential-based shaping is the version with a proof that it does not.
Discount as a design choice
γ is not just a mathematical convenience. Low γ produces short-sighted agents; high γ makes credit assignment harder and learning slower. It encodes how far ahead you want the agent to care.
Constraints beat penalties
For unacceptable behaviour, a hard constraint is safer than a negative reward — a penalty can always be outweighed by enough reward elsewhere.

Honest summary

RL has produced spectacular results in games, simulation and control, and in aligning language models to human preferences. In ordinary business settings it is used far less than the coverage suggests, because most problems either lack a simulator or turn out to be bandit problems in disguise. Bandits, by contrast, are genuinely underused: if you are allocating traffic across variants and want to spend less of it on the losers, a bandit is often better than a fixed-split A/B test.

Self-check

  1. What does γ = 0.99 versus γ = 0.5 change about an agent's behaviour?
    γ = 0.99 means a reward 100 steps away still counts for about 37% of its face value, so the agent will accept short-term cost for long-term gain. γ = 0.5 halves value every step — after 10 steps a reward is worth a thousandth — so the agent becomes myopic and will not pursue distant goals at all. High γ makes credit assignment harder and learning slower, so it is a genuine trade rather than a free choice.
  2. Why does Q-learning converge to the optimal policy even though it acts randomly some of the time?
    Because it is off-policy: the update target uses max over the next state's actions, which is the value of behaving optimally from there, regardless of what the agent actually did next. Exploration affects which state-action pairs get visited and updated, not what they converge to. Given enough visits to every pair and a decaying learning rate, the table converges to Q*.
  3. Your DQN's loss oscillates wildly and the agent never improves. Name two structural fixes.
    Experience replay and a target network. Without replay, consecutive samples are strongly correlated and the network overfits to whatever it is seeing right now. Without a frozen target network, the update target shifts with every gradient step — you are regressing toward a moving target you yourself are moving, which is a feedback loop that diverges. Beyond those two, reduce the learning rate and clip gradients.
  4. Your agent achieves a high score but behaves absurdly. What went wrong?
    Reward hacking — the reward function has a maximum that does not correspond to what you wanted. The agent is working correctly; the specification is wrong. Watch the actual behaviour rather than just the reward curve, add penalties or hard constraints for the exploit, and prefer potential-based shaping so the optimal policy is provably unchanged. Expect to iterate on the reward several times; this is normal rather than a sign of failure.
  5. When would you prefer SARSA to Q-learning?
    When exploration continues during deployment and mistakes are costly. Q-learning learns the value of the optimal policy, so on a cliff-edge task it hugs the edge and falls off whenever exploration nudges it sideways. SARSA learns the value of the policy actually being followed — including its randomness — so it keeps a safety margin. If the agent will act greedily in production, Q-learning's answer is the right one.
  6. You want to test five landing-page variants and stop wasting traffic on the bad ones. Is that RL?
    It is a multi-armed bandit, which is RL with a single state — no sequential dynamics, so the full machinery is unnecessary. Thompson sampling or UCB will shift traffic toward the winners as evidence accumulates, typically recovering far more reward than a fixed-split A/B test. The caveat is that adaptive allocation complicates the statistical inference, so if you also need a defensible significance claim, plan for that up front.
