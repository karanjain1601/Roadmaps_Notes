The five through-lines

If the unit reduces to anything, it reduces to these.

1 · Digests are identity; tags are labels

Layers, configs and manifests are all named by the hash of their content, which is why pushes transfer so little (ch. 3), why a rebuild produces a different artefact (ch. 8), why promotion is a pointer move rather than a rebuild (ch. 15), why a pipeline must pass a digest between jobs (ch. 16), and why deployments should name a digest (ch. 22). Every time something surprising happened with a tag, the fix was a digest.

2 · Invalidation cascades downward

A layer's identity depends on its parent, so changing anything invalidates everything after it. That single rule generates the instruction-ordering discipline (ch. 6), the argument for multi-stage builds (ch. 7), the reason apt-get update must share a RUN with install (ch. 6), and the shape of a fast CI pipeline (ch. 16). Order by change frequency and most build problems disappear.

3 · Stopping is harder than starting

PID 1 does not get default signal handling (ch. 5), so shell form swallows SIGTERM; docker stop waits a fixed grace period and then kills (ch. 9); a proxy switch gives zero downtime and still drops in-flight requests if the drain exceeds the timeout (ch. 20); and Kubernetes renames the same setting without changing the problem (ch. 22). Exec form, a drain handler, and a timeout above the drain — in that order.

4 · Nothing is bounded unless you bound it

Memory, CPU, PIDs, shared memory, log size and capabilities all default to unlimited or over-generous (ch. 10, 18, 19). The consequences differ — a full disk, an evicted host, a capability-gated escape — and the remedy is the same shape every time: set the bound, on everything, and let one container fail instead of the machine.

5 · Evidence without enforcement is documentation

A healthcheck nobody alerts on (ch. 9, 19), a scan nobody blocks on (ch. 17), attestations nobody verifies (ch. 15), a backup nobody has restored (ch. 20), a crash loop that reports as Restarting (ch. 9). Each of these is a real artefact that changes nothing. For every signal you produce, name the thing that acts on it — and if there isn't one, you have a report rather than a control.

What not to learn yet

Service meshes, operators, custom controllers and multi-cluster anything. Every one of them solves a problem you will recognise when you have it, and none of them is worth learning speculatively — they are large, they move quickly, and reading about them without the problem produces confident opinions about trade-offs you have never faced. The same is true of custom seccomp profiles (ch. 18) and bespoke build tooling (ch. 21).

Closing

The whole unit, in eight lines

  • A container is a process with restrictions; an image is a content-addressed stack of tarballs. Both facts explain nearly everything else.
  • Digests are identity, tags are labels. Pin, promote and deploy by digest.
  • Invalidation cascades downward, so order instructions by change frequency and ship the artefact rather than the toolchain.
  • Stopping is harder than starting. Exec form, a drain handler, and a stop timeout above the drain time.
  • Nothing is bounded unless you bound it — memory, CPU, PIDs, logs, capabilities.
  • Two things mean no boundary exists at all: the Docker socket and --privileged.
  • Evidence without enforcement is documentation. Name what acts on every signal you produce.
  • Almost none of this was Docker-specific, which is why it transfers to Podman, containerd and Kubernetes intact.

Check yourself

Explain to a colleague, in three sentences, what a container is.

It is an ordinary Linux process whose view of the system has been restricted — its own filesystem, network stack and process table via namespaces, its own resource ceilings via cgroups, and a reduced set of privileges via capabilities and seccomp. Its filesystem is assembled by stacking read-only layers, each a tarball named by the hash of its contents, with one writable layer on top that is discarded when the container is removed. Nothing about it is a virtual machine: there is one kernel, and the isolation is a set of settings on a process — all of which are optional, which is why the interesting question is always which of them you actually applied.

Which of the five through-lines explains the most incidents you have seen or read about?

Reasonable answers differ, and the two strongest cases are the fourth and fifth. Unbounded resources produces the classic single-host outage — a full disk from unrotated logs, or one container's memory taking the machine — and it is the cheapest to fix. Evidence without enforcement produces the worse category: the failures nobody noticed, because a crash loop reported as Restarting, a scan described an image nobody deployed, or a backup had never been restored. The first three through-lines cause more day-to-day friction; these two cause the incidents.

You are asked to review a container setup you have never seen. What do you look at, in order?

First, whether the Docker socket is mounted anywhere and whether anything runs --privileged — while either is true, nothing else is a boundary (ch. 18). Second, whether resource limits and log rotation exist, since those are what take hosts down (ch. 10, 19). Third, what is deployed: a digest or a mutable tag, and whether a rollback target exists (ch. 15). Fourth, the stop path — exec form, a handler, and a timeout above the drain (ch. 5, 9, 20). Then the build: cache ordering, multi-stage, secrets in ARG. The reviewers in chapter 23 encode roughly this order.

Someone tells you they have "finished learning Docker". What is the honest response?

That the tool is not the hard part and largely never was. The mechanics — images, layers, networks, volumes — are a few days of study, and this unit spent most of its length on the consequences instead: what happens when you stop a container mid-request, when a tag moves under you, when a node runs short of memory, when a scan reports 187 findings, when a restore has never been rehearsed. Those are operational judgement rather than Docker knowledge, they transfer to every runtime in chapter 21 and to Kubernetes in chapter 22, and they are learned mostly by running things and reading what broke.
