The first five minutes

Nothing you do later recovers from solving the wrong problem. Four things, in order, before any code.

Restate it in your own words
Not a recital — a compression. "So: given a list of intervals, find the fewest rooms such that no two overlapping meetings share one." If your restatement is wrong you find out in ten seconds instead of twenty minutes.
Ask about the input
Sorted? Duplicates? Negatives? Empty? How large? Every one of those has broken a pattern somewhere in this unit — chapter 4's pointers need sorted, chapter 5's window needs non-negative, chapter 17's dedup needs sorted, chapter 16's Dijkstra needs non-negative.
Work one example by hand
A small one, out loud, agreeing the expected output. This is where ambiguity surfaces: does "subarray" mean contiguous? Are ties broken somehow? Can the answer be empty?
Read the constraints
Chapter 1's point, and it is worth repeating last because people skip it. The bound on n eliminates most patterns before you have thought about the problem at all.
A cubic budget I had slightly wrong

The familiar rule is "n ≤ 500 means O(n³) is fine". Recomputed against a 10⁸ budget, 500³ is 125,000,000 — 1.25× over. Cubic is comfortable to about n = 450 (400³ is 64 million) and depends on the constant factor after that.

Not a large error, but the kind worth knowing you are making: the rule is marginal, not safe, and if you claim cubic will pass at n = 500 you should say it is close.

Thinking out loud

The interviewer is evaluating your reasoning, and silence gives them nothing to evaluate. But narration is not a running commentary on your typing.

Narrate decisions, not keystrokes.

Every row on the left names a reason. That is what is being assessed — not whether you arrive, but whether your route was chosen or stumbled into. Chapter 20's counterexample hunt is the strongest version of this: saying "let me check greedy on a small case before committing" and then finding the counterexample yourself is worth more than a correct answer produced silently.

A brute force stated aloud is never wrong to have

It costs nothing socially, it establishes that you understood the problem, it gives you something correct to compare against, and — most usefully — the optimisation usually comes from asking what is this recomputing? Chapter 18's whole subject is what happens when the answer is "the same subproblem, thousands of times".

Candidates avoid the brute force because it feels like an admission. It is the opposite: it converts "I am stuck" into "I have a working solution and I am improving it".

Chapter 2's measurement applies here too, re-derived: the optimal struggle window is 22 minutes, and 20 minutes of it captures 60% of what you would have got. In a 45-minute interview that number is smaller — asking for a hint at minute 20 costs far less than being silent until minute 40.

The last five minutes

Trace your own code on the example you agreed at the start — by hand, aloud, line by line. Almost every off-by-one this unit catalogued shows up in that trace: chapter 8's binary search boundary, chapter 12's node-versus-edge depth, chapter 19's a[i-1].

Then state the complexity, both time and space, and say what dominates. "O(n log n), dominated by the sort" tells the interviewer you know where the cost is. And if you space-optimised, say what you gave up — chapters 18 and 19 both found that rolling the array discards the ability to reconstruct the answer.

Volunteer the case you did not handle

"This assumes the input has at least one element; I'd add a guard." "This mutates the caller's array; in production I'd copy it." Chapter 11 flagged a function that returns the right value while mangling its input as a real defect even when the return is correct — noticing that unprompted is a cheap way to demonstrate care, and it is much better than the interviewer finding it.

Fresh code, fresh seeds, fresh generators — and the rates land within a few points. They are properties of the bugs, not of the test harnesses, which is the only reason quoting them is worth anything.

Plausible, silent, and wrong on a minority

Chapters 4, 7, 15, 16, 19, 20, 22 and 23 each found a shortcut that looks right, produces a plausible answer, throws no error, and is wrong on some fraction of inputs. Nothing about the code signals a problem. The tests you would naturally write pass.

And the low rates turned out to be the dangerous ones. Chapter 7's shortest-first interval selection is wrong on 1.8% of inputs and chapter 16's Dijkstra with negative edges on under 1% — those survive every hand-written test and fail on submission. The 86% failures announce themselves immediately.

Which is why nearly every chapter ended up with a verification section rather than a proof. The habit the unit is actually trying to build is not "know these patterns" but: when you reach for a plausible shortcut, spend thirty seconds generating random inputs and comparing against something obviously correct. Chapter 20 measured that hunt at a median of eight instances.

Check yourself

What are the four things to do before writing any code?

Restate the problem in your own words as a compression rather than a recital; ask about the input (sorted, duplicates, negatives, empty, size — each of those has broken a pattern in this unit); work one small example by hand aloud, agreeing the expected output; and read the constraints. Nothing you do later recovers from solving the wrong problem, and a wrong restatement is caught in ten seconds instead of twenty minutes.

Does n ≤ 500 really mean O(n³) is fine?

Marginally. Against a 10⁸ budget, 500³ is 125,000,000 — 1.25× over. Cubic is comfortable to about n = 450 (400³ is 64 million) and depends on the constant factor beyond that. Not a big error, but the kind worth knowing you are making: if you tell an interviewer cubic will pass at n = 500, say that it is close rather than that it is safe.

What should you narrate, and what should you not?

Decisions, not keystrokes. "The input is sorted, so two pointers instead of a hash map" names a reason; "now I'm writing a for loop" names nothing. What is being assessed is whether your route was chosen or stumbled into. The strongest version is chapter 20's counterexample hunt: saying "let me check greedy on a small case before committing" and finding the counterexample yourself beats a correct answer produced in silence.

You are stuck with no idea where to start. What is the first move?

Say what you know out loud — it costs nothing and lets the interviewer correct a wrong premise before you spend ten minutes on it. Then solve a smaller version (n = 1, n = 2 often expose the recurrence), then state a brute force. Candidates avoid the brute force because it feels like an admission; it is the opposite, converting "I am stuck" into "I have something correct and I am improving it". And the optimisation usually comes straight out of asking what it recomputes.

When should you ask for a hint?

Earlier than feels comfortable. Chapter 2's optimal struggle window re-derives to 22 minutes in a self-study setting, and a 45-minute interview is not that setting — you have a second half to get through. Asking at minute 20 costs far less than being silent until minute 40, and the ladder is ordered cheapest-first for that reason: say what you know, shrink the problem, brute force, look for redundancy, re-read the constraints, then ask.

You finish with five minutes left. What do you do with them?

Trace your own code on the example you agreed at the start, by hand and aloud. Almost every off-by-one this unit catalogued surfaces in that trace — chapter 8's binary search boundary, chapter 12's node-versus-edge depth, chapter 19's a[i-1]. Then state time and space complexity and say what dominates. Then volunteer what you did not handle: "this mutates the caller's array" is much better coming from you than from them.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Restate, ask about the input, work an example, read the constraints — before any code.
  2. The constraint bound eliminates most patterns before you have thought about the problem.
  3. Cubic at n = 500 is 1.25× over budget. Marginal, not safe.
  4. Narrate decisions, not keystrokes. Every sentence should name a reason.
  5. A brute force stated aloud is never wrong to have, and the optimisation usually follows from it.
  6. Ask for a hint at minute 20, not minute 40.
  7. Correct and slow beats elegant and unfinished, in every rubric.
  8. Trace your own code by hand — that is where the off-by-ones surface.
  9. Volunteer the case you did not handle. Much better from you than from them.
  10. Hunt for a counterexample before trusting a shortcut. Median eight instances.

Closing

Twenty-four chapters, forty-eight demos, and one finding that kept arriving unannounced.

Every demo in this unit runs the real algorithm rather than a description of it, and almost every chapter contains at least one figure that came out differently from what I expected to write. The trie made Word Search II slower until pruning was added. Path compression cost more pointer hops than union by rank. The naive edit-distance recursion was 21× slower rather than thousands. Consecutive Fibonacci numbers are Euclid's worst case because subtraction ties there. The sieve's i² start saves 6%, not a lot. Submask enumeration is exactly 3ⁿ with no correction term. Those are in the text because a chapter that only reports the figures matching its thesis is not worth reading.

The patterns themselves are the smaller half of what is here. The larger half is the habit of not believing a plausible claim — including your own — until something has run. Eight chapters found the same bug shape: a shortcut that looks right, produces a reasonable-looking answer, throws no error, and is wrong on a minority of inputs. The ones wrong 80% of the time are harmless, because you find them immediately. The ones wrong 2% of the time are the ones that ship.

Interviews reward the same thing, for the same reason. Nobody can tell from the outside whether your greedy rule is provable or merely appealing. You demonstrate it by trying to break it in front of them — and the thirty seconds that takes is the most reliably valuable thing in this entire unit.

Pattern first, code second. Then try to break it.
