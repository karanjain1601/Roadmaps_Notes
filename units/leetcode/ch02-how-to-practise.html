How to practise

Forty-five minutes on a problem captures 87% of what it can teach you. Ninety minutes captures 98%. The second forty-five are worth about a fifth of the first twenty, and almost everybody spends them anyway.

The loop

Most people practise by solving problems. That is one step of five, and it is not the one that produces the reflex chapter 1 described.

One problem, five steps
StepTimeWhat it builds
1. Read and classify2 minThe recognition reflex. Name the signals and the candidate patterns before anything else
2. State brute force and the target3 minThe habit that stops you optimising toward nothing
3. Attemptup to 40 minThe actual struggle — and the section below is about when to stop
4. Read the solution properly10 minUnderstanding why, not copying what
5. Debrief in writing5 minThe step that does most of the work, and the one everyone skips

Notice the ratio. Roughly a third of the session is spent not attempting the problem, and that third is where the transferable part comes from. A candidate who has solved four hundred problems without ever doing steps 1, 2 and 5 has four hundred memories and no method.

The struggle window

Struggling is necessary — a solution you reach for immediately teaches nothing, because retrieval strength is built by the effort of retrieval. But the returns diminish sharply, and the shape of the curve is not intuitive.

The measured curve for a normal problem: 20 minutes captures 60% of what it can teach, 45 minutes captures 87%, and 90 minutes captures 98%. Doubling from 45 to 90 adds eleven points — worth about a fifth of what the first twenty minutes bought.

Marginal learning per minute is highest at the very start, so taken naively the model says stop after one minute, which is obviously wrong. What is missing is that every problem carries a fixed cost that teaches nothing: reading it, classifying it, reading the solution, writing the debrief — call it fifteen minutes. Maximising learning per minute of clock time rather than per minute of attempting gives a real answer: stop at 22 minutes for a normal problem, capturing 63%. A familiar pattern is worth 15; a genuinely new one 30.

The rule that follows

Give it a hard time limit — 30 minutes for easy and medium, 45 for hard, which is deliberately generous against the model's 22 because a ceiling should not bind on your good days — and when the timer goes, take a hint rather than the solution. A hint restarts the struggle at the point where it stopped being productive; the full solution ends it. If the hint does not unstick you within another ten minutes, read the solution properly and move to step 4. The failure mode is not giving up too early; it is spending ninety minutes to buy eleven points you could have bought elsewhere at four times the rate.

Two caveats the model does not capture. It assumes the overhead is fixed at fifteen minutes, and if you skip the debrief the overhead falls and the optimum shortens — which is an argument for the debrief, not against it. And the time limit is for practice, not for the interview. In a real interview there is no hint button, and the ability to keep making visible progress while stuck is itself assessed. Chapter 24 covers what to do there.

The debrief does the work

Step 5 is five minutes of writing, and it is the difference between a problem you have seen and a pattern you have learned. The reason is that it forces the abstraction: a memory of one problem transfers to nothing; a note about the signal transfers to every problem sharing it.

the five questions — same five every time
1. What was the SIGNAL I should have spotted?
   → "k largest" and "streaming" — I reached for sorting instead of a heap.

2. What is the pattern, in one sentence?
   → Min-heap of size k; push, and pop when it exceeds k.

3. Where exactly did I go wrong, or slow?
   → I spent 12 minutes on an O(n log n) sort before reading "stream".

4. What is the ONE line I'd tell myself before starting this again?
   → "Streaming plus top-k means a heap of size k, never a sort."

5. When should I see this again?
   → Tomorrow, then in three days.

Question 4 is the one that pays. It produces a single sentence you can reread in ten seconds, and a hundred of those sentences is a document you can revise from — which a hundred solved problems is not.

Reading a solution is not learning it

Understanding a solution while looking at it is recognition, and it feels almost identical to knowing. It is not the same skill as recall, which is what the interview tests. The check is cheap: close the tab and re-derive the approach from the signal alone. If you cannot, you have recognised it and not learned it, and the next section is about the schedule that fixes that.

Spacing, and the arithmetic of forgetting

Solve a problem today and you will not be able to re-derive it in a week. That is not a failure of effort; it is the standard forgetting curve, and it has a shape you can plan around.

Two numbers make the case. With no review, retention of a freshly solved problem is 15% after three days and 1% after seven — which is why the problem you solved last Tuesday is genuinely gone. And the review gaps must widen: each successful recall multiplies the memory's strength, so the schedule runs 0.2 → 0.6 → 1.4 → 3.5 → 8.2 → 19.1 → 44 → 101 days, with every gap exactly 2.3× the one before it.

Eight reviews, not eight sittings

Those eight reviews cover six months and take a few minutes each — you are re-deriving the approach from the signal, not re-solving the problem. By the last one the memory is 783 times stronger than it was on day one, and retention a month later is 98%. Cramming the same eight reviews into a single session gets 88%: still far better than nothing, and ten points worse for identical effort, because spacing does work that repetition alone cannot.

In practice this does not need software. A note per problem with a "see again" date, sorted by date, reviewed at the start of each session, is enough — and the widening gaps mean the daily review load stays roughly constant even as the collection grows.

Cover the space, do not grind the list

"Do 300 problems" is the standard advice and it optimises the wrong variable. What you need is to have met every pattern several times; the problem count is a side effect.

Chapter 25 of the Computer Science unit has the arithmetic: if you pick problems at random and each teaches one of 20 patterns, this is the coupon collector, and you need n·H(n) draws to see them all — 72 problems. Choosing deliberately takes 20. Random practice costs 3.6× as much for the same coverage, and the excess is concentrated in the patterns you have already over-learned.

And "once" is the floor. Recognition needs three or four encounters per pattern spread over weeks, which is roughly 60 to 80 deliberately chosen problems — a very different plan from 300 random ones, and a much shorter one.

A schedule that works

Take one pattern per session. Solve two or three problems in it back to back, so the template becomes automatic while the context is loaded. Then interleave: the following session takes a different pattern, and your review queue mixes old ones in. Blocked practice — twenty sliding-window problems in a row — feels much more productive and transfers much less, because you never practise the step that actually matters, which is deciding which pattern applies.

The common thread is that all five substitute an easily measured proxy — problems solved, time spent, difficulty attempted — for the thing that is hard to measure and actually matters, which is whether you can name the pattern cold. The debrief note from section three is the cheapest available measurement of that, which is why it is worth five minutes a problem.

Check yourself

You have been stuck for 45 minutes. Push on, or read the solution?

Neither — take a hint. At 45 minutes you have captured about 87% of what the problem can teach; another 45 adds eleven points, which is roughly a fifth of what the first twenty minutes bought and less than a fresh problem would give you for the same time. A hint restarts the struggle at the point it stopped being productive, while the full solution ends it. If the hint does not unstick you within ten minutes, read the solution properly and do the debrief.

Which step of the loop matters most, and which gets skipped?

They are the same step: the five-minute written debrief. Solving produces a memory of one problem; the debrief produces a sentence about the signal, and that transfers to every problem sharing it. Question four — "what is the one line I'd tell myself before starting this again?" — is the one that pays, because a hundred of those sentences is a revisable document and a hundred solved problems is not. Roughly a third of a good session is spent not attempting the problem.

You understood the solution completely while reading it. Have you learned it?

Probably not. Understanding while looking at something is recognition, and it feels almost identical to knowing while being a different skill from recall, which is what an interview tests. The check costs nothing: close the tab and re-derive the approach from the signal alone. Most people cannot, the first time. That gap is exactly what the spaced review schedule exists to close, and it is why reviewing means re-deriving rather than re-reading.

Why must the review gaps widen rather than stay fixed?

Because each successful recall multiplies the memory's strength, so the interval before it decays to the same level multiplies too. With a 2.3× gain per recall the schedule runs 0.2, 0.6, 1.4, 3.5, 8.2, 19.1, 44 and 101 days — each gap exactly 2.3× the previous one. Reviewing at a fixed interval either wastes effort early, when the memory is still strong, or arrives too late once it has decayed past the point where recall is possible. The widening is also what keeps the daily load constant as the collection grows.

Is it better to do seven reviews today or seven reviews spread over six months?

Spread. Eight spaced reviews leave the memory 783 times stronger than day one and 98% retention a month later; the same eight crammed into one sitting give 88%. The reason is that spacing forces genuine retrieval each time — you have partly forgotten, so recall does work — whereas within a single session the answer is still in mind and you are rehearsing rather than retrieving. This is why the day before an interview is for review, not for learning anything new.

"Do 300 problems." What is wrong with that as a plan?

It optimises the count rather than the coverage. Picking at random, meeting all 20 patterns once takes about 72 problems by the coupon-collector arithmetic, against 20 chosen deliberately — 3.6× the work, with the excess concentrated in patterns you already knew. Since recognition needs three or four encounters per pattern spread over weeks, a deliberate plan is roughly 60 to 80 problems. That is a quarter of the usual advice, and a very different quarter.

Twenty sliding-window problems in a row: good idea?

Two or three is good; twenty is blocked practice and it transfers poorly. Within the block you never have to decide which pattern applies, because you already know — and that decision is the skill chapter 1 said was actually being tested. Blocked practice reliably feels more productive than interleaved practice while producing less durable learning, which is what makes it a trap. Do two or three to make the template automatic, then switch patterns and let the review queue mix old ones back in.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. Solving is one step of five, and a third of a good session is spent not attempting the problem.
  2. 45 minutes captures 87% of what a problem teaches; 90 minutes captures 98%.
  3. Counting the 15 minutes of overhead every problem carries, the best stopping point is 22 minutes for a normal problem.
  4. The second 45 minutes are worth a fifth of the first 20.
  5. Take a hint, not the solution. A hint restarts the struggle; the solution ends it.
  6. The written debrief is the step that transfers, because it abstracts from the problem to the signal.
  7. Recognition is not recall. Close the tab and re-derive, or you have only recognised it.
  8. Retention is 15% after three days and 1% after seven with no review at all.
  9. Review gaps must widen — 0.2, 0.6, 1.4, 3.5, 8.2, 19, 44, 101 days, each 2.3× the last.
  10. Spaced beats massed: 98% against 88% for exactly the same number of reviews.
  11. Random practice costs 3.6× as much coverage, so choose by pattern and interleave.
