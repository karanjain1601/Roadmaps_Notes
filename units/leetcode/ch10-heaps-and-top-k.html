Chapter 10 · Ordering and search

Heaps and top-k

To find the ten largest of a hundred thousand values you keep a heap of the ten smallest things you have decided to keep. The direction feels backwards for about a minute, and then it is the only direction that makes sense.

Partial order is the point

A heap is not a sorted array. Push 5, 3, 8, 1, 9, 2, 7 into a min-heap and the underlying array is [1, 3, 2, 5, 9, 8, 7] — the root is the minimum and nothing else is in order.

That is not a limitation to apologise for; it is the whole economy of the structure. Sorting establishes the relationship between every pair. A heap establishes only parent-below-child, which is enough to know the extreme in Θ(1) and costs Θ(log n) to maintain. The work it declines to do is why it is cheap.

What each operation costs

The last row is the boundary. If a problem needs to look things up by key, or asks for anything other than the extreme, a heap alone is the wrong structure — you need a hash map alongside it, or a different structure entirely.

// the weakest survivor, which is exactly the one a new candidate
// must beat to earn a place.
The rule, and why it is not arbitrary

For the k largest, keep a MIN-heap of size k. For the k smallest, keep a MAX-heap of size k.

The reason is the gate test. You need instant access to the weakest thing currently held, because that is the one a new candidate has to beat and the one you evict when it does. In a collection of the largest values, the weakest is the smallest — so the heap must surface the minimum.

Measured on 100,000 values with k = 10: the size-k min-heap does 100,618 comparisons, a max-heap of everything does 228,835, quickselect does 160,563, and a full sort does 1,529,198. The heap is about 15× cheaper than sorting.

Count the gate test, or you will flatter the heap

My first version of that measurement reported 628 comparisons for the min-heap — a 2,400-fold win — because I counted comparisons inside the heap and forgot that v > h.peek() is itself a comparison, performed once per element. Almost every element fails that test and never touches the heap at all, so the heap's internal counter stayed tiny while the real work sat in the loop.

The honest figure is 100,618, and the honest lesson is that n log k has an n in front of it. You still look at every element once. The heap saves you from ordering them, not from seeing them.

Even at k = n/2 the heap does fewer comparisons than sorting — which surprised me, and is worth being precise about rather than hand-waving. What degrades is not the comparison count but the memory: a heap holding 50,000 items is not meaningfully better than the array you started with.

The size-k heap is a space result before it is a time one

The argument for it is that k items fit in memory and n do not. That is why it is the right answer for a stream — a firehose of events where you want the top ten and cannot store the rest — and only marginally the right answer for an array you already hold. Say that out loud in an interview and it lands better than quoting n log k.

K-way merge, and an honest measurement

Merging k sorted lists with a heap: seed it with the first element of each list, then repeatedly pop the smallest and push its successor. The heap holds at most k items, one per list.

On 200 lists of 500 elements each, the heap merge does 1,459,550 comparisons and concatenating everything then sorting does 887,006. The naive method is cheaper.

So why does anyone use the heap?

Because the heap holds 200 items and concat-and-sort holds 100,000. That is the entire argument, and it is the same argument as the previous section: this is a space result. When the lists are files on disk, or streams from 200 servers, or a database's sorted runs during an external merge sort, holding all of them at once is not an option and the comparison count is irrelevant.

It would have been easy to report the heap as the winner here and leave it. It is not the winner on this input, and knowing why you would still choose it is worth more than a fabricated speedup.

The same structure solves Merge K Sorted Lists, Kth Smallest Element in a Sorted Matrix, Smallest Range Covering Elements from K Lists, and the merge phase of external sorting. The recognisable shape is several sorted sources, one ordered output.

Two heaps facing each other

A running median over a stream is the classic two-heap problem, and the construction is elegant enough to be worth knowing even outside interviews.

three lines, and each one is load-bearing
lo = max-heap   // the smaller half; its root is that half's LARGEST
hi = min-heap   // the larger half; its root is that half's SMALLEST

add(v):
    lo.push(v)                          // 1. always land in lo
    hi.push(lo.pop())                   // 2. hand lo's largest across —
                                        //    this is what keeps the halves ORDERED
    if (hi.size > lo.size)              // 3. rebalance so lo is never smaller
        lo.push(hi.pop())

median():
    return lo.size > hi.size ? lo.peek()
                             : (lo.peek() + hi.peek()) / 2

Verified against a full sort at every one of 2,000 steps. The two roots sit either side of the middle, so the median is one peek or the average of two — Θ(1) to read, Θ(log n) to insert.

Drop line 3 and it fails on the second element

Without the rebalance, every value passes straight through lo into hi: after 200 additions lo is empty and hi holds all 200. The median comes out as the minimum of the whole stream. It is wrong at 199 of the first 200 steps, and the first failure is at step 2 — reporting 4 where the answer is 97.5.

Untick the checkbox in the demo to watch lo drain. The two-line version looks like it should work, which is exactly why the third line is worth understanding rather than memorising: steps 1 and 2 establish the ordering between the halves, and step 3 establishes their sizes. Both invariants are needed and neither implies the other.

The last one causes more lost minutes than it should. Python has no max-heap, so the idiom is to push -v and negate on the way out. Java's PriorityQueue is a min-heap taking a comparator; C++'s priority_queue is a max-heap by default, which is the opposite. Know your language's default before the interview rather than during it.

Check yourself

Is a heap's underlying array sorted?

No. Pushing 5, 3, 8, 1, 9, 2, 7 into a min-heap gives [1, 3, 2, 5, 9, 8, 7] — the root is the minimum and nothing else is ordered. Only the parent-below-child relation holds. That partial order is the entire economy of the structure: sorting establishes the relationship between every pair, a heap establishes one relation per edge, and the work it declines to do is why peek is Θ(1) and maintenance is Θ(log n).

To find the k largest values, which way should the heap point?

A min-heap of size k. It holds the k largest values seen so far, and its root is the smallest of them — the weakest survivor, which is exactly the element a new candidate must beat and the one you evict when it does. If the heap surfaced the maximum you would have instant access to the value you least need to think about. For the k smallest, point it the other way: a max-heap of size k.

Why is "n log k" a misleading way to think about the saving?

Because the n is in front. You still examine every element once, and each examination is a comparison against the heap's root. On 100,000 values with k = 10 the true count is 100,618 comparisons, not the 628 you get by counting only what happens inside the heap — most elements fail the gate test and never enter it. Sorting costs 1,529,198, so the honest advantage is about 15×. The heap saves you from ordering the elements, not from seeing them.

At k = n/2, is the size-k heap still better than sorting?

In comparisons, yes — 1,197,094 against 1,529,198, which is not the answer people expect. What degrades is memory: a heap holding 50,000 of your 100,000 values is barely an improvement on the array you already had. The real argument for a size-k heap is that k items fit and n do not, which makes it a space result before it is a time one — and the right answer for a stream rather than for an array you are already holding.

Merging 200 sorted lists: heap, or concatenate and sort?

Measured on 200 lists of 500, concatenating and sorting does 887,006 comparisons and the heap merge does 1,459,550 — the naive method is cheaper. You still choose the heap when it matters, because it holds 200 items against the other method's 100,000. When the lists are files, or streams from 200 servers, or a database's sorted runs, holding everything is not an option. If the lists are small arrays already in memory, concatenate and sort and say why.

In the two-heap median, what does each of the three lines do?

Line 1 pushes into lo unconditionally. Line 2 moves lo's largest into hi, which is what guarantees every element of lo is at most every element of hi — the ordering invariant. Line 3 moves one back if hi has grown larger, which maintains the size invariant of differing by at most one. Both are needed and neither implies the other, which is why the two-line version looks correct and is not.

What happens if you skip the rebalance?

Every value passes straight through lo into hi, so lo ends up empty and hi holds everything — after 200 additions the sizes are 0 and 200. The reported median becomes the minimum of the entire stream. It is wrong at 199 of the first 200 steps, failing first at step 2 by reporting 4 where the answer is 97.5. The failure is immediate and total, which is at least merciful compared with the silent-on-a-quarter-of-inputs failures earlier in this unit.

Ten things to carry forward

  1. A heap gives partial order, and the ordering it declines to do is why it is cheap.
  2. The array is not sorted — [1,3,2,5,9,8,7] is a perfectly valid min-heap.
  3. For the k largest, keep a min-heap of size k, because you need the weakest survivor.
  4. The gate test is a comparison. Counting it turns a fake 2,400× win into an honest 15×.
  5. n log k still has an n: you see every element, you just do not order them.
  6. The size-k heap is a space result — k fits and n does not.
  7. Concat-and-sort beats a k-way heap merge in comparisons, and holds 500× more memory.
  8. Two heaps facing each other give a Θ(1) median over a stream.
  9. The ordering and the size invariants are separate, and both lines are needed.
  10. A heap cannot find an arbitrary element, so pair it with a hash map or use something else.
