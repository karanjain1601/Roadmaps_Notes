CHAPTER 14

Types, dates and categories

A column of dates written the British way and read the American way does not fail. It errors on 61% of rows and silently swaps the day and month on the other 36% — and on any subset where every day happens to be twelve or less, it errors on nothing at all and gets every single date wrong.

    THE AMBIGUITY

    Parsing dates

    Nothing in 03/04/2024 says which number is the day. Both readings are valid dates, so no parser can detect the mistake.

    03/04/2024 is the third of April to most of the world and the fourth of March in the United States. A parser can only guess, and when it guesses wrong on a value like this there is nothing to raise an error about — the result is a real date, in the right year, plausibly near the intended one.

    What happens when the format is read the wrong way round 20,000 dates written DD/MM/YYYY, parsed both ways
    FAILED TO PARSE0
    SILENTLY WRONG0parsed, and not the right date
    CORRECT0
    MONTH COUNTS OFF BY0at worst, per month

    Three results, and the third is the one to be frightened of.

    1. Read the wrong way, 61% of rows error and 36% parse silently wrong. Only 3% come out correct — the ones where the day happens to equal the month. So errors="coerce" leaves you a column that is three-fifths missing and one-third wrong.
    2. The derived features are what break. Every surviving row had its day and month swapped, so the month distribution is distorted by up to 65% per month. Any seasonality, month-on-month trend or day-of-week analysis built on that column is meaningless, and it will look entirely reasonable.
    3. On a subset where every day is 12 or less, nothing errors at all. A monthly report, a first-of-month extract, a small sample: 7,209 of 7,823 rows silently wrong, zero failures, and a column that passes every check you would think to run.
    Never let a date format be inferred

    State it, and the parse becomes incapable of this failure:

    pd.to_datetime(s, format="%d/%m/%Y")

    With an explicit format, a mismatch raises rather than guessing. That converts the worst failure mode in this chapter — silently wrong on rows that could not error — into an exception on the first bad row. Since pandas 2.0 the inference is at least consistent across a column rather than per row, which removed a worse variant of this, and stating the format is still the only thing that makes it safe.

    And prefer ISO 8601 — 2024-04-03 — whenever you control the format. It is unambiguous, it sorts correctly as text, and every tool reads it the same way.

    dates.py
    # state the format; a mismatch then raises instead of guessing
df["date"] = pd.to_datetime(df["date"], format="%d/%m/%Y")

# at read time, which is better still
pd.read_csv(path, parse_dates=["date"], date_format="%d/%m/%Y")

# mixed formats in one column: parse each subset explicitly
iso = df["date"].str.match(r"^\d{4}-")
df.loc[iso, "parsed"]  = pd.to_datetime(df.loc[iso, "date"], format="%Y-%m-%d")
df.loc[~iso, "parsed"] = pd.to_datetime(df.loc[~iso, "date"], format="%d/%m/%Y")

# and always check what you got
print(df["date"].min(), df["date"].max(), df["date"].isna().sum())

    That last line is the cheapest date check there is. A minimum in 1970 means an epoch-zero default; a maximum in 2087 means a two-digit year read as 20xx; a suspiciously round count of nulls means a coerced failure. Ten seconds, and it catches most date problems before they reach an analysis.

    THE OTHER HALF

    Timezones

    Two kinds of timestamp, and mixing them is the second-largest source of date bugs.

    Naive
    A timestamp with no zone: 2024-04-03 14:30:00. It means whatever the writer intended, which you have to know from somewhere else. Fine for a local business date, and ambiguous the moment two systems are involved.
    Aware
    A timestamp with a zone: 2024-04-03 14:30:00+01:00. Unambiguous. Comparable across systems. What you want for anything with a time component.
    timezones.py
    s.dt.tz_localize("Europe/London")     # naive -> aware: ATTACH a zone
s.dt.tz_convert("America/New_York")   # aware -> aware: CHANGE the zone
s.dt.tz_localize(None)                # aware -> naive: drop the zone, keep the clock

# comparing a naive series to an aware one raises. That is a feature.
# the fix is to make both aware, not to strip the zone from one.

# DST: these two calls are not the same thing
s + pd.Timedelta(days=1)              # exactly 24 hours later
s + pd.DateOffset(days=1)             # the same clock time tomorrow
    Three timezone failures worth recognising

    Storing local time without a zone. On the day the clocks go back, one local hour occurs twice and cannot be distinguished, so events cannot be ordered. tz_localize raises on those timestamps rather than guessing, which is correct and looks like a bug.

    Confusing Timedelta with DateOffset. Adding 24 hours across a DST boundary lands at a different clock time; adding one calendar day lands at the same clock time. Which you want depends on whether you mean "a day of elapsed time" or "tomorrow", and those genuinely differ twice a year.

    Grouping UTC timestamps by day. A UTC day is not a local day, so a daily total computed in UTC attributes an hour of one day to another. For anything reported to people, convert to the local zone before truncating to a date.

    The rule that avoids nearly all of this: store UTC, convert at the edges. Keep aware UTC timestamps everywhere internally, and convert to a local zone only when producing something a person will read. That is the same discipline the Data Engineer unit applies to pipelines, and it works identically inside one analysis.

    USING THEM

    Date arithmetic and components

    The .dt accessor, and the four operations that come up constantly.

    date_ops.py
    s.dt.year, s.dt.month, s.dt.day, s.dt.hour
s.dt.day_name()            # "Wednesday"
s.dt.dayofweek             # Monday = 0
s.dt.quarter
s.dt.days_in_month
s.dt.is_month_end

# truncate to a period — the readable way to group by month
df.groupby(df["date"].dt.to_period("M"))["revenue"].sum()
df["month"] = df["date"].dt.to_period("M").dt.to_timestamp()   # back to a date

# differences give Timedeltas; divide to get a number
(df["shipped"] - df["ordered"]).dt.days
(df["shipped"] - df["ordered"]) / pd.Timedelta(hours=1)        # in hours

# resample: groupby for a DatetimeIndex
df.set_index("date").resample("W")["revenue"].sum()            # weekly
    to_period("M") rather than strftime("%Y-%m")

    Both give you a month to group by. A period is a real ordered type, so it sorts correctly, supports arithmetic (period + 1 is next month), and knows its own boundaries. A formatted string sorts correctly only by luck of the ISO ordering and breaks the moment somebody uses "%b %Y", which gives you Apr before Jan.

    The same argument runs through the next section: an ordered type sorts by meaning, a string sorts alphabetically.

    One thing to be careful about with resample. It fills in periods with no data, which is usually what you want for a time series and is a surprise the first time a weekly total appears for a week your data does not cover. resample("W").sum() gives 0 for an empty week; .mean() gives NaN. Which is right depends on whether an absent week means zero revenue or unknown revenue, and that is a question about the data rather than about pandas.

    THE OTHER TYPE

    Categoricals

    Chapter 5 measured the memory saving. The behavioural differences matter more, and two of them will surprise you.

    Ordering, and the groups that come back empty a severity column, sorted and grouped both ways
    FIRST IN SORT ORDER—
    COMPARISONS—
    GROUPS RETURNED0
    EMPTY GROUPS0with no rows behind them

    Two behaviours, both worth knowing before you convert a column.

    An unordered category still sorts alphabetically
    Converting to category saves memory and does nothing for ordering. Low, Medium, High, Critical sorts to Critical, High, Low, Medium as plain strings and as an unordered category alike — so a chart axis, a summary table and a groupby all come out in a meaningless order. An ordered category fixes it, and also enables < and >, which an unordered one raises on.
    A category remembers levels that are no longer present
    That is the point of the type — the levels are the column's domain, not just the values it happens to contain. So after filtering to a region where the highest severity never occurs, groupby with observed=False returns 4 groups where only 3 have data, and the empty one has a count of 0 and a mean of NaN. observed=True returns 3.
    Which default you get has changed

    observed=False was the default for years and became observed=True in pandas 3.0. So the same code gives a different number of rows depending on the version, which is a reproducibility problem of exactly the kind chapter 1 asked you to record library versions for.

    Pass it explicitly. Both behaviours are useful — False guarantees every level appears, which is what you want for a fixed-shape report, and True gives only what the data contains. Neither is wrong and relying on the default is.

    categorical.py
    from pandas.api.types import CategoricalDtype

sev = CategoricalDtype(["Low", "Medium", "High", "Critical"], ordered=True)
df["severity"] = df["severity"].astype(sev)

df["severity"].sort_values()          # Low, Medium, High, Critical
df[df["severity"] >= "High"]          # works only because it is ordered
df.groupby("severity", observed=True).size()    # say which you mean

# a value not in the categories becomes NaN, silently
df["severity"] = df["severity"].astype(sev)     # "Urgent" -> NaN
print(df["severity"].isna().sum())              # so check

    That last point is a real trap: astype to a categorical dtype converts any value outside the declared categories to NaN without warning. If you declare four levels and the data has five, one level silently becomes missing data. Compare value_counts(dropna=False) before and after.

    CLEANING TEXT

    String methods and regex

    The .str accessor is vectorised. A loop over a text column is chapter 4's mistake in a different costume.

    strings.py
    s.str.strip().str.lower()                     # the two that fix most joins
s.str.replace(r"[^\w\s]", "", regex=True)     # drop punctuation
s.str.contains("refund", case=False, na=False)   # na=False or NaN propagates
s.str.extract(r"(\d{4})-(\d{2})")             # capture groups -> columns
s.str.split("|", expand=True)                 # one column per part
s.str.len(), s.str.startswith("GB")

# the diagnostic that finds the problem
s.str.strip().ne(s).sum()                     # how many have stray whitespace
s.value_counts().head(30)                     # near-duplicate spellings
    Trailing whitespace is the commonest cause of a join that matches nothing

    "North " and "North" are different keys, and they look identical in every output you will look at. Chapter 8 listed dtype mismatch as a cause of an empty join; whitespace is the other one, and it is more common.

    s.str.strip().ne(s).sum() counts affected rows in one line. Run it on every key column before joining, and .str.strip().str.lower() on both sides of a join is a habit worth making automatic.

    Two notes on regex in pandas. na=False on contains matters because the default propagates NaN, and a boolean mask containing NaN cannot be used for indexing — you get an error some lines later that says nothing about the real cause. And extract is the underused one: it turns a semi-structured column into several typed columns in one call, which is frequently the whole job for a code, a reference or an embedded date.

    DELIBERATELY

    A casting routine

    Five lines, run once at the top of an analysis, that prevent most of Part 4.

    cast.py
    SCHEMA = {"order_id": "string", "customer_id": "string",   # ids as text, per ch 9
          "revenue": "float64", "quantity": "Int64"}       # nullable int, per ch 12

df = pd.read_csv(path, dtype=SCHEMA,
                 parse_dates=["ordered_at"], date_format="%d/%m/%Y",
                 na_values=["unknown", "-", "999"])        # your sentinels, per ch 13

df["region"] = df["region"].str.strip().astype("category")
df["severity"] = df["severity"].astype(sev)                # ordered, per this chapter

assert df["ordered_at"].notna().all(), "some dates failed to parse"
assert df["revenue"].between(0, 1e7).all(), "revenue out of plausible range"
  1. Identifiers as string, because chapter 9 measured 17,045 distinct ids collapsing to 470 when they were read as floats.
  2. Nullable Int64 for counts that can be absent, so one missing value does not promote the column to float.
  3. Dates with an explicit format, so the failure in this chapter's first demo becomes an exception.
  4. Your own sentinels in na_values, found by chapter 13's nlargest check.
  5. Two assertions. One that the dates parsed, one that a key measure is in a plausible range. Both are one line and both fail at the top of the notebook rather than in a chart at the bottom.
    6. The through-line of Part 4

    Every chapter in this part has the same shape. There is a decision — how to handle missing values, what counts as an outlier, which way round the date is, whether a level exists — and the default makes it for you, silently, in a way that looks like no decision at all.

    Making the decision explicitly is the whole technique. Not because the default is usually wrong, but because a stated choice can be reviewed, reproduced and argued with, and an implicit one cannot.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Nothing in 03/04/2024 says which number is the day, so a wrong guess produces a real date rather than an error.

    3. The month distribution is distorted by up to 65% per month, so any seasonality or weekday analysis on that column is meaningless and looks fine.

    7. Store UTC, convert at the edges. A UTC day is not a local day, so truncate after converting.
