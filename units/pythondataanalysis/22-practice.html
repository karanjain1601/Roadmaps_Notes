    WHEN A NUMBER IS WRONG

    A routine, in order

    Seven steps. They are ordered by how often they find the problem, not by how sophisticated they are.

    1. Check the row count at every stage
    len(df) after each step. A count that grows is chapter 8's merge fan-out; one that shrinks unexpectedly is a filter or an inner join. This finds more problems than the other six put together, and it takes thirty seconds.
    2. Check the dtypes
    df.dtypes. An object where a number belongs explains a wrong sort, a wrong max, and a column missing from describe() — chapter 2 measured all three.
    3. Check the nulls, before and after
    df.isna().sum() at each stage. Nulls that appear from nowhere are almost always chapter 7's alignment or a failed join; nulls that vanish are a fill you did not intend.
    4. Recompute the total from the components
    If a rate looks wrong, sum the numerator and denominator and divide once. Chapter 15's averaged ratio is the most common single arithmetic error in analysis.
    5. Find one row and follow it
    Pick a single identifier and trace it through every step, printing it each time. Slow, unglamorous, and it localises the problem to one operation more reliably than any amount of aggregate inspection.
    6. Plot it
    Chapter 16's five plots. Spikes at round numbers, two populations, a floor at zero, and impossible values are all invisible in summary statistics and obvious in a histogram.
    7. Halve the pipeline
    If the answer is wrong at the end and right at the start, bisect. Check the middle step, then the middle of whichever half is wrong. Twelve steps takes four checks rather than twelve.
    The question that shortcuts all seven

    What is the last point at which you know the number was right? Usually the answer is "the raw file", and then the routine is a bisection between there and the output. Usually the answer is not "I have no idea", and the ten seconds spent asking saves the whole search.

    And the corollary: if you cannot answer it, you have found the real problem, which is that nothing in the pipeline checks itself. Chapter 21's three cheap assertions turn every subsequent debugging session from a search into a lookup.

    The one thing every result has in common

    None of them raised an error. Every single failure in that table produced a plausible number, a clean-looking column, or a chart that a reasonable person would present — which is why the defences throughout this unit are checks you add rather than exceptions you handle.

    If there is one habit to take from twenty-two chapters: after every step, assert something you believe about the result. The row count, the dtype, a total that must reconcile, a share that must sum to one. Chapter 21 measured that three such assertions catch eight of twelve real failure modes, at a fraction of the cost of the unit tests most people write instead.

    The most useful practice

    Take an analysis you have already done and add chapter 21's assertions to it. If any of them fires, you have found something — and that is a better use of an afternoon than a new dataset.

    The unit in eight sentences

    1. Python's defaults are not neutral. Floats are inexact, mutable defaults persist, and a column read as text sorts lexicographically while looking entirely fine.
    2. Vectorised operations are not a style preference. Row-at-a-time iteration measured 2,000 times slower, and the ratio held at every size.
    3. The index is what makes Pandas Pandas, and it is the source of both automatic alignment and the two quietest bugs in the unit.
    4. Getting data in is where most defects are created — by an inferred dtype, a guessed date format or a duplicated join key, never by an exception.
    5. Cleaning is a sequence of decisions the defaults make for you. Missing values, outliers and types each change the answer, and each looks like no decision at all.
    6. Summary statistics hide shape; plots hide their parameters. Six datasets shared a mean and standard deviation, and one histogram told three different stories.
    7. Almost nothing in this unit failed loudly. Not one measured failure raised an error, which is why the defence is an assertion rather than a try block.
    8. Four cheap assertions catch nine of twelve real failures. The last two are judgement, and only another person reading the work will find them.
