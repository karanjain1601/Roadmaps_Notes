Making it repeatable

    WHY IT MATTERS

    The arithmetic of silent failure

    A crash is free to detect. Everything this unit has measured is the other kind, and the other kind compounds.

    Every failure in Parts 3 to 5 produced a plausible number rather than an error: the swapped dates, the merged identifiers, the inflated total, the assignment that did nothing. An analysis is a chain of steps, and each step has some chance of going quietly wrong.

    A chain of steps, each of which can be quietly wrong and what checking a fraction of them buys
    RUNS THAT ARE CORRECT0%
    WRONG, AND BELIEVED RIGHT0%
    OVER 250 RUNS A YEAR0wrong answers shipped
    COVERAGE NEEDED FOR 1%0%

    Three results, and the third is the one that changes what you do.

    1. With no checks, 30.6% of runs are wrong and look fine. Twelve steps at 3% each gives 0.9712 = 69.4% correct. Nobody would accept a three-in-ten error rate stated that way, and it is the default.
    2. Catching four silent errors in five still leaves 7.0% wrong. Good discipline is not enough on its own, because the failures multiply across steps and 80% coverage only divides the per-step rate by five.
    3. The step count is as strong a lever as the check quality, and usually easier to move. At 80% coverage, three steps gives 1.8% wrong and fifty gives 26.0%. Fewer, larger, verified steps beat many small ones.
    What the target implies

    To keep "wrong but believed right" under 1%, a twelve-step analysis needs 97.2% of its silent errors caught. Three steps needs 88.9%. Those are demanding numbers, and they are not reachable by being careful — carefulness is roughly what produces the 3% per-step rate in the first place.

    Which is the argument for the rest of this chapter. Structure, pinning and automated checks are not process for its own sake; they are the only things that move a number that care alone cannot.

    LAYOUT

    A project structure that survives six months

    Not a framework. A convention, so that "where does this go" and "which file made this" both have obvious answers.

    layout
    project/
  data/
    raw/            # exactly as received. NEVER edited, never written to
    interim/        # parsed and typed, the output of step 1
    processed/      # analysis-ready, the input to the notebooks
  src/
    load.py         # reading and typing — the SCHEMA lives here
    clean.py        # the decisions from Parts 4 and 5
    features.py
  notebooks/
    01-explore.ipynb
    02-churn-analysis.ipynb
  tests/
  outputs/          # figures and tables, all regenerable
  README.md
  requirements.txt  # or pyproject.toml, with versions pinned
    data/raw is immutable
    The single most valuable convention here. Once you have edited the raw file there is no way back to what you were given, and no way to tell whether a surprising number came from the source or from your edit. Read it, never write it.
    Three data stages, and the boundary that matters
    Raw → interim → processed. The interim step is where chapter 9's awkward parse happens exactly once: encoding, dtypes, dates. Everything downstream reads typed Parquet and none of it repeats that work.
    Everything in outputs is disposable
    If deleting the outputs directory loses work, something in there is not reproducible — and that is worth knowing now rather than when somebody asks where a chart came from.
    The README answers four questions
    What this analysis is for, how to run it, where the data comes from, and what is known to be wrong with it. Four short paragraphs, and it is the difference between a project someone else can pick up and one they will rewrite.

    THE REAL TENSION

    Notebooks and modules

    Notebooks are the right tool for exploration and a poor one for anything that runs twice. The resolution is not to abandon them.

    What notebooks are good at

    • Seeing intermediate results immediately
    • Plots next to the code that made them
    • Narrative alongside analysis
    • Trying something in ten seconds

    These are genuine and not replaceable by a script.

    What they are bad at

    • Hidden state — chapter 1 measured 96 of 120 execution orders erroring and 20 producing cleanly wrong output
    • Diffing and reviewing
    • Reuse across analyses
    • Testing
    The split that works

    Logic in modules, narrative in notebooks. Anything you would write twice, anything with a subtle rule, and anything you would want to test goes in src/. The notebook imports it, calls it, and shows the result.

    That single change fixes most of the list above at once: the logic becomes diffable, reviewable, reusable and testable, while the notebook keeps everything it was good at. And it makes the next demo possible, because you cannot unit-test a cell.

    Two supporting habits: %load_ext autoreload and %autoreload 2 so edits to the module appear without restarting, and restart-and-run-all before believing any result. If it does not survive that, the result depends on state you cannot see.

    notebook_pattern.py
    %load_ext autoreload
%autoreload 2

from src.load import load_orders          # the schema and dates live in here
from src.clean import apply_exclusions    # and the decisions from Parts 4 and 5

df = load_orders("data/interim/orders.parquet")
df = apply_exclusions(df, min_date="2024-01-01")

df.groupby("region")["revenue"].sum().sort_values().plot.barh()

    One tool worth knowing: jupytext pairs each notebook with a plain .py file that stays in sync. You get diffs and code review on the Python file while working in the notebook, which removes the usual objection to keeping notebooks in version control at all.

    WHAT ACTUALLY CATCHES THINGS

    Which tests catch which failures

    Twelve failure modes this unit has measured, seven kinds of check, and a result that contradicts where most people start.

    Coverage against cost the twelve failures are the ones measured in earlier chapters
    FAILURES CAUGHT0of 12 measured in this unit
    COST0relative effort to set up
    CAUGHT PER UNIT OF COST0value for effort
    STILL MISSED0

    Coverage as checks are added, cheapest first
    Checks in placeCostCaught
    Nothing00 / 12
    Schema assertion13 / 12
    + row-count assertion24 / 12
    + invariant checks46 / 12
    + distribution checks69 / 12
    + golden-output test910 / 12
    + unit tests1310 / 12 — no change
    + code review1812 / 12

    Three findings, and the first is uncomfortable for anyone who reaches for pytest first.

    1. Unit tests alone catch 1 of 12, at a cost of 4. Schema, row-count and invariant assertions catch 6 for the same cost of 4, and adding distribution checks reaches 9 for a cost of 6. Analysis failures are overwhelmingly about the data rather than about function logic, and a unit test asserts things about logic.
    2. Distribution checks are the underrated one. Adding them takes coverage from 6 to 9, because they are the only automated check that notices a swapped date column, a collapsed identifier count or a shrunken variance — all of which leave the schema and the row count intact.
    3. Automation tops out at 10 of 12. The remaining two are choosing the outlier rule after seeing the result and ranking a segment table by a noisy rate. Both are judgement, both produce technically valid code, and only review catches them.
    checks.py
    def load_orders(path):
    df = pd.read_parquet(path)

    # schema: cheapest check, catches the most per line
    expected = {"order_id": "string", "revenue": "float64", "ordered_at": "datetime64[ns]"}
    for col, dt in expected.items():
        assert col in df.columns, f"missing column {col}"
        assert str(df[col].dtype) == dt, f"{col} is {df[col].dtype}, expected {dt}"

    # distribution: catches what schema cannot
    assert df["ordered_at"].dt.day.max() > 12,  "dates look day/month swapped"
    assert df["order_id"].nunique() > 0.99 * len(df), "identifiers have collapsed"
    assert df["revenue"].between(0, 1e7).all(), "revenue outside a plausible range"
    assert df["revenue"].isna().mean() < 0.02, "unexpected missing revenue"

    return df

# row count, around every join
before = len(orders)
merged = orders.merge(customers, on="customer_id", how="left", validate="m:1")
assert len(merged) == before, "the join changed the row count"

# invariant, after any share calculation
assert np.allclose(df.groupby("region")["share"].sum(), 1.0), "shares do not sum to 1"
    The golden-output test, which is one line and catches ten of the twelve

    Save a small fixed input and the output your pipeline currently produces. On every change, run the pipeline on that input and compare. Anything that alters the answer shows up immediately, including all the failures above except the two judgement ones.

    pd.testing.assert_frame_equal(pipeline(fixture), pd.read_parquet("tests/expected.parquet"))

    The discipline it requires is that when the output legitimately changes, you look at the diff and update the fixture deliberately. A golden test that gets updated without being read is worse than none, because it launders changes as approvals.

    THE THREE THINGS TO PIN

    Environment, data, parameters

    "It gave a different number this time" is nearly always one of these three, and each has a cheap fix.

    The environment
    Pin versions, not ranges: pandas==2.2.1 rather than pandas>=2. Chapter 1 measured how fast conflicts arrive across projects, and chapter 14 found a pandas default that changed between major versions and silently alters a row count. uv pip freeze or a lockfile, committed.
    The data
    A query against a live table returns different rows tomorrow. Either snapshot the extract with a date in its filename, or record the exact query and the timestamp it ran. "Which data" is the first question of any dispute about a number.
    The parameters
    Thresholds, date ranges, exclusion rules and seeds, in one place at the top of the module rather than scattered as literals. That makes them reviewable, and it makes "what did we run last time" answerable.
    params.py
    from dataclasses import dataclass, asdict
import json, subprocess, datetime

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Params:
    start: str = "2024-01-01"
    end: str = "2024-06-30"
    min_orders: int = 3
    outlier_pct: float = 0.995      # stated in advance, per chapter 13
    seed: int = 42

def record(params, path="outputs/run.json"):
    """Written beside every output, so a number can be traced back."""
    meta = {
        "params": asdict(params),
        "run_at": datetime.datetime.now().isoformat(timespec="seconds"),
        "git": subprocess.run(["git", "rev-parse", "--short", "HEAD"],
                              capture_output=True, text=True).stdout.strip(),
        "pandas": pd.__version__,
        "rows_in": len(df),
    }
    json.dump(meta, open(path, "w"), indent=2)
    A seed is necessary and not sufficient

    Chapter 6 measured this precisely: on a shared global stream, inserting three unrelated draws changed all five downstream values, while a per-analysis generator changed none. So np.random.default_rng(seed) passed explicitly, rather than np.random.seed() at the top.

    And even then, the same seed can give different numbers under a different library version — which is why the version and the seed have to be recorded together, and why the record function above writes both.

    THE TEST THAT MATTERS

    Handover

    Whether someone else can run and change this, without you.

  1. Can a colleague clone it and get the same numbers? The strongest single test of everything above, and it takes an hour to try. Most projects fail on an undocumented data path or an unpinned version.
  2. Is every output regenerable from committed code? If a chart in a deck cannot be traced to a script, it will be wrong eventually and nobody will know when it became wrong.
  3. Are the decisions written down where the code is? Which rows were excluded and why, per chapter 13. In the module, next to the code that does it — not in a message thread.
  4. Does the README say what is known to be wrong? Every analysis has caveats the author holds in their head. Writing them down is the difference between a limitation and a landmine.
  5. Would you understand it in six months? The realistic version of the handover question, since the person picking this up is usually you.
    6. The proportionate amount of this

    A one-off answer for one person needs none of it — a notebook and a clear message is correct, and building infrastructure around it is waste. A number that goes into a monthly report, or that someone will act on, or that you will be asked to explain, needs all of it.

    The failure mode is not choosing wrong at the start; it is never revisiting the choice. Most permanent analyses began as one-off notebooks, and the moment to add structure is the second time someone asks for the number — which is exactly when it feels least urgent.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Twelve steps at 3% silent error each gives 69.4% correct — so 30.6% of runs are wrong and look fine.
    2. Catching four silent errors in five still leaves 7.0% wrong. Discipline alone does not reach an acceptable number.
    3. Step count is as strong a lever as check quality: at 80% coverage, 3 steps gives 1.8% wrong and 50 gives 26.0%.
    4. A 1% target needs 97.2% of silent errors caught at twelve steps. That is not reachable by care.
    5. data/raw is immutable. Once edited, there is no way to tell whether a surprising number is yours or the source's.
    6. Do the awkward parse once, into typed Parquet, and let everything downstream read that.
    7. Logic in modules, narrative in notebooks. That makes it diffable, reviewable, reusable and testable at once.
    8. Restart-and-run-all before believing any result. Chapter 1 measured 20 of 120 execution orders producing cleanly wrong output.
    9. Unit tests alone catch 1 of 12 failures at a cost of 4; three assertions costing exactly the same catch 6, and four reach 9.
    10. Distribution checks take coverage from 6 to 9 — they are the only automated check that sees a swapped date column or a collapsed identifier count.
    11. Automation tops out at 10 of 12. The last two are judgement failures and only review catches them.
    12. A golden-output test catches ten of the twelve for one line, provided you read the diff before updating the fixture.
    13. Pin the environment, the data and the parameters. "It gave a different number" is almost always one of the three.
    14. A seed is necessary and not sufficient: use a per-analysis generator, and record the library version beside it.
    15. The handover test is whether a colleague can clone it and get the same numbers. An hour to try, and most projects fail it.
    16. Add structure the second time someone asks for the number, which is when it feels least urgent.
