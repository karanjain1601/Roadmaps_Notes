CHAPTER 12

Missing data

Filling missing values with the column mean is the most common cleaning step in analysis, and it is not neutral. It preserves the mean exactly, which is why people trust it — while shrinking the standard deviation by 1.61 and pulling the correlation down by 0.08. This chapter measures what each strategy costs against a known truth.

    FOUR KINDS OF NOTHING

    NaN, None, NA and NaT

    Pandas has several missing values for historical reasons, and knowing which you have determines which tests work.

    The missing markers, and where each appears
    MarkerTypeAppears inNote
    np.nanA floatFloat columnsNot equal to itself, and truthy
    NonePython objectobject columnsOften coerced to NaN on read
    pd.NAPandas singletonNullable dtypes: Int64, boolean, stringPropagates through comparisons rather than being False
    pd.NaTDatetimedatetime64, timedelta64The NaN of time
    pd.isna() is the only test worth learning

    It handles all four. x == np.nan is always False, because NaN is not equal to itself — chapter 2's result. x is None misses NaN. np.isnan raises on object and integer arrays.

    And the trap worth repeating from chapter 2: astype(str) turns NaN into the four-character string "nan", after which isna() reports the column as complete. A missing-value count taken after that cast is wrong and will agree with itself every time you check.

    One genuinely useful difference: the nullable dtypes propagate. With Int64, a comparison against a missing value gives pd.NA rather than False — so df[df["count"] > 5] does not silently treat missing as "not greater than 5". That is more correct and it will surprise you the first time a filter returns fewer rows than expected, which is the point.

    THE QUESTION FIRST

    Why is it missing?

    Three mechanisms, and they need different handling. Reaching for fillna before asking this is the mistake the whole chapter is about.

    Missing completely at random
    The missingness has nothing to do with the data. A sensor dropped packets; a form field was optional and people ignored it arbitrarily. Dropping rows is unbiased here — you lose precision and not accuracy.
    Missing at random, given what you observe
    The missingness depends on other columns you do have. Older records lack a field added later; one region's system did not collect it. Dropping is biased, and filling conditioned on the other columns is defensible — a per-region mean rather than a global one.
    Missing not at random
    The missingness depends on the missing value itself. High earners decline to state income; failing schools do not submit results; broken sensors report nothing precisely when readings are extreme. Nothing in your data can fix this, and every strategy is biased. The honest response is to say so.
    How to tell which you have, in three lines

    Compare the rows with a missing value against the rows without, on every other column:

    df.groupby(df["income"].isna()).mean(numeric_only=True)

    If the two groups look alike, missingness is plausibly random. If they differ systematically — one group is younger, or from one region, or has higher balances — it is not random, and you have just learned which columns to condition on. This is five seconds of work and it decides everything that follows.

    Three things worth taking from that table.

    1. Mean fill preserves the mean exactly and that is why it is dangerous. The statistic people check is untouched, so the step looks free. The standard deviation drops by 1.61 and the correlation by 0.08, because every filled row now sits precisely at the centre with no variation and no relationship to anything.
    2. Forward fill has the best standard deviation and the worst correlation. It preserves the spread — the filled values are real observations — while attaching each to the wrong row, which destroys the relationship more thoroughly than mean-filling does. If you are about to correlate or model, this is the worst of the five.
    3. Zero fill is wrong on every measure and it is startlingly common, because fillna(0) is the shortest thing to type. It moves the mean by 9.91 and inflates the standard deviation by 9.07, since it invents a cluster of values ten units below anything real.
    Under MNAR, every strategy is biased and dropping is not safe

    Switch the mechanism to not-at-random. Now 32% of values are missing, concentrated among the high ones, and dropping the rows moves the mean by −7.37 — from 50.17 to 42.80. Mean-filling moves it identically, because it fills with a mean that is already wrong.

    There is no cleaning step that recovers this, and pretending otherwise is worse than acknowledging it. The honest options are to report the analysis on the subset you have while stating clearly what is missing and in which direction it biases the result, or to obtain the missing data. A confident number from MNAR data is a confident wrong number.

    THE ONE WITH A LEGITIMATE USE

    Forward fill

    The right answer for one specific shape of data, and a silent error everywhere else.

    Forward fill carries the previous value forward. That is correct when the data represents a state that persists and is only recorded on change — a price that holds until it moves, a configuration value, a daily reading where a missing day means "unchanged". In that case the missing value genuinely is the previous one.

    ffill.py
    # correct: a state that persists, on a sorted time index
prices = prices.sort_index().ffill()

# and bound it, or one gap fills the rest of the series
prices = prices.ffill(limit=3)          # at most three periods

# per group, or one entity's value leaks into the next
df["price"] = df.sort_values("date").groupby("ticker")["price"].ffill()

# interpolation, where the value genuinely varies continuously
df["temp"] = df["temp"].interpolate(method="time")
    Three ways forward fill goes wrong quietly

    Unsorted data. ffill operates in row order, not date order. On an unsorted frame it carries values forward through time in whatever sequence the rows happen to sit, which is meaningless and produces no error.

    Across groups. Without groupby, the last value of one customer fills the first missing value of the next. Every group boundary is a potential wrong number, and there is nothing to see.

    Unbounded. A single gap at the start of a long quiet period fills every subsequent row with one stale observation. limit= turns that into a decision rather than an accident.

    And note what the measurement above showed: even used on a sorted series, forward fill was the worst of the five strategies for correlation, at 0.604 against a true 0.747. It preserves the marginal distribution beautifully and scrambles the joint one. If the next step is a correlation, a regression or a model, that trade is the wrong way round.

    THE CASE FOR KEEPING IT

    When the missingness is the signal

    Sometimes the most predictive thing about a row is that a value is absent. Filling it deletes that information permanently.

    Thirty thousand credit applications. Income is optional, and about a third decline to state it. The demo measures what that absence tells you.

    The measured difference is large: applicants who declared an income default at 5.83%, and those who did not at 26.66% — a gap of 20.83 percentage points, or 4.58 times the rate. Declining to answer is one of the strongest predictors available.

    Filling the column destroys all of it

    Fill the missing incomes with the mean and the two groups become indistinguishable in that column: 9,527 rows now carry a plausible middling income and nothing anywhere in the data marks them out. A 20.8-point difference in outcome has been deleted by a cleaning step, and nothing warns you.

    The fix costs one column. df["income_missing"] = df["income"].isna() before filling preserves every bit of it, and it is the habit worth building: record the missingness before you remove it. Then filling is safe, because the information has been moved rather than lost.

    Two notes on this. Adding an indicator is standard practice in modelling and rare in exploratory analysis, where it is just as useful — a groupby on the indicator is often the most informative table in a first pass. And be alert to the reverse case: if the indicator turns out to be extremely predictive, check whether it encodes something that will not be available at decision time, which is a leakage question rather than a cleaning one.

    THE ROUTINE

    Six steps, in order

    Each takes one line, and doing them in this order stops most of the chapter happening to you.

  1. Count it, per column. df.isna().sum().sort_values(ascending=False). A column that is 90% missing is a different problem from one that is 2% missing, and they need different decisions.
  2. Look at the pattern, not just the total. df.isna().sum(axis=1).value_counts() tells you whether missingness is spread across rows or concentrated in a few. Rows missing eleven of twelve fields are usually a load artefact rather than data.
  3. Compare the groups. df.groupby(df["x"].isna()).mean(numeric_only=True). This is the mechanism test from earlier, and it decides everything after it.
  4. Record the missingness before removing it. An indicator column, per the previous section, if the absence might mean anything.
  5. Choose deliberately, and write down why. Not fillna(0) because it was shortest. The demo priced each option; pick the one whose cost you can live with, and say so in a comment.
  6. Report what you did. "Excluding 4,231 rows with no recorded income, which skew towards higher balances" is a defensible sentence. Silence about it is not.
    7. The strategy this chapter has not mentioned

    Leave it missing. Pandas skips missing values in nearly every aggregation by default, so df["x"].mean() already gives you the mean of what is present without you filling anything. If the next step is a summary statistic or a plot, filling is often work that only adds bias.

    Filling is required when a downstream tool cannot accept nulls — most scikit-learn estimators, some plotting paths, a database column with a not-null constraint. That is a real requirement, and it is worth checking that you actually have it before imputing.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Four missing markers — NaN, None, NA, NaT — and pd.isna() is the only test that handles all of them.
    2. astype(str) turns NaN into "nan", after which the column reports as complete.
    3. Ask why it is missing before choosing what to do. df.groupby(df["x"].isna()).mean() answers it in one line.
    4. Mean fill preserves the mean exactly, which is why it looks free — and shrinks the standard deviation by 1.61 and the correlation by 0.08.
    5. Forward fill has the best standard deviation and the worst correlation: 0.604 against a true 0.747. It preserves the marginal distribution and scrambles the joint one.
    6. Zero fill is wrong on every measure — the mean off by 9.91, the spread inflated by 9.07 — and it is common because it is short to type.
    7. Dropping is unbiased under MCAR and costs only precision.
    8. Under MNAR, every strategy is biased. Dropping moved the mean by −7.37, from 50.17 to 42.80, and no cleaning step recovers it.
    9. Forward fill needs sorting, grouping and a limit. Unsorted it fills through time in row order; ungrouped it leaks across entities; unbounded one gap fills a whole quiet period.
    10. Missingness can be the strongest signal you have: 5.83% against 26.66% default rate, a 20.83-point gap.
    11. Filling deletes that permanently. Record it first: df["x_missing"] = df["x"].isna() costs one column.
    12. Six steps: count, look at the pattern, compare the groups, record the missingness, choose deliberately, report it.
    13. Leaving it missing is a strategy. Pandas already skips nulls in aggregations; filling is required only when a downstream tool cannot accept them.
