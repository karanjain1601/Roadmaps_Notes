    Three things follow from that table.

    1. The mean and standard deviation carry almost no information about shape. They are identical across a bell curve, a uniform block, two separate populations, a distribution with a tail reaching 374, and a column containing two distinct numbers.
    2. The IQR separates them where the standard deviation cannot. It ranges from 0.26 to 30.00 — a factor of 114 — across datasets whose standard deviations are all 15.00. That is because the standard deviation is dominated by the tail and the IQR describes the middle half.
    3. No single extra statistic is enough. Kurtosis distinguishes the bell curve from the two-population case; the median distinguishes the two-value column; skew catches the tails. Different pairs need different statistics, and the pair-comparison in the demo shows several that only one of the four separates.
    What to look at instead

    df.describe() gives count, mean, std, min, 25%, 50%, 75% and max — which is better than most people use, because the three quartiles carry the shape information the mean and standard deviation do not.

    Add more percentiles and it gets better still: df.describe(percentiles=[.01, .05, .25, .5, .75, .95, .99]). Seven points on the distribution catch a heavy tail, a floor at zero, and a spike, and it costs one argument.

    And then plot it, which is chapter 16. No set of summary numbers reveals two populations as immediately as a histogram does, and the two-population row above has a skew of 0.01 against the bell curve's 0.00, so the statistic people reach for to detect shape says nothing at all about it.

    THREE DIFFERENT QUESTIONS

    Mean, median, mode

    They are not three estimates of the same thing. They answer different questions, and on skewed data they diverge.

    The mean answers: if the total were shared equally, what would each get?
    It is the right statistic when the total is what matters — revenue per customer where you care about the revenue, load per server where you care about the load. It is sensitive to every value, which is a feature when you are reasoning about totals and a problem otherwise.
    The median answers: what is a typical case?
    Half above, half below. This is what people usually mean by "average" in conversation, and it is the right statistic for describing a typical experience — typical income, typical page load, typical order.
    The mode answers: what happens most often?
    Rarely useful for continuous data and the only sensible choice for a category. Also the statistic that reveals a floor or a default value: if 40% of a column is exactly zero, the mode tells you and the mean and median may not.
    The gap between mean and median is a measurement

    On the right-skewed dataset above, the mean is 50.00 and the median 45.65 — the mean sits above the median because the tail pulls it. That gap is a quick, robust skew indicator, and its direction tells you which way.

    Report both when they differ, and say what the difference means: "mean order value £340, median £115" tells the reader immediately that a small number of large orders dominate the total, which is a finding rather than a caveat.

    One case where the mean is simply wrong: averaging rates and ratios. The mean of three conversion rates is not the overall conversion rate unless the three groups are the same size. That is the last section of this chapter, and it is a more common error than anything else here.

    HOW SPREAD OUT

    Spread, and why the standard deviation is hard to use

    Four measures, and the most popular one is the hardest to interpret without an assumption most data does not satisfy.

    Measures of spread
    MeasureWhat it isBreakdown pointWhen to use it
    Standard deviationRoot mean squared deviation0%Symmetric data; anything feeding a normal-theory test
    IQR75th minus 25th percentile25%The default for describing spread
    MADMedian absolute deviation50%Outlier detection, per chapter 13
    RangeMax minus min0%Almost never — it grows with sample size
    "Two standard deviations covers 95%" is only true for a normal distribution

    It is the single most commonly misapplied fact in descriptive statistics. On the right-skewed dataset above, mean plus two standard deviations is 80 — and the maximum is 374. The interval describes almost nothing about where the data actually is.

    Percentiles make no assumption at all. s.quantile([.05, .95]) tells you where 90% of the values are, whatever the shape, and it is the honest way to state a range. The standard deviation earns its place in inference, where the central limit theorem does the work; as a description of a skewed column it is close to meaningless.

    A note on coefficient of variation — the standard deviation divided by the mean. It is useful for comparing spread across quantities on different scales, and it is meaningless when the mean is near zero or the data can be negative, because the denominator stops being a stable reference. If you see a CV above about 1, check whether the mean is a sensible thing to divide by before believing the number.

    TWO MORE NUMBERS

    Skewness and kurtosis

    Useful as flags rather than as quantities. Both are extremely sensitive to the tail, which is a problem given that describing the tail is what they are for.

    Skewness

    Positive means a long right tail: income, revenue, session length, file size. Negative means a long left tail: exam scores near a ceiling, ages at retirement.

    Rough reading: below 0.5 is near-symmetric, above 1 is substantially skewed, and above 2 means the mean is not describing a typical case. The skewed dataset above scores 5.30.

    Kurtosis

    Not "peakedness" — that is the common wrong gloss. It measures tail weight. Pandas reports excess kurtosis, so 0 is normal-like, positive means heavier tails than normal, negative means lighter.

    Negative kurtosis is the useful signal people miss: the uniform dataset scores −1.19 and the two-population one −1.77, both indicating less tail than a bell curve, which is what a flat or split distribution looks like.

    Use them to decide what to look at, not to report

    Both statistics involve cubes and fourth powers of deviations, so a single extreme value moves them a great deal — the breakdown point is 0% and worse in practice than for the mean. A kurtosis of 67.71, as on the skewed dataset, is mostly telling you that one value is very far out.

    So the honest use is as a triage flag: high absolute skew means look at a log scale, high kurtosis means look at the tail, negative kurtosis means look for two populations. All three of those are instructions to plot something, which is the next chapter.

    THE REVERSAL

    Simpson's paradox

    A provider better at easy cases and better at hard cases, with a worse overall record. Not a trick — arithmetic, and it happens in real comparisons constantly.

    A OVERALL0%
    B OVERALL0%
    BETTER ON EASY / HARD—
    REVERSAL—

    At a 90/20 case mix the numbers are unambiguous and contradictory:

    Provider B is better at both kinds of case and worse overall
    Easy casesHard casesOverall
    Provider A97.0% of 90068.0% of 10094.1%
    Provider B99.0% of 20075.0% of 80079.8%
    Who is betterBBA

    Nothing is wrong with any of those six numbers. A's overall rate is high because 90% of A's cases are easy ones; B's is low because 80% of B's are hard. The overall figures compare different populations and describe the case mix rather than the providers.

    What to do about it, and what not to

    The paradox is not a reason to distrust aggregates. It is a reason to ask what varies between the groups you are comparing. Here the answer is case difficulty; in an A/B test it is often traffic source or device; in a regional comparison it is usually customer mix.

    Three defences. Segment by anything plausibly related to both the grouping and the outcome — and note that the confounder has to affect both, so a short list of candidates usually covers it. Check whether group sizes are wildly unequal within segments, since that imbalance is what drives the reversal. And report the segments alongside the total, which is the honest presentation and prevents the reader drawing the reversed conclusion.

    The demo also shows where it stops: holding A at 90% easy cases, the reversal survives until B reaches 80% easy. With identical case mixes it disappears entirely and B leads overall as well — which is the point. The reversal is caused by the imbalance, not by anything about the providers.

    simpson.py
    # the aggregate
df.groupby("provider")["survived"].mean()

# and immediately, the segments
df.groupby(["provider", "difficulty"]).agg(
    rate=("survived", "mean"), n=("survived", "size"))

# the imbalance that causes it, in one line
pd.crosstab(df["provider"], df["difficulty"], normalize="index")

    THE COMMON ERROR

    Averaging averages

    The mean of group means is not the overall mean unless the groups are the same size. This is the most frequent numerical mistake in analysis.

    weighted.py
    # WRONG: every region counted equally, regardless of size
df.groupby("region")["conversion_rate"].mean()

# RIGHT: recompute from the components
df.groupby("region").apply(
    lambda g: g["conversions"].sum() / g["visits"].sum())

# or, overall
df["conversions"].sum() / df["visits"].sum()

# a weighted mean where you only have the rates and the sizes
np.average(rates, weights=sizes)
    The rule: never average a ratio

    A conversion rate, a click-through rate, a cost per unit, an error rate and a margin are all ratios. To aggregate them, sum the numerators and sum the denominators, then divide once. Averaging the ratios weights a region with 40 visits equally with one that has 400,000.

    The tell that you have done it: the aggregate does not lie between the smallest and largest component in the way you expect, or it changes when you split the data more finely. If a number moves when you regroup without changing anything else, it is being averaged rather than recomputed.

    Two related traps. Medians do not aggregate at all — there is no way to combine group medians into an overall median, and the mean of them is not it. If you need an overall median you need the underlying values, which is the awkward case for the chunked processing of chapter 9. And percentages of percentages compound: a 10% rise followed by a 10% fall is a 1% net loss, not a return to where you started.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Six datasets shared a mean of 50.00 and a standard deviation of 15.00 while being a bell curve, a uniform block, two populations, a heavy tail reaching 374, a spike, and a two-value column.
    2. Their IQRs ranged from 0.26 to 30.00 — a factor of 114 — because the standard deviation follows the tail and the IQR describes the middle half.
    3. No single extra statistic separates every pair. Kurtosis catches two populations, the median catches the two-value column, skew catches the tails.
    4. Use describe(percentiles=[.01,.05,.25,.5,.75,.95,.99]) — seven points on the distribution for one argument.
    5. Mean, median and mode answer different questions: share the total, describe a typical case, find what happens most.
    6. The gap between mean and median is a robust skew indicator, and reporting both is a finding rather than a caveat.
    7. "Two standard deviations covers 95%" needs a normal distribution. On the skewed dataset, mean plus two sd is 80 and the maximum is 374.
    8. Use percentiles to state a range; they assume nothing about shape.
    9. Kurtosis is tail weight, not peakedness, and negative kurtosis is the underused signal — the two-population dataset scores −1.77.
    10. Skew and kurtosis are triage flags, not quantities to report. A kurtosis of 67.71 mostly says one value is very far out.
    11. Simpson's paradox: B was better on easy cases (99% vs 97%) and hard ones (75% vs 68%), and worse overall (79.8% vs 94.1%).
    12. The reversal is caused by the imbalance, not the providers. With identical case mixes it disappears; holding A at 90% easy it survives until B reaches 80%.
    13. Segment by anything related to both the grouping and the outcome, and report segments alongside the total.
    14. Never average a ratio. Sum the numerators, sum the denominators, divide once.
    15. Medians do not aggregate at all, and percentage changes compound — up 10% then down 10% is a 1% loss.

    Take any groupby that produces a rate and recompute it by summing the numerator and denominator instead. If the two answers differ, the one in your report is the wrong one.
