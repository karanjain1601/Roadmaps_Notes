    THE PARAMETER NOBODY SETS

    Bin width decides the conclusion

    A histogram has one free parameter and it determines what you see. The default is a guess made by a formula that does not know your data.

    The data is two overlapping populations centred at 35 and 62. What you conclude depends entirely on the bin count:

    And the four standard rules for choosing a bin count give 13, 19, 19 and 29 bins on this data. That looks like disagreement, and the useful observation is that all four land in the range where the two populations are visible. The defaults are not the danger here. The danger is a hand-picked number, and the fix is to try several rather than accepting the first.

    The ECDF has no binning parameter at all

    An empirical cumulative distribution function plots each value against the proportion of data at or below it. Every point appears, nothing is aggregated, and there is nothing to tune — so two people plotting the same data get identical pictures.

    The cost is that it takes practice to read: a mode is a steep section rather than a bump, and two populations show as two steep stretches separated by a flatter one. Switch the demo's view to see the same data as an ECDF.

    The practical habit: look at both. A histogram for intuition, an ECDF to check that the histogram's story survives without a bin width. And when a finding depends on the bin count, say so — that dependence is information about how strong the finding is.

    Ways to look at one distribution

    Each hides something different, which is the argument for using more than one.

    A box plot cannot show two populations

    This is the important entry in that table. A box plot draws five numbers, and chapter 15 showed that a two-population dataset can have a mean, a standard deviation and a near-zero skew identical to a bell curve's. So the two look much the same as boxes, and the finding — that there are two groups — is invisible.

    Box plots are excellent for comparing many groups on one axis, which is what they were designed for. They are a poor way to examine a single distribution, and a violin plot or a strip plot alongside fixes it.

    Two cautions about KDE, because it is the prettiest option and the most quietly wrong. It smooths across genuine gaps, so a bimodal distribution with clear separation can render as one wide hump at the default bandwidth. And it spills past hard boundaries: a duration or a count that cannot be negative gets density assigned below zero, which is visibly impossible and routinely published.

    CHOOSING

    The chart for the question

    Chart choice follows from what you are comparing, and there are only about six cases.

    What you are asking, and what to draw
    The questionThe chartNote
    How is one variable distributed?Histogram + ECDFBoth, per the first section
    How do two numbers relate?ScatterAdd transparency or hexbin when it overplots
    How does something change over time?LineTime on the x-axis, always
    How do categories compare?Bar, sorted by valueNot alphabetically, and not by category code
    How does a distribution differ by group?Violin, or overlaid ECDFsBox plots for many groups, with care
    How do several variables relate?Small multiplesBetter than one crowded chart, nearly always
    Sort bar charts by value

    The commonest easy improvement to an analytical chart. A bar chart in alphabetical order makes the reader do the ranking themselves; sorted by value it answers "which is biggest" instantly. df["x"].value_counts().plot.barh() sorts for you, and horizontal bars leave room for real labels rather than rotated text.

    The exception is an ordinal category — severity, size band, month — where the natural order carries meaning and sorting by value destroys it. Chapter 14's ordered categorical is how you get that order without sorting manually at every use.

    Two chart types to avoid in analytical work. Pie charts ask the reader to compare angles, which people do badly; a sorted bar chart carries the same information and is read accurately. And dual-axis charts, where two series with different units share a plot: the apparent relationship between them depends entirely on where you put the two scales, so the chart can show correlation, anti-correlation or nothing from identical data. Use two stacked panels with a shared x-axis instead.

    THE ARITHMETIC OF DECEPTION

    What a truncated axis does

    Not a question of taste. The exaggeration factor is computable, and it is unbounded.

    Two values, 103 and 100 — a 3% difference. Draw them as bars from a baseline of zero and one is 3% taller. Draw them from a baseline of 99 and one is four times the height of the other. Every number on the chart is correct.

    When truncating an axis is right

    Not never. Line charts of a quantity that never approaches zero — a temperature, a share price, a percentage hovering around 96% — are unreadable from a zero baseline, and truncating shows the variation that is the point of the chart. That is legitimate and standard.

    Bar charts are different, because a bar encodes a quantity by its length. Truncating the axis makes the length stop meaning the quantity, and the reader compares lengths whether or not they read the axis. So: bars from zero, lines wherever is readable, and label the axis clearly either way.

    The general test is whether the visual encoding still matches the data. A bar's length says "this much"; a line's slope says "changing this fast". Truncation breaks the first and preserves the second.

    Three other ways a technically accurate chart misleads, worth recognising in other people's work as much as avoiding in your own:

  1. Plotting means without spread. A bar chart of group means implies the groups differ; the same chart with error bars or an overlaid strip plot frequently shows they overlap almost entirely. Chapter 15's six identical-mean datasets are the extreme version.
  2. Selective time ranges. Starting a trend line at a local minimum makes any series look like growth. The defence is to show the longest range you have, then zoom in as a second panel.
  3. Aggregating away the finding. A monthly average can hide that every Monday is catastrophic. If a pattern exists at a finer grain than you are plotting, the plot cannot show it — which is why plotting at several grains is worth the two extra lines.

    4. THE TOOLS

    Enough matplotlib and seaborn

    One structural idea and about ten calls covers most exploratory plotting.

    plotting.py
    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns

# the structural idea: a Figure holds Axes, and you draw on Axes
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))
axes[0].hist(df["amount"], bins=40)
sns.ecdfplot(df, x="amount", ax=axes[1])
axes[0].set(title="Distribution", xlabel="Amount (£)", ylabel="Orders")
fig.tight_layout()

# seaborn takes a DataFrame and column names, which is usually what you want
sns.histplot(df, x="amount", hue="region", element="step", stat="density")
sns.violinplot(df, x="region", y="amount")
sns.scatterplot(df, x="visits", y="revenue", alpha=0.3)   # alpha for overplotting
sns.relplot(df, x="visits", y="revenue", col="region", col_wrap=3)  # small multiples

# pandas for a quick look, which is often all you need
df["amount"].plot.hist(bins=40)
df.groupby("region")["revenue"].sum().sort_values().plot.barh()
    Figure and Axes
    A Figure is the canvas; an Axes is one plot on it. Almost every confusing matplotlib error is about calling a method on the wrong one. Create both explicitly with plt.subplots and pass ax= to seaborn, and the whole API becomes predictable.
    alpha on any scatter with more than a few thousand points
    Without it, overplotting makes a dense region and a saturated one look identical, so you cannot see where the mass is. alpha=0.2 or hexbin for very large data. This is the single most common fixable defect in an exploratory scatter plot.
    stat="density" when comparing groups of different sizes
    Overlaid counts show you that one group is larger, which you already knew. Densities show whether the shapes differ, which is the actual question.
    Small multiples over one crowded chart
    col= in seaborn's figure-level functions. Four small panels are read more accurately than one plot with four overlapping series, and the code is shorter.

    THE ROUTINE

    Five plots for a new dataset

    In this order, before any analysis. Ten minutes, and it catches most of Parts 4 and 5.

  4. A histogram of every numeric column. df.hist(figsize=(14, 10), bins=40) in one line. You are looking for spikes at round numbers, floors at zero, two humps, and impossible values — the sentinels of chapter 13.
  5. A bar chart of every categorical column's counts. Near-duplicate spellings, an unexpected dominant level, a category with three rows. Chapter 14's whitespace problem is visible here.
  6. A line chart of row counts over time. df.set_index("date").resample("D").size().plot(). Gaps mean missing loads, steps mean a schema change, and a ramp at the start means your history is incomplete.
  7. A scatter of your two most important numbers, with alpha. This is where a data-entry pattern, a cluster or a hard ceiling shows itself.
  8. An ECDF of your key measure. No parameters to tune, and it answers percentile questions directly — "what fraction are under £50" is read straight off the axis.
    9. Why plotting first rather than after

    Every failure this unit has measured is visible in one of those five plots. The identifier collision of chapter 9 shows as a spike where distinct values merged. The swapped dates of chapter 14 show as a month distribution that is wrong in a specific way. The two populations of chapter 15 show as two humps and appear in no summary statistic.

    None of them is visible in df.head(), and all of them change the answer. Ten minutes of plots at the start is the cheapest quality check in the whole unit.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. The same 3,000 values showed 0, 2, 3, 16 or 76 modes at 3, 20, 40, 120 and 400 bins. The bin count determines the conclusion.
    2. The four standard rules gave 13, 19, 19 and 29 bins — and all four landed where the two populations are visible. The defaults are not the danger; a hand-picked number is.
    3. An ECDF has no free parameter, so two people plotting the same data get the same picture. Use both.
    4. When a finding depends on the bin count, say so. That dependence is information about how strong it is.
    5. A box plot cannot show two populations, because it draws five numbers and those five can match a bell curve exactly.
    6. KDE smooths across real gaps and spills past hard boundaries, assigning density below zero to quantities that cannot be negative.
    7. Sort bar charts by value, except for ordinal categories where the natural order means something.
    8. Avoid pie charts and dual axes. A dual axis can show correlation or anti-correlation from identical data, depending on the scales.
    9. A 3% difference looks like 300% from a baseline of 99 — a 3.9× exaggeration, rising to 30× at 99.9 and unbounded beyond.
    10. Bars from zero; lines wherever is readable. A bar's length encodes the quantity, so truncation breaks the encoding. A line's slope encodes change, so it survives.
    11. Plotting means without spread implies differences that may not exist. Add error bars or an overlaid strip plot.
    12. Create Figure and Axes explicitly and pass ax=. Most confusing matplotlib errors are about which object you called.
    13. alpha on any dense scatter, and stat="density" when comparing groups of different sizes.
    14. Five plots for a new dataset: histograms of numerics, bars of categoricals, row counts over time, a scatter of the key pair, an ECDF of the key measure.
    15. Every failure measured in this unit is visible in one of those five, and none of them is visible in head().
