    WHAT IT ACTUALLY MEASURES

    Pearson correlation

    Not "how related are these". Specifically: how well does a straight line describe them. Every failure below follows from that word.

    Four distinct failures in that table, and they are not edge cases.

    1. A perfect relationship can score zero. The parabola and the cosine wave are deterministic — y is exactly a function of x, with no noise at all — and both coefficients report essentially nothing. Pearson measures linearity, and neither is linear.
    2. A perfect monotone relationship scores 0.763. For x⁷, Spearman is exactly 1.000 and Pearson understates it substantially, because the relationship is monotone but curved.
    3. Two clusters give r = 0.942 with no relationship at all inside either. Taken apart, the within-cluster correlations are −0.020 and −0.026. The coefficient is measuring the gap between two groups, and reporting it as a relationship between two variables.
    4. One point can manufacture a correlation. Add a single distant point to four hundred uncorrelated values and r goes from 0.003 to 0.865. Spearman moves from −0.004 to 0.004, because a rank cannot be extreme.
    Which is why a scatter plot comes first, not second

    Every one of those four is obvious in a scatter plot and invisible in a number. The parabola looks like a parabola; the two clusters look like two clusters; the influential point is visibly one point.

    Never report a correlation you have not plotted. It takes one line — sns.scatterplot(df, x=..., y=..., alpha=0.3) — and it is the only way to know which of the seven rows above you are in.

    THE OTHER COEFFICIENTS

    Spearman and Kendall

    Both work on ranks rather than values, which buys robustness and monotone sensitivity and costs the linearity interpretation.

    correlation.py
    df[["a", "b", "c"]].corr()                    # Pearson by default
df[["a", "b", "c"]].corr(method="spearman")   # ranks — robust and monotone
df["a"].corr(df["b"], method="kendall")

# a p-value too, which .corr() does not give you
from scipy.stats import pearsonr, spearmanr
r, p = spearmanr(df["a"], df["b"])

# and the plot, which comes first
sns.pairplot(df[["a", "b", "c"]], plot_kws={"alpha": 0.3})
    Report both, and treat a large gap as a finding

    If Pearson and Spearman agree, the relationship is roughly linear and either number is fine. If they differ substantially, that difference is telling you something specific:

    Spearman much higher — monotone but curved, so a transform (log, rank) will linearise it. Pearson much higher — an outlier or a cluster gap is driving it, so look at the scatter and at the extreme rows. Both cases are more informative than either coefficient alone.

    Two notes on r², since it usually appears next to these. It is the share of variance in y that a linear fit explains, so r = 0.7 explains only 49% — correlations feel stronger than they are. And r² inherits every failure above: the parabola has an r² of zero against a relationship that a quadratic fit would explain perfectly.

    THE OBVIOUS ONE, PRECISELY

    Correlation and causation

    Everyone knows the slogan. The useful version is the list of what else could produce the correlation, because that is what you check.

    You observe that A and B move together. There are six explanations, and "A causes B" is one of them.

    A causes B
    The hypothesis you were hoping for.
    B causes A
    Reverse causation, and more common than it sounds. Support-ticket volume correlates with revenue; the arrow runs the other way from the one people assume.
    A third variable causes both — a confounder
    Ice cream sales and drowning both rise with temperature. The classic, and the one people check for.
    The correlation is an artefact of aggregation
    The two-cluster row above: r = 0.942 across groups and zero within them. Comparing group means when the groups differ in composition, which is chapter 15's Simpson's paradox.
    Selection created it
    The correlation exists in your sample and not in the population, because of how the sample was chosen. The next section, and the least well known of the six.
    Chance
    With enough variables, some pairs correlate. Chapter 18 measures how quickly this becomes likely — at fourteen comparisons it is more probable than not.
    What would change your mind

    The practical discipline is not to avoid causal language but to state what would distinguish the explanations. Does the timing work? A cause precedes its effect, so check whether A moves first. Is there a dose response? More A giving more B is harder to explain by confounding than a simple split. Does it survive within subgroups? If it holds within every segment, aggregation is not the explanation. Can you find a natural experiment? A policy change, a staged rollout, an outage — something that moved A for reasons unrelated to B.

    None of these proves causation. All of them narrow the list, and writing down which you checked is the difference between an analysis and an assertion.

    THE ONE THAT SURPRISES PEOPLE

    Selection can create a correlation from nothing

    Two independent causes of an outcome become correlated as soon as you look only at cases with a high outcome. Not a bias in the data — a consequence of the filter.

    Suppose success is talent plus luck, and the two are entirely independent. Among successful people, they will be negatively correlated — because to be successful without much talent you need luck, and vice versa. The filter itself manufactures the relationship.

    Three variable roles, and only one of them you should control for

    A confounder causes both A and B. Controlling for it removes spurious correlation. This is the one everybody knows, and it is the reason "control for more variables" became a reflex.

    A collider is caused by both A and B — success, in the demo above. Controlling for it creates spurious correlation, exactly as measured. Adding it to a model makes the analysis worse, and it looks like diligence.

    A mediator sits on the path from A to B. Controlling for it removes the very effect you were trying to measure, so a real effect disappears and you conclude there is none.

    So "control for everything available" is wrong, and it is wrong in two different directions. Which variables to condition on depends on how you believe they are causally arranged, and that belief has to come from knowing the domain rather than from the data.

    Where this shows up in ordinary analysis: any dataset that only contains cases that passed a filter. Customers who converted, applications that were approved, papers that were published, users who did not churn, servers that stayed up. In every one of those, two independent predictors of the filter will appear negatively related — and switching the demo's control to select on talent alone shows the contrast, because filtering on a single cause does not produce the effect.

    ONE STEP FURTHER

    Reading a simple regression

    A line through a scatter plot, and the four things its output is telling you.

    regression.py
    import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

m = smf.ols("revenue ~ visits + region", data=df).fit()
print(m.summary())

# the four numbers worth reading, in order:
m.params          # the coefficients: the effect size
m.conf_int()      # the confidence intervals: the precision
m.rsquared        # how much variance is explained
m.pvalues         # whether the coefficient is distinguishable from zero
    The coefficient is the effect size, and it has units
    "£4.20 of revenue per additional visit" is a statement you can check against reality. Read the coefficient before the p-value, always — a coefficient can be statistically significant and too small to matter, which chapter 18 takes apart.
    The confidence interval carries more information than the p-value
    A p-value tells you whether zero is plausible. The interval tells you that and which other values are plausible. "£4.20, interval £0.10 to £8.30" and "£4.20, interval £4.05 to £4.35" have the same p-value and mean quite different things.
    Every coefficient is conditional on the others
    "Holding the other variables constant" is doing real work in that sentence. A coefficient changes — sometimes changes sign — when you add or remove another variable, which is why the collider and mediator distinction above matters for anything you interpret.
    Look at the residuals
    sns.scatterplot(x=m.fittedvalues, y=m.resid). A pattern in that plot means the linear form is wrong — a curve means you need a transform, a fan means the variance grows with the fit, and both make the standard errors optimistic.
    The parabola has an r² of zero

    Which is the same warning as the first demo, arriving at a model. A linear regression on a curved relationship reports no effect and no explained variance, and the fitted-values-against-residuals plot shows an unmistakable arc.

    That plot is the diagnostic worth running every time, because it catches the case where the model is fine, the data is fine, and the form is wrong — which no coefficient or p-value reveals.

    FOUR MORE

    Traps worth naming

  1. Regression to the mean. Select the worst-performing stores, intervene, and they improve — because extreme values are partly noise and noise does not repeat. Any "we targeted the worst and they got better" claim needs a control group, since the improvement would have happened anyway.
  2. Correlating a series with its own components. Total revenue correlates with every revenue component by construction, and the correlation measures the component's share rather than a relationship. The same applies to any ratio correlated with its own numerator or denominator.
  3. Spurious correlation between trending series. Any two quantities that both grow over time correlate strongly, whether or not they are related. Correlate the changes rather than the levels, or include time in the model.
  4. Correlating a variable with itself in disguise. Price and revenue-per-unit, or headcount and total salary, are close to the same measurement twice. High correlations between plausible-sounding variables are worth checking for this before being interpreted.
    5. The one-line check for the trending-series case

    df[["a", "b"]].diff().corr(). If the correlation between the levels is 0.95 and between the first differences is 0.02, the relationship was time and nothing else. Two lines of output, and it settles a question that otherwise generates a great deal of confident writing.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Pearson measures linearity, not relatedness. Every failure below follows from that.
    2. A perfect parabola and a perfect cosine wave both score −0.000, with y exactly determined by x and no noise at all.
    3. A perfect x⁷ scores 0.763 on Pearson and exactly 1.000 on Spearman. Monotone but curved.
    4. Two clusters give r = 0.942 with within-cluster correlations of −0.020 and −0.026. The coefficient measures the gap between groups.
    5. One added point took r from 0.003 to 0.865 on 400 uncorrelated values. Spearman moved to 0.004.
    6. Never report a correlation you have not plotted. All four failures are obvious in a scatter and invisible in a number.
    7. Spearman is the sensible default for exploration; Pearson when you believe it is linear and have checked.
    8. A large Pearson–Spearman gap is a finding: Spearman higher means curved, Pearson higher means an outlier or a cluster gap.
    9. r = 0.7 explains 49% of the variance. Correlations feel stronger than they are.
    10. Six explanations for a correlation, and "A causes B" is one. The others: reverse causation, a confounder, aggregation, selection, chance.
    11. Selection creates correlation from nothing. Talent and luck, independent by design, correlate −0.709 among the top 10% by their sum and −0.838 among the top 1%.
    12. Control for confounders, never for colliders or mediators. "Control for everything available" is wrong in two directions.
    13. Any dataset filtered on an outcome has this problem: converted customers, approved applications, published papers, surviving servers.
    14. Read the coefficient and its interval before the p-value. Two coefficients with the same p-value can mean very different things.
    15. Plot fitted values against residuals. It catches the case where the model and data are fine and the form is wrong.
    16. Two trending series always correlate. df[["a","b"]].diff().corr() settles it in one line.

    Take the strongest correlation in your data and plot it. Then compute Spearman as well. If the two coefficients differ by more than about 0.1, the scatter will show you why.
