CHAPTER 18

Testing and uncertainty

Ten thousand experiments with no real effect produced a p-value distribution that is flat to within 0.6 percentage points, and 5.3% of them were significant. That is what 0.05 means. It does not mean a 5% chance of being wrong — at a realistic hit rate, a third of significant findings are false.

    THE DEFINITION, AND THE SIMULATION

    What a p-value is

    The probability of seeing data at least this extreme if there were no effect. Every misreading comes from moving the conditional to the other side.

    Ten thousand experiments, run two arms of 60, a two-sided test, with the true effect set by you
    REACHED THE THRESHOLD0%
    DISTRIBUTION UNDER THE NULL—
    WHAT THIS IS—
    MISSED—

    Two results from running it, and the first is the definition made concrete.

    1. With no effect at all, 5.3% of experiments were significant at 0.05. Not approximately, not because of a flaw — that is precisely what the threshold specifies. Choosing 0.05 is choosing to be wrong one time in twenty when nothing is happening.
    2. Under the null, the p-value distribution is uniform — flat to within 0.6 percentage points across ten deciles. A p-value from a null experiment is a random number between 0 and 1. It is as likely to be 0.03 as 0.83.
    Four things a p-value is not

    Not the probability the null is true. That would require knowing how often your hypotheses are true before testing, which the p-value cannot know. The next section computes what happens when you supply it.

    Not the probability the result will replicate. A p of 0.04 in one study says little about the next.

    Not a measure of effect size. With enough data, a trivially small effect gives a tiny p-value. Raise the sample size in the demo and watch the significance rate climb with the effect held fixed.

    Not a threshold nature respects. p = 0.049 and p = 0.051 are the same evidence. The line at 0.05 is a convention and treating it as a boundary between true and false is the source of most of the trouble.

    THE NUMBER THAT MATTERS

    How many significant findings are false

    The question people think the p-value answers. Answering it needs two more numbers, and the result is uncomfortable.

    Suppose one hypothesis in ten that you test is genuinely true, and your studies have 80% power. Of a thousand tests: 100 are real and you detect 80 of them; 900 are null and 45 clear p < 0.05. So 125 findings are significant and 45 of them — 36% — are false.

    The false discovery rate at p < 0.05
    Share of hypotheses that are truePowerFalse discovery rate
    50%80%6%
    20%80%20%
    10%80%36%
    10%40%53%
    5%40%70%
    Two things follow, and the second is the actionable one

    Testing implausible hypotheses is expensive. The more speculative your hypotheses, the lower the share that are true, and the higher the proportion of your significant results that are noise. Exploratory testing of many unlikely ideas has a false discovery rate that can exceed half.

    Low power is worse than it looks. An underpowered study does not merely miss real effects — it raises the share of your positive findings that are false, from 36% to 53% in the table above. A small study that finds something is weaker evidence than a large study that finds the same thing, and the usual intuition that a positive result is a positive result gets this wrong.

    THE COMPOUNDING

    Multiple comparisons

    Each test carries a 5% false-positive rate. Run enough of them and a false positive is the expected outcome rather than an unlucky one.

    At fourteen tests it is more likely than not that something looks significant with nothing whatever going on. And fourteen is a small number of tests — the trap is that they are rarely counted.

    The tests you did not know you ran

    Three outcome measures, four subgroups, two time windows is 24 implicit comparisons, and a 70.8% chance of finding something with no effect present. Nobody wrote 24 tests; they looked at the data a few ways.

    The same arithmetic covers: trying several transformations, testing before and after excluding outliers, adding a covariate to see if it helps, and stopping data collection when the result looks good. Each is a defensible individual choice and collectively they are a search.

    The defence is counting rather than not looking. Exploratory analysis is exactly how you find things worth testing. Record how many ways you looked, and treat what you find as a hypothesis to check on new data rather than as a result.

    Bonferroni

    Divide the threshold by the number of tests. Controls the chance of any false positive, which is what you want when a single false claim is costly.

    Conservative, and increasingly so: at 100 tests the threshold is 0.0005.

    Benjamini–Hochberg

    Controls the proportion of your discoveries that are false, rather than the chance of any. Far more powerful for screening many hypotheses.

    statsmodels.stats.multitest.multipletests(pvals, method="fdr_bh"). The right default for exploratory work.

    Correction is not free

    The demo measures the cost. At n = 200 per arm and a true effect of 0.3, power is 85% at a single test and 32% after correcting for 100. So the price of not being fooled by noise is missing two real effects in three.

    Which is the honest framing: correction moves error from one type to the other rather than removing it. The way out is not a cleverer correction — it is fewer, pre-specified tests, or a larger sample, or treating the whole exercise as exploratory and confirming on fresh data.

    BEFORE THE EXPERIMENT

    Power and sample size

    Computing this afterwards is nearly useless. Computing it beforehand tells you whether the experiment is worth running.

    power.py
    from statsmodels.stats.power import TTestIndPower

# how many do I need to detect an effect I would care about?
TTestIndPower().solve_power(effect_size=0.3, power=0.8, alpha=0.05)
# -> 176 per arm

# for a conversion rate, work in the rate's own units
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_effectsize
es = proportion_effectsize(0.11, 0.10)      # 10% -> 11% is a 1-point lift
TTestIndPower().solve_power(effect_size=es, power=0.8, alpha=0.05)
# -> about 14,750 per arm
    The question power analysis actually answers

    Not "is my sample big enough" in the abstract, but: given the traffic I have, what is the smallest effect I could reliably detect? If that number is larger than any effect the change could plausibly produce, the experiment cannot succeed and running it is a way of generating a null result you will misread as evidence of no effect.

    That calculation takes two minutes and regularly saves a fortnight. It also reframes the conversation usefully: "we can detect a 2-point lift and you are hoping for 0.3" is a far more productive thing to say before the test than after.

    One thing to avoid: post-hoc power computed from the observed effect. It is a deterministic function of the p-value and adds nothing — a non-significant result always yields low observed power, which is circular. If you want to know what a null result rules out, report the confidence interval instead, which is the next section.

    WHAT TO REPORT

    Effect sizes and intervals

    The p-value answers a yes-or-no question that is rarely the one being asked.

    Three results, and what each supports
    ResultpReading
    +0.2% [−0.1%, +0.5%]0.19Inconclusive, and the effect is small either way
    +0.2% [+0.02%, +0.38%]0.03Significant, and too small to act on
    +9.0% [−1.0%, +19.0%]0.08Not significant, and possibly very valuable

    The second and third rows are the ones that matter. A statistically significant result can be commercially irrelevant, and a non-significant one can be the most promising thing in the analysis — the third row's interval includes a 19% lift, which is not a result to discard.

    "Not significant" does not mean "no effect"

    It means the data cannot distinguish the effect from zero. Whether that is because there is no effect or because the study was too small to tell is exactly what the confidence interval answers, and the p-value does not.

    A narrow interval around zero is evidence of no meaningful effect. A wide interval around zero is evidence that you learned very little — and reporting the two identically, as "no significant difference", throws away the distinction.

    Report, in this order: the effect in its own units — pounds, percentage points, seconds, not standardised units nobody can picture. The interval. The sample size. And then the p-value if the audience expects it. Reversing that order is how a 0.2% lift becomes a headline.

    The bootstrap deserves the last row more than it usually gets

    Resample the data with replacement a few thousand times, recompute the statistic each time, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. That gives a confidence interval for any statistic — a median, a 95th percentile, a ratio of two medians, a Gini coefficient — with no distributional assumption and no lookup of which test applies.

    It is slower and it is far harder to get wrong. For an analyst rather than a statistician, "bootstrap the thing I actually care about" is usually better advice than choosing between six named tests, and it sidesteps the question of whether your data meets an assumption you were not going to check.

    Two assumptions worth knowing about. Independence is the one that actually breaks results: repeated measurements from the same user, or users within the same account, are not independent, and treating them as such makes the effective sample size far smaller than the row count and the p-values far too small. Normality matters much less than people fear for means, because the central limit theorem does the work — but it matters entirely for small samples and for anything about extremes rather than averages.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. A p-value is the probability of data this extreme if there were no effect. Every misreading moves the conditional to the other side.
    2. With no effect, 5.3% of 10,000 simulated experiments were significant at 0.05. That is the threshold working as specified.
    3. Under the null, p-values are uniform — flat to within 0.6 percentage points. A null p-value is a random number between 0 and 1.
    4. p is not the probability the null is true, not the chance of replication, not an effect size, and 0.049 against 0.051 is the same evidence.
    5. At 10% of hypotheses true and 80% power, 36% of significant findings are false. At 40% power, 53%.
    6. Low power raises the false-positive share of your discoveries, so a small study that finds something is weaker evidence than a large one that finds the same thing.
    7. At 14 tests, a false positive is more likely than not — 51.2% with nothing going on. At 100 tests, 99.4%.
    8. 3 measures × 4 subgroups × 2 windows = 24 implicit tests, and a 70.8% chance of finding something that is not there.
    9. Count the tests rather than avoid looking. Exploration is how you find things worth testing; treat what you find as a hypothesis for fresh data.
    10. Bonferroni for "no false claims"; Benjamini–Hochberg for screening.
    11. Correction costs power: 85% falls to 32% at 100 tests. It moves error between types rather than removing it.
    12. Compute power before, not after. The useful question is the smallest effect you could reliably detect given the traffic you have.
    13. Post-hoc power from the observed effect is circular and adds nothing to the p-value.
    14. A significant result can be too small to act on; a non-significant one can include a 19% lift. Report the effect, the interval, the sample size, then the p-value.
    15. "Not significant" means the data cannot distinguish it from zero, and a narrow interval around zero is a different finding from a wide one.
    16. Bootstrap anything awkward. It gives an interval for any statistic with no distributional assumption.
    17. Independence is the assumption that actually breaks things. Repeated measurements make the effective sample far smaller than the row count.
