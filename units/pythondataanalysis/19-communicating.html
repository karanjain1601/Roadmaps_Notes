CHAPTER 19

Communicating results

"Cuts the risk by half" can mean helping twenty thousand people or twenty, depending on a baseline the sentence does not mention. And a table of forty segments that all have exactly the same underlying rate, sorted by observed rate, ranks them by sample size — with the smallest at the top.

    STRUCTURE

    Lead with the finding

    The order you discovered things in is almost never the order to present them in, and the temptation to justify the work first is strong.

    The order it happened

    1. Got the data
    2. Cleaned it, which took a week
    3. Tried three approaches
    4. Found the thing
    5. Checked it

    Reasonable, chronological, and it buries the answer in the fourth section of a document most people will not finish.

    The order to write it

    1. The finding, in one sentence
    2. What it means for a decision
    3. The evidence, briefly
    4. What would change the conclusion
    5. Method and caveats, at the end

    The reader gets the point in ten seconds and can choose how far to read.

    The one-sentence test

    If you cannot state the finding in a sentence, you do not have one yet — you have a set of observations. "Churn is 3.2 points higher among customers who never used the mobile app, worth about £180,000 a year" is a finding. "We looked at churn by several dimensions" is a status update.

    Write the sentence first, before the document. If it turns out to be unwriteable, that is information about the analysis rather than about your writing.

    Two structural habits that cost nothing. Put the number in the heading — "Mobile non-users churn 3.2 points higher" rather than "Churn analysis" — because headings are what people read. And keep the method out of the summary; the reader who wants to know whether you used a chi-squared test will look for it, and the reader who does not should not have to walk past it.

    THE SAME NUMBER, FOUR WAYS

    Relative, absolute, and the factor of a thousand

    A relative change is baseline-independent, which is exactly what makes it unreadable on its own.

    The number needed to treat ranges from 5 to 5,000 — a factor of a thousand — behind an identical headline. The relative figure is not wrong; it is simply insufficient, and it is the one that gets quoted.

    The rule: never report one without the other

    "Reduces churn by 30%, from 2.0% to 1.4% — about 600 customers a year on our base" takes one extra clause and cannot be misread. Relative alone hides the scale; absolute alone hides whether the change is large for that quantity.

    And the mirror trap exists too. A rise from 62% to 64% is 2 percentage points and only 3.2% relative, so somebody wanting to minimise it will reach for the relative figure. Neither framing is dishonest. Reporting only one of them is.

    Two more framings worth having. Natural frequencies — "1 in 50 becomes 1 in 100" — are read more accurately than percentages by nearly everyone, including specialists. And number needed to treat is the honest translation of an effect into effort: at an NNT of 5,000 you must reach five thousand people for one to benefit, which is a fact about feasibility that no percentage conveys.

    THE TABLE THAT LIES

    Sorting segments by a noisy rate

    The most common misleading artefact in a business report, and it is produced by an entirely reasonable groupby.

    Forty segments, every one of which has exactly the same true rate of 5%. Their sizes vary the way real segments do — from a dozen to a few thousand. Sort by observed rate and see what happens.

    Forty segments with an identical true rate ranked by observed rate, and by a shrunk estimate

    TRUE RATE5.0%identical in all forty
    WHAT THE RANKING SHOWS—

    Three measured results.

    1. The top and bottom of the table are the small segments. The ten most extreme segments average 218 rows against 983 across all forty. The best performer has 44 rows and the worst has 16.
    2. The spread is entirely a sample-size effect. Observed rates vary by 9.1 percentage points among the smaller half and 2.5 among the larger — from data where the true rate is identical everywhere.
    3. Shrinkage collapses it. Pulling each segment towards the pooled rate in proportion to its uncertainty reduces the spread from 9.1 points to 2.8, and moves the mean size of the top five from 163 to 709. The ranking stops being a ranking of sample sizes.
    Why this is worse than an ordinary error

    It produces confident, actionable-looking conclusions. "Region S37 converts at 9.1%, nearly double the average — what are they doing differently?" is a natural response to that table, and there is nothing to find, because S37 has forty-four customers and got lucky.

    Three defences, in increasing order of effort. Always show n next to the rate, which costs one column and lets the reader discount the small rows themselves. Suppress segments below a minimum size, stating the threshold. And shrink towards the pooled rate, which is what the demo does — a segment with 20 observations barely moves off the average, and one with 4,000 keeps its own figure.

    shrinkage.py
    g = df.groupby("segment").agg(hits=("converted", "sum"), n=("converted", "size"))

pooled = g["hits"].sum() / g["n"].sum()
k = 200                                    # ~ the sample size you would trust
g["shrunk"] = (g["hits"] + k * pooled) / (g["n"] + k)

g.sort_values("shrunk", ascending=False)   # and always display n alongside

# the sanity check: are the extremes just the small segments?
g["rate"] = g["hits"] / g["n"]
print(g.nlargest(5, "rate")["n"].mean(), g["n"].mean())

    That last check is the two-line version of this whole section. If the mean size of your top five segments is far below the overall mean, the ranking is measuring noise, and no amount of further analysis of the top segment will find anything.

    SAYING HOW SURE

    Uncertainty, for a reader who does not want statistics

    Three phrasings that carry real information without requiring the reader to know what a confidence interval is.

    A range, in the quantity's own units
    "Between £120,000 and £340,000 a year, most likely around £210,000." No jargon, and it communicates precision exactly. This is the confidence interval of chapter 18, written for someone who has other things to do.
    A plain statement of what the data can and cannot distinguish
    "The difference could be anywhere from nothing to a 19% improvement — this test was too small to tell." Far more useful than "not statistically significant", which most readers hear as "no effect".
    What would change the answer
    "If the December spike turns out to be a data error, the effect halves." This is the single most valuable sentence in most analyses, and it is almost never written, because it feels like undermining your own work.
    False precision reads as confidence

    "£4,382.19" and "about £4,400" are the same estimate, and the first implies a precision you do not have. If the interval spans thousands, the pence are noise dressed as rigour — and a reader who notices will trust everything else less.

    The workable rule: round to roughly one digit finer than your uncertainty. An estimate of £4,382 with an interval of ±£1,500 should be reported as "about £4,400", or better, "£3,000 to £6,000".

    One thing to avoid entirely: hedging that carries no information. "Results may vary" and "further research is needed" and "this is only directional" are all true of every analysis ever conducted, so they tell the reader nothing while making the work sound weaker. Replace each with the specific version — which data would settle it, how much it might vary, and in which direction.

    READABILITY

    Numbers a reader can hold

  1. Round hard in prose, keep precision in the appendix. "About 3 in 10" is retained; "29.7%" is skimmed past. The exact figure belongs in the table beneath.
  2. Give a comparison alongside every unfamiliar magnitude. "£1.4m — roughly what we spend on the London office" tells the reader whether to care. A number with no reference point cannot be judged.
  3. Use consistent units and say which. Mixing percentages and percentage points in one paragraph is the most reliable way to be misunderstood, and they differ by exactly the factor this chapter's first demo is about.
  4. Avoid more than three or four numbers per paragraph. Beyond that they stop being read individually. A table is not a failure of prose; it is the correct container.
  5. Name the denominator every time. "12% of what" is the question every rate raises, and answering it in the same sentence prevents a whole class of misreading.
    6. The sentence that makes a table useful

    Above every table, write what the reader should notice: "The bottom two rows are the finding — both regions moved the wrong way." Without it, a table is a set of numbers the reader must interpret unaided, and they will either skip it or reach a different conclusion from yours.

    The same applies to charts. A title that states the finding — "Mobile churn rose after the March release" — is read; one that names the variables — "Churn by month and platform" — is not.

    THE POINT

    Tie it to a decision

    An analysis nobody acts on has failed, however correct it is. Usually the failure is that it never named a decision.

    The same result, presented two ways
    Not actionableActionable
    Finding"Churn correlates with app usage""Customers who never open the app churn 3.2 points more"
    Scale—"That is 340 customers, about £180,000 a year"
    Decision—"Onboarding to the app is worth testing"
    Confidence"p < 0.05""Between 1.8 and 4.6 points; and this is correlational"
    Next step"More analysis needed""A two-week trial on new signups would settle it"
    Ask what decision this changes, before starting

    If the answer is "none", the analysis may still be worth doing — understanding a system has value on its own — but be clear that is what it is, and do not be surprised when nobody acts.

    The more useful version of the question: which answer would lead to a different action? If every plausible result leads to the same next step, the analysis cannot change anything and the time is better spent elsewhere. That question takes thirty seconds and saves whole projects.

    And be explicit about the causal claim, since chapter 17 listed six explanations for a correlation. "Customers who use the app churn less" supports "test whether driving app adoption reduces churn". It does not support "app adoption reduces churn", and the gap between those two sentences is where analyses lose their credibility — usually not in the numbers.

    MAKING IT CHECKABLE

    Five things a reviewer needs

  6. The population. Which rows, over what period, with which exclusions and how many. "Active UK customers, Jan–Jun 2024, excluding 412 test accounts" — not "our customers".
  7. The definitions. What counts as churn, as active, as a conversion. The Data Engineer unit measured four binary choices in a revenue definition producing sixteen distinct figures spanning 31%; the same applies to any metric.
  8. The decisions you made. Which rows were dropped and why, how missing values were handled, what counted as an outlier. Parts 4 and 5 of this unit measured what each of those choices costs.
  9. The number of comparisons. Including the ones made by eye. Chapter 18's table turns that count into a probability.
  10. Enough to reproduce it. The query or the notebook, the library versions, and the seed — which chapter 6 showed is necessary and not sufficient on its own.
    11. Write the caveats you would want to have been told

    The useful test is not "have I disclosed enough to be safe" but "if this turns out to be wrong in six months, what will the post-mortem say I should have flagged?" That question surfaces the real caveats and filters out the ritual ones.

    It also tends to shorten the caveats section, because most of what people write there is defensive rather than informative.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Lead with the finding, then the decision, then the evidence. Method and caveats at the end.
    2. If you cannot state it in one sentence you do not have a finding yet. Write the sentence before the document.
    3. A 50% reduction gives an NNT of 5 at a 40% baseline and 5,000 at a 0.04% baseline — a factor of a thousand behind an identical headline.
    4. Never report relative without absolute. One extra clause and it cannot be misread.
    5. The mirror trap: 62% to 64% is 2 points and only 3.2% relative. Neither framing is dishonest; reporting one is.
    6. Natural frequencies read more accurately than percentages, and NNT translates an effect into effort.
    7. Forty segments with an identical 5% true rate spread 9.1 points among the smaller half and 2.5 among the larger.
    8. The ten most extreme segments averaged 218 rows against 983 overall. Sorting by rate ranks by sample size.
    9. Shrinkage cut the spread from 9.1 to 2.8 points and moved the top five's mean size from 163 to 709.
    10. Show n beside every rate, suppress tiny segments with a stated threshold, or shrink towards the pooled figure.
    11. Two-line check: if the top five segments are far smaller than average, the ranking is noise.
    12. State uncertainty as a range in real units, and say what the data cannot distinguish rather than "not significant".
    13. Round to about one digit finer than your uncertainty. False precision reads as confidence.
    14. Replace hedges that fit every analysis with the specific version: which data would settle it, and how much it might move.
    15. Name the denominator, give a comparison for unfamiliar magnitudes, and put the finding in the chart title.
    16. Ask which answer would lead to a different action. If none would, the analysis cannot change anything.
    17. A reviewer needs the population, the definitions, the decisions, the comparison count and the means to reproduce it.
