CHAPTER 13

Outliers

Five standard methods run on the same five thousand values agree on 4% of the points they flag. That is not a defect in any of them — it is the finding. "Outlier" is a decision you make, not a property the data has, and the methods differ because they encode different decisions.

    THE DISTINCTION

    Three things that get called outliers

    They need completely different handling, and no detector can tell them apart.

    An error
    A value that is not a measurement of anything: a sentinel like 999999 used to mean "unknown", a negative age, a temperature in the wrong unit, a decimal point in the wrong place. These should be removed or corrected, and finding them is a data quality task.
    A genuine extreme
    A real observation from the tail of a real distribution. The customer who spent £400,000, the day with fifty times normal traffic. Removing these is not cleaning — it is deleting the most interesting data you have, and for anything involving risk, fraud or capacity it is deleting exactly the observations that matter.
    A different population
    A value that is fine but belongs to something else: a wholesale order in a retail dataset, a bot session among human ones, a test account. The right response is usually to segment rather than to remove — and to notice that the presence of two populations is itself the finding.
    No statistical test distinguishes these

    A detector reports that a value is far from the others. Whether that is a typo, a whale customer or a bot is a question about the world, answerable only by looking at the row and knowing what it represents.

    Which gives the rule the rest of this chapter is built on: look at the flagged rows before doing anything to them. Not the count — the rows. Twenty minutes reading the actual records tells you which of the three you have, and no amount of threshold tuning will.

    1. z-score has perfect precision and terrible recall. It flagged 10 points and every one was a genuine defect — and it missed twenty of them, because the thirty defects inflated the standard deviation enough to hide each other. That is the next section.
    2. IQR × 1.5 has the opposite problem. 90% recall at 11% precision: 227 legitimate high-earning values flagged as anomalies, because a skewed distribution has a real right tail and the 1.5 rule was designed for symmetric data.
    3. The percentile rule caught all thirty and flags 2% of any column you point it at whether or not anything is wrong. It cannot tell you that a dataset is clean.
    4. Agreement between methods is very low. z-score and IQR × 1.5 agree on 4% of their combined flags. Switching the distribution to symmetric raises the agreement substantially, which tells you the disagreement is about skew rather than about the methods being arbitrary.
    The 1.5 in the IQR rule is a convention, not a result

    Tukey chose it so that roughly 0.7% of a normal distribution falls outside the fence. On a skewed distribution it flags far more, and the amount depends entirely on the skew rather than on anything being wrong. Income, revenue, session length, file size and city population are all right-skewed, so this is the common case rather than the exception.

    For skewed data, either work on a log scale — where the distribution is roughly symmetric and the rule means what it was designed to mean — or use a percentile rule and accept that it flags a fixed fraction.

    WHY Z-SCORES FAIL

    The ceiling, and masking

    Two results about the standard deviation that make the z > 3 rule much weaker than it appears. One of them is a hard mathematical limit.

    A z-score divides by the sample standard deviation. But the outlier is in the sample, so it contributes to the very quantity it is being measured against. Two consequences follow, and the first is a theorem.

    The z-score ceiling, and outliers hiding each other a mathematical limit, verified by pushing a value to 1012

    Both results are stronger than the usual advice suggests.

    A z-score cannot exceed (n−1)/√n, whatever the value is

    For a sample of n points, the largest possible absolute z-score is (n-1)/√n. At n = 10 that is 2.846. So on a ten-row sample, the z > 3 rule can never flag anything at all, no matter how extreme a value is — the demo pushes one point to 1012 and its z-score converges on 2.846 and stops.

    The rule only becomes capable of firing at n = 11, where the ceiling is 3.015. If you have applied a three-sigma rule to a small group — per customer, per day, per category — it may have been mathematically incapable of flagging anything, and it reported zero outliers as though that were a finding.

    The second result is masking. Switch the view and add outliers. One value at 400 in a distribution centred on 50 has a z-score of 12.91 and is flagged immediately. Twenty of them have z-scores of 3.14 — just clearing the threshold. Forty of them have z-scores of 2.22, and the rule flags none of the forty, because together they have inflated the standard deviation from 27 to 131. The MAD-based rule flags all forty throughout.

    The breakdown point: how much contamination a statistic tolerates
    StatisticRobust versionBreakdown pointMeaning
    MeanMedian0% against 50%One value moves the mean arbitrarily far
    Standard deviationMAD0% against 50%One value inflates it arbitrarily

    A breakdown point of 0% means a single observation can move the statistic anywhere. That is the property that makes mean and standard deviation unsuitable for detecting the very thing that breaks them, and it is why the MAD-based rule exists.

    robust.py
    from scipy.stats import median_abs_deviation

def mad_z(s):
    """Robust z-scores. 0.6745 makes MAD comparable to a standard deviation."""
    mad = median_abs_deviation(s.dropna())
    if mad == 0:                       # more than half the values are identical
        return pd.Series(0, index=s.index)
    return 0.6745 * (s - s.median()) / mad

flagged = df[mad_z(df["amount"]).abs() > 3.5]

# and for skewed data, the honest alternative: work on a log scale
flagged = df[mad_z(np.log1p(df["amount"])).abs() > 3.5]

    Note the mad == 0 guard. If more than half a column's values are identical — common for counts, ratings and anything with a floor — the MAD is zero and every non-modal value gets an infinite score. That is a real failure mode and it needs handling rather than dividing.

    WHAT TO DO

    Four responses

    Removal is the reflex and the least often correct. Each of these is defensible in the right circumstance.

    Remove

    Correct when the value is genuinely not a measurement — a sentinel, an impossible sign, a duplicate. Record how many and why.

    Wrong when the value is real, because you are then deleting the tail and every statistic about spread, risk or capacity becomes optimistic.

    Cap (winsorise)

    df["x"].clip(lower=lo, upper=hi). Keeps the row and limits its influence, which is often the honest middle ground for a regression or a mean.

    It does bias the mean toward the centre, and it makes the maximum a fiction — so never report a maximum from a capped column.

    Transform

    np.log1p on a right-skewed column pulls the tail in without discarding anything, and frequently makes the whole question disappear: on a log scale the distribution is roughly symmetric and the outliers are no longer extreme.

    The cost is interpretability — coefficients and means are now in log units.

    Keep, and use robust statistics

    Report the median rather than the mean, Spearman rather than Pearson, a quantile regression rather than least squares.

    Nothing is removed, nothing is invented, and the summary is simply less sensitive. Frequently the best answer and the least often chosen.

    The response that is almost always right

    Report both. "Mean order value is £340, or £115 excluding the twelve orders above £50,000" is a sentence nobody can argue with, and it is more informative than either number alone — because the gap between them is the finding.

    An analysis that quietly removes rows and reports one number has hidden its most interesting result. An analysis that reports both has explained the distribution.

    WHERE THEY COME FROM

    Six sources, and how to recognise each

    The recurring causes, and the tell
    CauseLooks likeThe tell

    Unit mixingValues ~1,000× or ~2.2× apartTwo clusters. Grams against kilograms, pounds against kilos, cents against pounds
    Decimal shiftOne value exactly 100× a plausible oneDivide the flagged values by 100 and see if they look ordinary
    A different populationA separate cluster, not a tailPlot it. Bimodal means two populations, and chapter 16 shows how easily that is hidden
    Aggregate rowsA value near the sum of the othersChapter 9's subtotal row from a spreadsheet. Often has a null identifier
    Genuine extremesA smooth heavy tailNo gap between the flagged values and the rest — the tail is continuous
    The single most useful diagnostic

    df["x"].nlargest(20) and df["x"].nsmallest(20). Twenty seconds, and it distinguishes almost all six: repeated identical values are sentinels, a gap before the extremes means a separate population, a smooth run means a real tail, and a value exactly a hundred times its neighbour is a decimal point.

    Then df.nlargest(20, "x") without the column selector, so you see the whole row. That is where you learn that all twelve are from one customer, or one date, or one source system.

    SAYING SO

    What to write down

    Whatever you decide, the decision belongs in the output rather than only in the code.

  1. The rule, stated as a number. "Excluded values above the 99.5th percentile" rather than "removed outliers". The second is not reproducible and cannot be reviewed.
  2. How many rows, and what share. Twelve of 400,000 is a footnote. Twelve thousand of 400,000 is a finding about the data that deserves its own paragraph.
  3. What they had in common. If the excluded rows are 80% from one region or one week, that is the result, and removing them has hidden it.
  4. The effect on the headline number. Both figures, per the previous section. If exclusion moves the answer by 3% nobody needs to think about it; if it moves it by 60%, the choice of rule is the analysis.
  5. Do it before looking at the outcome. Choosing an exclusion rule after seeing which rule gives the answer you expected is how honest people produce unreliable work, and it does not feel like anything at the time.
    6. The order matters more than the rule

    Any of the five detectors is defensible if chosen in advance and stated. None is defensible if chosen by trying several and keeping the one whose result looked best — and given that they flagged 10, 59, 102, 168 and 254 points on the same column, there is a great deal of room to choose an answer.

    Write the rule down before you compute the outcome. That single ordering removes most of the risk, and it costs nothing.

    Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Three different things get called outliers — an error, a genuine extreme, a different population — and no statistical test distinguishes them.
    2. Look at the flagged rows, not the count. Twenty minutes reading records beats any amount of threshold tuning.
    3. The five standard methods flagged 10, 59, 102, 168 and 254 points on the same column. z-score and IQR × 1.5 agree on 4% of their combined flags.
    4. z-score had 100% precision and 33% recall; IQR × 1.5 had 90% recall and 11% precision. There is no winner, only a trade.
    5. The 1.5 in the IQR rule assumes a symmetric distribution. On skewed data it flags the real tail, and most business quantities are skewed.
    6. A percentile rule flags a fixed fraction whatever the data, so it can never tell you a column is clean.
    7. A z-score cannot exceed (n−1)/√n. At n = 10 that is 2.846, so z > 3 can never fire on a ten-row sample, however extreme the value.
    8. The rule only becomes capable of firing at n = 11. A three-sigma rule applied per small group may have been mathematically unable to flag anything.
    9. Outliers mask each other. One value at 400 scores z = 12.91; forty identical ones score 2.22 and the rule flags none of them. MAD flags all forty throughout.
    10. Mean and standard deviation have a breakdown point of 0%. One observation moves them arbitrarily, which is why they cannot detect the thing that breaks them.
    11. Guard against MAD = 0, which happens when over half a column's values are identical.
    12. Four responses: remove, cap, transform, keep with robust statistics. Removal is the reflex and the least often correct.
    13. Report both numbers. The gap between them is the finding.
    14. nlargest(20) on the whole row distinguishes almost every cause in twenty seconds.
    15. Choose the rule before computing the outcome. With five methods flagging between 10 and 254 points, there is a great deal of room to pick an answer.
