Cloud posture at scale

The Cyber Security unit covers what goes wrong in a cloud account. This chapter is about the hundred accounts: how to make the secure configuration arrive by default, which layer of control to use for which rule, and how to shrink permissions that nobody can safely shrink by hand.

~28 min read Landing zones · guardrails · right-sizing · posture 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: Cyber Security ch 14

14.1Accounts as the isolation primitive

The Cyber Security unit argued that account boundaries isolate better than policy inside an account. At scale that becomes an organising principle rather than a preference — and it changes what "an account" is for.

How to split, and what each split buys
Split byContainsNote
EnvironmentA production mistake cannot touch development, and vice versaThe minimum viable split, and the one everybody does first
WorkloadOne compromised service reaches only its own resourcesThe strongest practical isolation, and it makes cost attribution free
SensitivityRegulated data in its own account with tighter guardrails and separate accessKeeps audit scope small, which is worth real money — ch 21
Shared servicesLogging, networking, identity and the artefact registry, each separateThe logging account in particular must not be reachable from the accounts it records
SandboxSomewhere people can experiment, with hard spend limits and automatic expiryPrevents experiments happening in production accounts, which is what happens otherwise
Many accounts is only safer if they are consistent

Splitting into fifty accounts and configuring each one by hand produces fifty differently-wrong accounts and an inventory problem — which is worse than one carefully-run account. The prerequisite is a vending process: a new account is created by automation, arrives with the baseline already applied, is enrolled in centralised logging and identity, is attached to the right guardrails, and is registered with an owner before anyone can use it. If creating an account is a form to fill in and a runbook to follow, the estate will drift within months and the accounts nobody remembers are where the incidents come from.

14.2The control hierarchy

Which layer stops it?

Cloud permissions are the intersection of several layers. They stop different things, and only one survives a compromised account administrator.

What belongs at each layer 
ORGANISATION GUARDRAILS  (service control policies and peers)
  The only layer that constrains an ACCOUNT ADMINISTRATOR.
  Use for a small number of absolute rules:
    · no disabling or deleting the audit trail
    · no leaving the organisation
    · no using regions we do not operate in
    · no deleting the security tooling's roles
  Keep the list SHORT. These apply to everyone including
  break-glass, and a mistake here is an estate-wide outage
  you may not be able to undo from inside.

PERMISSION BOUNDARIES
  A ceiling on what a role can be granted, so that teams can
  create their own roles without being able to create one
  more powerful than the boundary allows. This is what makes
  delegating IAM safe.

IDENTITY POLICIES
  What this principal may do. Where right-sizing happens.

RESOURCE POLICIES
  Who may touch this resource, from where. The place to say
  "only principals in this organisation" — which is the
  condition that stops a copied snapshot landing in someone
  else's account.

AND THE INTERSECTION RULE
  Access requires ALLOW at every applicable layer and DENY at
  none. So a guardrail deny cannot be overridden by any
  identity policy, however permissive — which is exactly why
  the top layer is the one that survives compromise.

14.3Right-sizing permissions

Every estate accumulates over-permission, because granting is easy and narrowing is risky. The way out is to stop guessing and use the usage data the provider already collects.

Granted against used

Access analysers compare what a role may do with what it has done. The gap is the reduction available.

The observation window is a trap in both directions

Right-sizing from usage means removing permissions that were not exercised during the window, and that is only safe if the window covers everything the role legitimately does. A thirty-day window misses the quarterly reconciliation job, the annual audit export, the disaster-recovery procedure that ran once in March, and the incident path that has not been needed yet. Removing those permissions produces an outage at the worst possible moment — during the rare event, which is usually also the stressful one. But a very long window drifts the other way: it preserves permissions used once eighteen months ago by a person who has left. The workable approach is a window of at least a quarter, plus explicitly asking owners about periodic and emergency paths, plus making the reduction reversible — a boundary you can widen quickly beats a deletion you have to reconstruct.

14.4Posture management

What a posture tool is for, and what it is not
Good atPoor at
Finding resources created outside the pipeline, in accounts and regions nobody inventoriedTelling you which findings matter. The default rule packs generate thousands
Detecting drift from the baseline across many accounts at oncePrevention. It is detective by nature; the guardrails in §14.2 are what prevent
Answering "is this true everywhere?" for a specific controlApplication-layer issues, which it cannot see
Attack-path analysis: which findings combine into a route to something valuableWorking without an ownership mapping — findings with no owner are never fixed

The feature worth paying for is the last one. A tool that reports "this bucket is public" and "this role is over-permissioned" produces a list; a tool that reports "this public bucket contains credentials for this over-permissioned role which can read the production database" produces a priority. That is the same graph reasoning as attack-path analysis in a directory (Cyber Security ch 10), and it is the difference between thousands of findings and a handful of chains.

Making the findings actionable 
1  OWNERSHIP FIRST
   A finding without an owner is never fixed. Require owner
   and environment tags at creation — enforce it in the
   module (ch 11) and at the organisation layer — and treat
   an untagged resource as a finding in its own right.

2  ROUTE TO THE SOURCE, NOT TO A DASHBOARD
   The fix belongs in the Terraform module, so the finding
   should arrive as a pull request against the repository
   that defines the resource — not as a ticket in a queue
   the team does not read.

3  DEDUPLICATE BY CAUSE
   Two hundred findings from one module is one fix. Group by
   the defining module before reporting anything.   → ch 11

4  SUPPRESS WITH AN EXPIRY, NEVER PERMANENTLY
   Same rule as policy exceptions.                  → ch 10

5  PROMOTE THE STABLE RULES INTO GUARDRAILS
   Anything the tool reports repeatedly and that nobody ever
   legitimately needs should stop being a finding and start
   being prevented. A posture tool that reports the same
   thing every month is describing a missing guardrail.
Why is an organisation-level guardrail different in kind from an IAM policy?

Because it constrains the account administrator, and nothing below it does. Cloud access is the intersection of several layers — organisation guardrails, permission boundaries, identity policies, resource policies, and sometimes session policies — and access requires an allow at every applicable layer with a deny at none. An identity policy can be edited by anyone with IAM permissions in the account, which includes the account administrator and anyone who compromises them, so every control expressed there is only as strong as the weakest administrative credential in that account. An organisation guardrail is set outside the account and cannot be altered from within it, so it holds even when the account is fully compromised. That makes it the only layer that survives the scenario you most need to survive, and it is why a small number of absolute rules belong there: no disabling the audit trail, no leaving the organisation, no unapproved regions, no deleting the security tooling's roles. Three cautions follow from the same property. The list must be short and stable, because these apply to everyone including break-glass access, and a mistake can be an estate-wide outage that is awkward to undo from inside. They are deny-only in effect — a guardrail does not grant anything, so it cannot be used to give teams access, only to bound it. And they need testing in a non-production organisational unit first, because the blast radius of a bad one is every account beneath it. Everything else — the day-to-day question of what a given service may do — belongs in identity policies where it can be iterated on safely.

An access analyser says a role has used 4 of its 380 permissions in 90 days. Do you cut it to 4?

Not to exactly four, and the direction is right. Ninety days is long enough to cover weekly and monthly work and not long enough to cover everything a role legitimately does: the quarterly reconciliation, the annual audit export, the disaster-recovery procedure, the incident path that has not been exercised yet, and the seasonal peak that arrives in November. Cutting to the observed set means the first time one of those runs it fails, and it fails during precisely the rare, stressful event you least want to debug. So the sequence is: extend the window to at least a quarter and ideally a year for anything with periodic work; ask the owner explicitly about periodic and emergency paths, since that question surfaces things no telemetry will; and cut to the observed set plus what the owner names, rather than to the observed set alone. Two things make this dramatically safer. Apply a permission boundary before removing anything — the boundary caps what the role can do without changing what it is granted, so you get most of the risk reduction immediately and reversibly, and you can then narrow the policy underneath at leisure. And make the change through the infrastructure code as a pull request, so it is reviewed by the team that owns the role and can be reverted in minutes rather than reconstructed from memory. Finally, note the 4-of-380 shape itself: that is almost always a broad managed policy attached because it was convenient. The systemic fix is to stop attaching those in the module and template layer, which prevents the next four hundred roles from having the same problem.

Your posture tool reports 4,000 findings. Where do you start?

Not with the findings. Three passes before anyone remediates anything. Deduplicate by cause: group findings by the module or template that defines the resource, and the four thousand will usually collapse to a few dozen distinct defects, most of them fixable in one module change that resolves hundreds at once. Reporting a raw count implies an intractable backlog; reporting distinct defects usually implies a week of work. Establish ownership: a finding with no owner is never fixed, so the resources with no owner tag are a separate and more urgent problem than the findings themselves — and enforcing owner tags at creation stops the set growing. Look for chains rather than items: the valuable output of a posture tool is not "this bucket is public" but "this public bucket holds credentials for this over-permissioned role that can read the production database", because that is a route to something that matters and it is three findings that individually looked medium. If your tool cannot do attack-path analysis, doing it manually for the top few assets is still worth an afternoon. After those passes, prioritise by exposure and asset value rather than by the tool's severity rating, exactly as in Cyber Security chapter 13. And one structural move: any rule the tool reports repeatedly, that nobody ever legitimately needs, should be promoted from a finding into an organisation guardrail. A posture tool reporting the same thing every month is not doing detection work — it is describing a control you have not implemented.

Recap

  1. Accounts are the isolation primitive, and many accounts is only safer if they are created consistently.
  2. Account vending is the prerequisite — baseline, logging, identity and an owner, before anyone can use it.
  3. Access is the intersection of every layer, and a guardrail deny cannot be overridden below.
  4. Only organisation guardrails survive a compromised account administrator. Keep that list short.
  5. Permission boundaries are what make delegating IAM safe.
  6. Resource policies are where you say "only principals in this organisation" — the condition that stops snapshot exfiltration.
  7. Right-size from usage data, but the window must cover periodic and emergency paths.
  8. Apply a boundary before deleting permissions — reversible beats reconstructed.
  9. Posture tools detect; guardrails prevent. Anything reported every month is a missing guardrail.
  10. Deduplicate by cause and establish ownership before remediating a single finding.
