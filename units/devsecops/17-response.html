Response with engineering teams

The Cyber Security unit covers the response lifecycle. This chapter is about the awkward part that unit assumes away: the people who understand the affected system do not work in security, have never run an incident of this kind, and are the ones who have to do most of the work.

The instinct that causes the most damage

Every engineer's trained reflex is to restore service quickly, and in a security incident that reflex destroys the investigation. Restarting the pod, rotating the instance, redeploying from the pipeline, clearing the logs to free disk — each is a normal, competent operational action, and each removes evidence you cannot get back. The fix is not to retrain the instinct in the moment, which does not work under stress; it is to make the safe action the easy one in advance: a one-command "isolate this workload" that applies a deny-all policy and snapshots state, so the fast thing to do is also the correct thing. If isolating requires improvisation, people will restart instead.

17.2Who gets paged

Routing an alert

Both extremes fail. Security alone lacks the system knowledge; the team alone lacks the incident knowledge.

A runbook an engineer at 3am will actually follow 
WHAT MAKES ONE USABLE
  · It starts with what NOT to do, because that is the
    instinct that fires first.
  · Commands are copy-pasteable, with the placeholders
    obvious. Not "isolate the affected workload".
  · It says who to wake and how, by role, with the escalation
    path if they do not answer in ten minutes.
  · It fits on one screen. Anything longer is a reference
    document, and a reference document is not a runbook.
  · It has been executed at least once by someone who did
    not write it. Otherwise it is a hypothesis.

SKELETON — SUSPECTED WORKLOAD COMPROMISE

  DO NOT: restart the pod, redeploy, scale to zero, or
  delete anything. All of it destroys evidence and the
  replacement has the same vulnerability.

  1  ISOLATE          (network only — leave it running)
       kubectl label pod <POD> -n <NS> quarantine=true
       # a pre-existing NetworkPolicy denies all traffic
       # for pods with this label

  2  PRESERVE
       kubectl cp / node snapshot per the platform runbook
       Capture: process list, connections, env, recent logs

  3  MAINTAIN CAPACITY
       kubectl scale deploy/<NAME> --replicas=<N+1>
       # so isolating does not become an outage, which is
       # the pressure that makes people kill it instead

  4  DECLARE          #incident-<date>-<short-name>
       Say what you observed, not what you concluded.

  5  PAGE             service on-call + security on-call
       If the platform itself may be compromised, move to
       the out-of-band channel. Do not discuss it here.

  6  ASSESS SCOPE     before any remediation
       Same image elsewhere? Same credentials? Same base?

17.3The pipeline is a response tool

Capabilities you already have, if delivery is healthy
NeedWhat the pipeline gives you
"Which services use this library?"SBOM attestations, queried across the estate — minutes rather than a week of asking — ch 9
"Roll back to the last known-good"Redeploy a previous signed digest. Fast, and it is a known artefact rather than a rebuild
"Stop anything vulnerable from deploying"An admission policy pushed in minutes, blocking the affected version fleet-wide
"Rotate every credential this service holds"If secrets are managed and short-lived, a rotation is a deploy — ch 7
"What changed just before this started?"Deployment history correlated with the alert timeline. Usually the fastest route to root cause
"Ship the fix"A pipeline that normally deploys in fifteen minutes deploys the fix in fifteen minutes. This is where delivery speed becomes a security property
The pipeline is also a target during the incident

Everything above assumes the pipeline is trustworthy, and if the intrusion reached it (Chapter 8) then it is neither trustworthy nor safe to use — deploying the fix through a compromised build system hands the attacker your remediation. Establish early whether the pipeline is in scope. If it might be, you need an out-of-band deployment path and a way to verify artefacts independently, and that is not something to design during an incident. It is worth deciding in advance: how would we deploy if we could not trust CI? Most organisations have no answer, and the honest ones write down a manual break-glass procedure and test it once a year.

17.5Afterwards

A retrospective that changes something 
BLAMELESS MEANS SPECIFIC, NOT VAGUE
  Not "mistakes were made" — that avoids the analysis as
  much as blame does. Say exactly what happened, including
  who did what, while treating every action as reasonable
  given what that person knew at the time. The question is
  never "why did they do that?" but "what made that the
  sensible thing to do?"

THE TIMELINE FIRST, INTERPRETATION SECOND
  Build it from logs and commands, with timestamps, before
  anyone offers a theory. Theories reorganise memory.

FOR EACH DELAY, ASK WHAT WOULD HAVE REMOVED IT
  Not what the person should have known. Delays are the
  most actionable output because they are mechanical:
    detection took 6 hours   → no alert existed for this
    ownership took 40 min    → no owner tag on the resource
    isolation took 25 min    → improvised, no prepared action
    scope took 3 hours       → no SBOM to query
  Each of those is a system fix, and each is cheap next to
  the incident it shortens.

ACTIONS NEED OWNERS AND DATES, AND SHOULD BE FEW
  Five owned actions beat thirty listed ones. Thirty is a
  document; five is work.

SHARE IT WIDELY
  A retrospective read by one team teaches one team. The
  same failure exists in three other services, and the
  people who will hit it next are the ones who have not
  heard about it.

AND FEED IT BACK INTO THE PIPELINE
  The most durable output is a control, not a lesson: a new
  admission policy, a detection rule, a paved-road change,
  a required test. Anything that depends on people
  remembering will decay.
Should the service team or the security team be on call for security alerts?

Both, with clearly different jobs, because each alone is missing something the other has. Security alone cannot act: they do not know whether that outbound connection is normal for this service, whether that process is part of the application, what the deployment does, or what breaks if they isolate it — so every step requires waking someone from the team anyway, and the delay while they orient is where the damage happens. They also cannot fix anything, since the remediation is almost always a change to the service. The service team alone can act and frequently acts wrongly: they have the operational reflex to restore service, they have not seen this failure mode before, and they do not know the obligations — the regulatory clock, the evidence-handling requirements, whether the discussion channel is safe. The arrangement that works is the service team as the primary responder, since they can actually do things, with security joining as an advisor who brings the incident expertise, owns the scope and communications questions, and makes the calls the team is not equipped to make. For anything beyond a routine case, add a named incident commander whose only job is coordination — not investigation, not fixing — because the most common failure in a multi-team incident is five competent people investigating the same thing while nobody tracks scope or decides what to do next. Two supporting requirements. The alert must identify the owning team automatically, or the first twenty minutes go on working out who to wake. And the on-call engineer must have authority to isolate production without approval, because a containment action gated on finding a manager at 3am is a containment action that happens an hour late.

Why is "restart it" so often the wrong first move in a security incident?

Because it destroys the evidence and does not remove the cause, while looking exactly like a resolution. Restarting discards process memory, the process tree, open network connections, anything the attacker wrote to the container filesystem, and the environment of the one instance you had — so afterwards you cannot establish how they got in, what they reached, whether they persisted, or whether other instances are affected. And since the replacement is created from the same image with the same dependencies and the same configuration, an entry route that lives in the artefact rather than in that instance is still there; the attacker returns, often within minutes, and now you have no evidence from either occurrence. The deeper problem is that it feels like it worked: the alert clears, the metrics normalise, and the incident gets closed. This reflex is not carelessness — it is the correct instinct for an outage, drilled in by every other incident the engineer has handled, and it fires hardest under stress. So the countermeasure is structural rather than educational: make the safe action the fast one. A pre-existing network policy keyed to a label, so isolation is one command; a documented snapshot step; and a scale-up so that isolating a replica does not reduce capacity, which removes the pressure that pushes people toward killing it. Then the runbook's first line can be "do not restart" and it will hold, because the alternative is right there and takes ten seconds. The exception is real and should be explicit: active destruction, encryption, or exfiltration you cannot otherwise stop justifies killing the workload, as a deliberate decision with a name attached rather than as a reflex.

What makes a retrospective produce change rather than a document?

Focusing on delays rather than on decisions, and converting each into a system change with an owner. A retrospective that asks "what went wrong" produces a narrative and some resolutions to be more careful, which decay within weeks. A retrospective that builds a timestamped timeline and then asks, for each gap in it, "what would have removed this delay?" produces mechanical answers: detection took six hours because no alert existed for this behaviour; identifying the owner took forty minutes because the resource had no owner tag; isolation took twenty-five minutes because nobody had a prepared command; establishing scope took three hours because there was no SBOM to query. Every one of those is a concrete, cheap, testable fix, and each of them shortens every future incident rather than only this class of one. Three practices make it work. Timeline before interpretation — build it from logs and command history before anyone offers a theory, because a theory reorganises everyone's memory of what happened. Blameless meaning specific rather than vague — name exactly what happened including who did what, while treating each action as reasonable given what that person knew, and asking what made it the sensible choice rather than why they made it; vagueness protects nobody and prevents the analysis. And few owned actions — five with names and dates beat thirty in a list, because thirty is a document. Two things extend the value beyond the affected team: share it widely, since the same weakness usually exists in several other services and the people who will hit it next have not heard about it, and prefer outputs that are controls rather than lessons — an admission policy, a detection rule, a paved-road change — because anything relying on people remembering will not survive the year.

Recap

  1. Restoring service fast is the trained reflex and the wrong one here. Understand scope before you restore.
  2. Make the safe action the easy one in advance — a one-command isolate, or people will restart.
  3. Scale up by one when isolating, so containment does not become an outage and create pressure to kill.
  4. Service team responds; security advises; a commander coordinates for anything non-routine.
  5. The on-call engineer needs authority to isolate production without finding a manager first.
  6. A runbook starts with what not to do and fits on one screen.
  7. An unrehearsed runbook is a hypothesis. Someone who did not write it must have run it.
  8. The pipeline is a response tool — SBOM queries, rollback to a signed digest, fleet-wide admission blocks.
  9. Decide in advance how you would deploy if CI could not be trusted.
  10. Retrospectives should attack delays, and their best output is a control rather than a lesson.
