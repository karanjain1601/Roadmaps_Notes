The delivery pipeline as a system

Every security control in this unit lands somewhere in the path from an idea to running code, and where it lands determines almost everything about it — how fast the feedback is, how much it costs, whether it can block, and whether anyone will tolerate it. This chapter is that map.

2.1The stages

Two stages carry more than their share

Design, because it is the only place architectural flaws are cheap, and because no tool will ever find them — a scanner cannot tell you that your authorisation model cannot express the rule you need. And deploy, because it is the last moment at which saying no costs nothing: refusing a configuration takes a second, whereas removing it from production is a change with a rollback plan. Most organisations invest heavily in the middle of this pipeline, where the tooling is, and thinly at both ends, where the leverage is.

2.3Gates and guardrails

The distinction that decides how a control feels to use, and it is not the same as blocking versus warning.

Two shapes of control
GateGuardrail
Says"Stop and wait for a decision""This particular thing is not permitted"

ScalesNo — it is a queue, and the queue is the bottleneckYes — it is a rule, evaluated in milliseconds
Failure modeBecomes a rubber stamp, or gets skipped under pressureBecomes a blind spot if the rule is wrong, or noise if too broad

Turning a gate into a guardrail 
GATE      "Security must review any change touching auth."
          → a queue, an SLA nobody meets, and a review that
            becomes a signature once the team is busy.

GUARDRAIL "Changes to the auth module require a test proving
           an unauthorised caller gets 403, and the policy
           file is validated against the schema. Both run in
           CI. A new auth PATTERN — not a new usage — opens a
           design conversation."
          → the common case is automated and instant; the rare
            case gets the human attention it deserves.

THE CONVERSION RECIPE
  1  Look at the last thirty times the gate fired. What did
     the reviewer actually check?
  2  Most of it will be a checklist. Encode the checklist.
  3  What remains is the judgement — usually a small
     fraction, and genuinely worth a person.
  4  Route only that fraction to a human, and make the
     trigger specific enough that it fires rarely.

AND ALWAYS: A DOCUMENTED EXCEPTION PATH
  With an owner, a reason and an expiry. A guardrail with no
  exception path becomes a gate the moment it is wrong, and
  it will be wrong.                                   → ch 10

2.4The cycle-time budget

Pipeline time is a shared resource with a fixed budget, and security spends from the same account as everything else. Treating it as free is how a security programme becomes something teams work around.

What pipeline time costs

Slow pipelines change behaviour: bigger batches, less frequent integration, and more work in flight.

The ten-minute threshold

There is a behavioural cliff somewhere around ten minutes, and where exactly it sits matters less than that it exists. Below it, an engineer waits for the pipeline — they stay in context, and a failure is corrected immediately. Above it, they switch to something else, and now a failure costs the switch back plus the reconstruction of what they were doing, which is far more than the wall-clock difference suggests. Worse, it changes behaviour upstream: when integration is expensive, people batch their work, and larger batches mean more changes per run, harder debugging, and more merge conflict. A pipeline that crosses that threshold makes the whole team's work lumpier, which is a cost that never appears on the security team's ledger and is real anyway.

2.5The paved road

What a paved road contains, and why it beats a policy document 
A SERVICE TEMPLATE
  Authentication wired in, structured logging, health checks,
  a sensible dependency set, and the CI configuration already
  present. New services inherit a security posture instead of
  re-deriving one.

VETTED BASE IMAGES
  Minimal, rebuilt and patched centrally, scanned once rather
  than by every team. When a base-layer vulnerability lands,
  one rebuild fixes everyone.                        → ch 12

INFRASTRUCTURE MODULES
  A bucket module that is private, encrypted and logged by
  default; a database module that is not publicly reachable.
  The secure configuration is what you get by NOT thinking
  about it.                                          → ch 11

A SHARED PIPELINE DEFINITION
  So the checks exist without each team assembling them, and
  improving the pipeline improves every team at once.

LIBRARIES THAT MAKE THE SAFE CALL THE OBVIOUS ONE
  A query builder that parameterises; a template engine that
  encodes by context; an HTTP client with sane timeouts and
  certificate validation that cannot be casually disabled.

THE TEST OF A PAVED ROAD
  It is not "is it mandatory" but "is it the path of least
  resistance". If the fastest way to ship is to use it, you
  do not need a policy; if leaving it is faster, no policy
  will hold. Measure ADOPTION, not compliance.

2.6Environments

Promotion, and the mistakes at each boundary
EnvironmentShould beCommon mistake
LocalFast, with fake dataA copy of production data on laptops — the most common data exposure nobody counts
CIEphemeral, isolated, minimal credentialsLong-lived cloud credentials with broad permissions — ch 8
StagingStructurally identical to production, with synthetic dataReal customer data, weaker controls, and public exposure "because it is only staging"
ProductionReached only through the pipelineStanding human access, and console changes that drift from the code

Two rules make promotion trustworthy. Build once, promote the same artefact — rebuilding per environment means the thing you tested is not the thing you shipped, and it re-opens every supply chain question at each step. And configuration differs, code does not: the artefact is identical and the environment supplies its own configuration and credentials, which is what makes "it worked in staging" a meaningful statement.

Why is a security review of every change an anti-pattern, when reviewing changes finds real problems?

Because it is a gate, and gates are queues whose throughput is set by the size of the reviewing team rather than by the rate of change. At any meaningful delivery rate the arithmetic fails immediately: a handful of security engineers against hundreds of changes a week means the queue grows without bound, and the organisation resolves that in one of three ways, all bad. The review becomes a rubber stamp — the reviewer glances and approves, so the control's real coverage is near zero while its cost is fully paid. It gets skipped under pressure, reliably at the moment the change is most consequential, because that is when the release pressure is highest. Or it becomes a bottleneck people route around, which turns the security function into an obstacle and destroys the relationship the whole model depends on. None of that means review is worthless — it means review is the wrong shape for the common case. The productive move is the conversion in §2.3: look at what the reviewer actually checks, notice that most of it is a checklist, encode the checklist as guardrails that run in CI and are resolved by the engineer in seconds, and route only the genuine judgement to a human. What remains is usually a small fraction — a genuinely novel authentication pattern, a new trust boundary, a design that handles a new class of data — and that volume is one a small team can review properly rather than glancing at. The measure to watch is not how many changes were reviewed but how few needed to be.

Your security scans add six minutes to a nine-minute pipeline. What is the real cost?

Considerably more than six minutes multiplied by the number of runs, because the pipeline has crossed a behavioural threshold. At nine minutes engineers wait — they stay in context, watch the run, and fix a failure immediately. At fifteen they switch to something else, and now every failure costs the switch away, the switch back, and the reconstruction of what they were doing, which research on interruption consistently puts at far more than the interruption itself. So the marginal six minutes buys you a much larger effective cost than the direct arithmetic suggests. There are three second-order effects that are larger still. Batch size grows: when integration is expensive people accumulate more work before pushing, which means more changes per run, harder debugging when something fails, and more merge conflict — a well-documented dynamic in delivery research. Fewer integrations means the feedback loop from Chapter 1 lengthens for every other check too, not only the security ones. And the control becomes a target: a check that visibly costs the team time is the first thing proposed for removal when a deadline approaches, so its long-term survival is at risk regardless of its value. What to do about it rather than accept it: split the checks so a fast high-confidence subset blocks the merge and the slow comprehensive pass runs asynchronously against the main branch, cache and run incrementally against the diff rather than the whole tree, and parallelise the security stage with the test stage rather than after it. The target to hold is total pipeline time, with security's share treated as a budget to spend deliberately.

Why insist on promoting the same artefact rather than rebuilding per environment?

Because rebuilding breaks the chain of evidence between what you tested and what you are running. If staging and production are built separately, then every property you established in staging — the tests that passed, the scan that was clean, the image that was signed, the dependency set that was reviewed — applies to a different artefact, and the production build could differ for reasons nobody controlled: a dependency resolved to a newer version, a base image tag moved, a build tool updated, a transient network failure pulled a different mirror. The whole supply chain question reopens at each rebuild, and the signature and attestation from Chapter 9 become meaningless because they attest to something you are not deploying. There is also a mundane failure that is more common than the sinister one: the build is simply not reproducible, so "it worked in staging" stops being informative and debugging a production-only failure becomes an exercise in comparing two builds. The alternative is straightforward and is standard practice for good reasons: build once, produce an immutable artefact with a digest, sign it, promote that exact digest through each environment, and supply everything environment-specific as configuration injected at deploy time. That makes the promotion decision meaningful, lets admission control verify that the running digest is one that came from your pipeline, and means a rollback is a redeploy of a known artefact rather than a rebuild of an old commit that may no longer produce the same output.

Recap

  1. The loop matters more than the line. Every finding should return to "write" as fast as possible.
  2. Design and deploy carry the most leverage and get the least investment, because the tooling lives in the middle.
  3. No tool finds architectural flaws. That is what threat modelling is for.
  4. A gate is a queue; a guardrail is a rule. Convert gates by encoding the checklist and routing only the judgement.
  5. Every guardrail needs an exception path with an owner and an expiry, or it becomes a gate the moment it is wrong.
  6. Pipeline time is a shared budget, and security spends from the same account as everything else.
  7. Around ten minutes, engineers stop waiting and start switching, and batch sizes grow.
  8. Split fast blocking checks from slow asynchronous ones rather than choosing between speed and depth.
  9. A paved road works when it is the fastest path, not when it is mandatory. Measure adoption.
  10. Build once and promote the same artefact, or what you tested is not what you shipped.
