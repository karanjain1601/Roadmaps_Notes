Automating security work

Automation is how a small security function serves a large engineering organisation, and it is also how a small security function causes a large outage. This chapter is the decision procedure for which side of that you end up on.

~28 min read What to automate · SOAR · auto-remediation · blast radius 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapters 10, 17

20.1The decision

Three properties decide whether an action should be automated, and only the first is usually considered.

Frequency, reversibility, and the cost of being wrong 
FREQUENCY        how often does this happen?
  The usual criterion, and on its own it justifies
  automating things that should not be automated.

REVERSIBILITY    if it was wrong, how fast can it be undone?
  The most important and least considered. Applying a
  network policy is reversible in seconds. Deleting an
  IAM role is reversible in minutes if you have the
  definition and never if you do not. Terminating an
  instance is not reversible at all.

CONSEQUENCE      what does a false positive cost?
  Not what the true positive prevents — what the MISTAKE
  costs, multiplied by how often the detection is wrong,
  multiplied by how many things it can touch before
  anyone notices.

THE PRODUCT IS WHAT MATTERS
  A weekly action that is instantly reversible: automate it
  without much thought.
  A daily action that is irreversible and fires on a 60%-
  precise signal: this will eventually delete production,
  and the fact that it saves time is not a defence.

AND A FOURTH, WHICH IS ABOUT YOU RATHER THAN THE ACTION
  CAN YOU EXPLAIN WHAT IT DID? An automation whose actions
  are not logged, attributable and reconstructable is one
  you cannot debug, cannot audit, and cannot defend after
  it does something surprising.

Should this be automated?

Pick an action and see where it lands. The quadrant is reversibility against consequence.

20.2Levels between manual and autonomous

Most useful automation is not fully autonomous
LevelWhat it doesRight for
EnrichGathers the context a human would gather — owner, recent deploys, related alerts, asset criticality — and attaches itAlmost everything. Pure upside, no risk, and it removes most of the elapsed time in triage
RecommendProposes the action with a one-click approveAnything consequential. Keeps the judgement and removes the typing
PrepareOpens the pull request, drafts the policy, stages the change — but does not apply itRemediation. The reviewer sees exactly what will happen
Act with a holdExecutes after a delay, unless a human cancelsTime-sensitive actions where waiting for approval costs more than a rare wrong action
ActExecutes immediatelyReversible, high-precision, high-frequency actions only
Enrichment is where the returns are, and it is not what gets bought

Look at where the time actually goes in Chapter 17's containment model: the large terms are orienting and deciding, not executing. Automation that gathers context — who owns this service, what deployed in the last hour, what else fired, is this asset important, has this alert fired before and what was the outcome — removes most of that elapsed time and cannot be wrong in a way that breaks anything. It is the highest-return, lowest-risk automation available, and it is consistently under-invested in relative to auto-remediation, which is riskier and saves less.

20.3Blast radius

When the automation is wrong at scale

An automation that acts on a batch can be wrong about all of them before anyone notices.

The automation will be wrong about everything simultaneously

A human making the same decision four hundred times gets tired, notices the third odd case, and stops to ask. An automation applies the same wrong reasoning to all four hundred at machine speed and reports success. That difference is the entire risk, and it is not addressed by making the rule more accurate — it is addressed by bounding what one run can do. Four controls, in order of value: a cap on actions per run, so a bad rule affects ten things rather than everything; an anomaly stop, so a run that is about to act on far more than usual halts and asks; a canary, acting on one and verifying before proceeding; and state captured before every action, so undo is a command rather than an archaeology project. None of these makes the automation better. All of them make being wrong survivable.

20.5Operating the automation

Treat it as production software, because it is 
IT NEEDS THE THINGS YOUR SERVICES NEED
  · version control, review and tests — including tests for
    the case where it should do NOTHING
  · a staging environment, and a dry-run mode that is the
    default until someone opts in
  · monitoring: how often it fires, how often it is
    overridden, how often it is reverted
  · an owner, and a pager

THE METRIC THAT TELLS YOU IT IS WRONG
  THE OVERRIDE RATE. If humans reverse its decision often,
  the rule is wrong and everyone has quietly learned to
  treat its output as a suggestion. Track it per automation
  and treat a rising override rate as an outage.

PRIVILEGE
  Automation runs with permissions to change things, which
  makes it a target and makes its credentials worth more
  than most. Scope per playbook rather than one identity
  that can do everything; use workload identity so nothing
  static exists; and log every action attributably.

AND THE FAILURE NOBODY PLANS FOR
  The automation itself goes wrong at 3am and there is no
  runbook for stopping it. Every automation needs an OFF
  SWITCH that one person can reach without a deploy, and
  everyone on call should know where it is.
Why is reversibility more important than precision when deciding to automate?

Because precision is never one, and reversibility determines what happens on the occasions it is not. A rule at 95% precision that opens a pull request is wrong five times in a hundred and each mistake costs someone a moment's reading. The same rule terminating instances is wrong five times in a hundred and each mistake is an outage you cannot undo, so the expected cost differs by orders of magnitude for identical accuracy. Improving the rule to 99% does not change the shape of that — it reduces the frequency of an unbounded loss, which is a much weaker guarantee than bounding the loss itself. There is a second reason, which is about how you learn. A reversible automation can be deployed, observed and corrected: you watch the override rate, find the cases it gets wrong, and tighten it, and the cost of that learning is small. An irreversible one has to be right before you turn it on, which means it never gets the operational exposure that would have revealed its failure modes, and the first real test is in production on something that matters. So the practical ordering when assessing any proposed automation: establish what a mistake costs and how fast it can be undone first, and only then look at how often the trigger is right. If the answer is that a mistake is expensive and slow to reverse, the correct design is almost always to prepare the action and have a human approve it — which keeps nearly all the time saving, since the work was in gathering context and drafting the change rather than in clicking, while keeping the judgement where the consequences justify it.

Your auto-remediation acted on 400 resources and was wrong about all of them. What should have prevented that?

Not a better rule — bounds on what one run can do. The failure mode here is specific to automation and does not exist with people: a human performing the same task four hundred times notices the third strange case and stops to ask, whereas an automation applies identical reasoning to the whole batch at machine speed and reports success. So the defences are about limiting damage rather than improving accuracy, and there are four, roughly in order of value. A cap on actions per run, so a wrong rule affects ten things and someone investigates, rather than affecting everything. An anomaly stop, halting when a run is about to act on far more items than its own historical baseline — which catches the case where a data-source change or a broken query suddenly matches everything, and that is a more common cause than a subtly wrong rule. A canary: act on one, verify the outcome is what was expected, then proceed. And state captured before every action, so undo is a command rather than reconstruction from memory and backups. Two further points. The time to notice is a multiplier on all of this — an automation running hourly with nobody watching until morning has eight hours to work through everything, so alerting on the automation's own activity matters as much as alerting on what it is responding to. And every automation needs an off switch reachable without a deploy, which is the thing nobody builds and everybody wants at 3am; a rollout that cannot be stopped by one person in one command is a rollout that will run to completion while people are still discussing it.

Why is alert enrichment better value than auto-remediation?

Because it attacks the part of the work that actually takes the time, and it cannot break anything. Look at where the elapsed time goes in an incident: the containment model in Chapter 17 puts most of it in orienting and deciding — working out who owns the affected service, what changed recently, whether this has fired before and what happened, how important the asset is, what else is going on. Executing the action, once decided, is usually seconds. Auto-remediation optimises the seconds and leaves the minutes untouched, while introducing the possibility of doing the wrong thing at scale. Enrichment optimises the minutes: the alert arrives with the owner attached, the recent deployments listed, related alerts correlated, the asset's criticality noted, and the previous outcomes for this rule linked — so the responder starts from a position it would have taken them twenty minutes to reach. And the risk profile is completely different. The worst case for enrichment is that some context is missing or wrong, and the human notices, because they are reading it. The worst case for auto-remediation is §20.3's panel. This is also the honest reason to be sceptical of SOAR products sold on their playbook automation: the playbooks are the part that needs the most care and delivers the least, while the integration and enrichment layer — connecting the alert to the asset inventory, the deployment history and the ownership data — is the part that pays immediately. Buy for the second and be conservative with the first.

Recap

  1. Frequency, reversibility and cost-of-error decide it, and only the first is usually considered.
  2. Reversibility matters more than precision, because precision is never one.
  3. Most useful automation is not autonomous. Enrich, recommend and prepare cover most of the value.
  4. Enrichment is the highest return and lowest risk, because orienting is where the time goes.
  5. An automation is wrong about everything at once. A human notices the third odd case.
  6. Cap actions per run and stop on anomalous volume — these bound the damage rather than improving the rule.
  7. Capture state before acting, so undo is a command rather than archaeology.
  8. Track the override rate. A rising one means the rule is wrong and people have stopped trusting it.
  9. Scope credentials per playbook, because automation holds permissions worth stealing.
  10. Every automation needs an off switch reachable without a deploy.
