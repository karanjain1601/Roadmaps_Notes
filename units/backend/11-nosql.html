NoSQL and the CAP trade-off

Six families of database that gave something up to gain something else. The interesting question is never "SQL or NoSQL" — it is which specific guarantee you are willing to surrender, and whether you actually get paid for surrendering it.

What actually justifies leaving the relational default
  • Write volume beyond one machine. Not read volume — replicas handle that. Sustained writes past what a large server can absorb genuinely need a different architecture.
  • A query shape relational is bad at. Variable-depth graph traversal, relevance-ranked text search, time-window aggregation over billions of points.
  • A latency floor. Sub-millisecond lookups mean an in-memory key-value store, because a networked disk-backed database cannot get there.
  • Genuinely schemaless data. Per-tenant custom fields, third-party payloads whose shape you do not control.

What does not justify it: "joins are slow" (chapter 9 — it is a missing index), "we might scale one day" (you will not, and if you do you will have money and evidence), "the schema might change" (migrations exist, and a document store just moves the schema into your application where nothing enforces it), and "it is web scale". Postgres comfortably runs applications with tens of thousands of writes per second on ordinary hardware, and does JSON, full-text search, time-series partitioning and geospatial queries competently enough that adding a second database is often a net loss.

CAP, stated properly

The popular version — "pick two of consistency, availability and partition tolerance" — is misleading, because partitions are not something you choose. Networks fail. The theorem is narrower and more useful than the slogan.

What it actually says

When a network partition occurs, a distributed system must choose between remaining consistent and remaining available. That is the whole theorem. It says nothing at all about behaviour when the network is healthy, and it is not a menu of three from which you select two — partition tolerance is a fact of the world, so the real choice is CP or AP during a partition. A single-node database is not "CA"; it simply has no partitions to survive.

Same failure, two policies
PACELC — the half of the trade CAP omits

Partitions are rare. The more consequential trade happens the rest of the time, and PACELC names it: if there is a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, choose Latency or Consistency. Every strongly consistent read must reach a quorum, which costs a round trip to other nodes — so consistency is not free on a healthy network either, it is simply billed in milliseconds instead of errors. Postgres with synchronous replication is PC/EC; Cassandra with tunable quorums is PA/EL by default; DynamoDB lets you choose per request, which is why its API has an eventually consistent read flag with a different price.

Replication and quorums

Store every value on N nodes, require W of them to acknowledge a write and R to answer a read. Three numbers, one inequality, and most of distributed data consistency falls out of it.

$$R + W > N \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{every read set intersects every write set}$$

If the read quorum and the write quorum are together larger than the number of replicas, they must share at least one node — and that node has the latest write. This is why the inequality gives strong consistency, and why violating it does not.

Read replicas produce the same problem without a NoSQL database

Send writes to the primary and reads to a replica, and you have built an eventually consistent system. Replication lag is usually milliseconds and occasionally seconds, and the symptom is distinctive: a user updates their profile, is redirected, and sees the old value — because the redirect's read went to a replica that had not caught up. The fixes are the same ones distributed databases use: route a user's reads to the primary for a few seconds after they write, or pass the write's log position with the read and wait for the replica to reach it. Chapter 19. The point is that "eventual consistency" is not something you opt into by choosing Cassandra; you opt into it the moment you add a replica.

The cost of a second database

Every additional store is another thing to back up, monitor, secure, upgrade, size, and page someone about at three in the morning. It is another client library, another connection pool, another failure mode, and — the expensive one — another place the truth might live, with no transaction spanning them. Writing to Postgres and Elasticsearch in one request means designing for the case where the first succeeds and the second does not. That is a real distributed-systems problem, and chapter 18's outbox pattern is the standard answer. Polyglot persistence is often right; it is never free, and the second database should be justified with the same rigour as the first.

Self-check

  1. Why is "pick two of C, A and P" a misleading statement of CAP?
    Because partition tolerance is not a choice — networks fail whether or not you planned for it, so any distributed system must tolerate partitions and the menu really has one item. The theorem's actual content is about behaviour during a partition: a system must either refuse requests it cannot serve consistently, or serve them and risk divergence. It says nothing about the healthy case, which is where systems spend nearly all their time.
  2. What does PACELC add?
    The healthy-network half. Even with no partition, a strongly consistent read must reach a quorum, so consistency costs a round trip — meaning the trade is latency against consistency the rest of the time. That is the more consequential choice in practice, because partitions are rare and every request pays the latency. It also explains why systems like DynamoDB expose the decision per request rather than as a global setting.
  3. Why does R + W > N give strong consistency?
    Because two sets drawn from N nodes whose sizes sum to more than N must overlap in at least one node — pigeonhole. That shared node participated in the latest acknowledged write, so it holds the current value, and the read can identify it by version. Drop below the inequality and the read set can miss the write set entirely, returning a value that is stale with no way to know.
  4. N=3, W=3, R=1. What have you optimised, and what have you broken?
    Reads are as fast and available as possible — one node answers, and two can fail without affecting reads. R+W=4>3, so consistency still holds. But writes now require every replica, so a single node being down or slow blocks all writes entirely, and write latency is bounded by the slowest of the three. It is a defensible choice for a read-heavy workload that can tolerate write outages, and a poor one otherwise.
  5. A user updates their profile, is redirected, and sees the old value. What happened and what are the two fixes?
    The write went to the primary and the subsequent read went to a replica that had not yet applied it — replication lag. Either route that user's reads to the primary for a few seconds after they write, or capture the write's log position and have the read wait until the replica has reached it. Note this happens with ordinary Postgres read replicas; you do not need a distributed database to inherit eventual consistency, only a second copy.
  6. Someone proposes MongoDB because "the schema might change". What is the counter-argument?
    The schema does not disappear, it moves — out of the database, where it was declared and enforced, and into every piece of code that reads the data, where it is implicit and unenforced. You still have to handle documents written under the old shape, except now nothing tells you which shapes exist. Relational migrations are a solved problem, and a JSONB column covers the genuinely open-ended fields while keeping constraints on everything else. Flexibility is a real benefit for genuinely unknown data; it is not a reason to give up validation for data you do know.
