Observability

Every chapter so far has assumed you can tell what the system is doing. You cannot, by default. The gap between "the service is up" and "we know why that request took four seconds" is closed deliberately, and mostly by decisions made before the incident.

RED — for services
Rate, Errors, Duration. Three metrics per endpoint, and they cover almost every question you will ask during an incident about a request-serving system.
USE — for resources
Utilisation, Saturation, Errors. Applied to CPU, memory, disk, connection pools and queues. Saturation is the one people omit and the one that predicts failure — a pool at 100% utilisation with an empty queue is fine; the queue length is the warning.
Cardinality
A metric's cost is the product of its label values. http_requests{method, status, endpoint} is perhaps 400 series. Add user_id and it is 400 × your user count — millions of time series, and an invoice to match. Identifiers belong in logs and traces, never in metric labels.

Three rules about percentiles
  • Never alert on an average. It is dominated by the bulk of fast requests and moves only when almost everything is broken. Alert on p95 or p99, and chart p50 alongside so you can tell a general slowdown from a growing tail.
  • You cannot average percentiles. The mean of each instance's p99 is not the fleet's p99, and averaging over time is equally wrong. Aggregate the underlying histogram buckets, which is what Prometheus histograms and similar exist for.
  • Your percentile is not your user's. A page making twelve calls hits the p99 far more often than one per cent of the time — the chapter 17 arithmetic again. The number that matters is the percentage of user journeys that were slow, not of requests.

Tracing

A metric says the endpoint is slow. A log line says one request failed. A trace says where the four seconds went, across every service the request touched — which is the question you actually have.

the parts of tracing that break in practice
context propagation
    the trace id travels in the  traceparent  header (W3C Trace Context)
    and must be forwarded by every service, every HTTP client, every
    queue message and every background job. One service that drops it
    splits the trace in two, and the half you are looking at will not
    contain the problem.

sampling
    head-based: decide at the start, cheap, and it discards the rare
      slow request you most wanted to see.
    tail-based: buffer the whole trace, keep it if it was slow or
      errored. Far more useful, and needs a collector that can hold
      spans in memory until the trace completes.
    the pragmatic default: sample 1% of successes and 100% of errors
      and anything over a latency threshold.

span attributes
    the http status, the SQL statement shape, the queue name, the row
    count, the cache hit or miss. A span with only a name and a
    duration tells you where the time went and not why.

exemplars
    a link from a histogram bucket to an actual trace in that bucket.
    this is what turns "the p99 is 3 seconds" into "here is one of
    those requests" in a single click, and it is the highest-value
    integration to set up.

SLOs and alerting

SLI
A measurement of something users care about. "The proportion of checkout requests that succeed within 500 ms." Note that it is a ratio, and note what it is measured at — the edge, not inside one service.
SLO
A target for that indicator over a window. "99.5% over 30 days." The number should be chosen from what users need and what the system can sustain, not by adding nines until it sounds impressive.
Error budget
The complement: 0.5% of 30 days is about 3.6 hours of failure you are permitted. That budget is the point of the whole exercise — it converts reliability from an argument into arithmetic. Budget remaining means ship; budget exhausted means stop shipping features and fix reliability.
Burn rate
How fast the budget is being consumed relative to the window. Alert on burn rate rather than raw error rate: a 14× burn over an hour is a page, a 2× burn over six hours is a ticket. This is what stops both alert fatigue and slow-burning outages nobody noticed.

The test for an alert

Every page should be able to answer three questions: is a user affected right now, is it going to get worse on its own, and is there something a person can do about it at this hour? If any answer is no, it is a ticket or a dashboard, not a page. The failure mode here is cumulative and quiet: an alert that fires spuriously once a week trains everyone to acknowledge without reading, and by the time it fires for a real incident the response is already automatic. An alert nobody trusts is worse than no alert, because it also consumes the attention that a real one needs — the same argument as the flaky tests in chapter 16, applied to people at three in the morning.

Self-check

  1. Why is a user id an acceptable field in a log and a serious problem in a metric label?
    Because metrics are stored as one time series per unique combination of label values. A metric with method, status and endpoint might be a few hundred series; adding a user id multiplies that by your user count, producing millions of series, enormous memory use in the metrics store, and slow or impossible queries. Logs and traces are per-event records where a high-cardinality field costs one field. Metrics answer "how many"; logs and traces answer "which one".
  2. Your average latency is 140 ms and users say the site is slow. Explain.
    The mean is dominated by the majority of fast requests, so a small fraction of very slow ones barely moves it — 1% of requests at four seconds adds about 40 ms to an average of 100. Meanwhile that 1% is thousands of real people per hour, and because a page makes many calls, the proportion of page loads touching a slow request is far higher than 1%. Look at p95, p99 and the count of requests over a threshold, and measure journeys rather than requests.
  3. Why can you not average the p99 across ten instances?
    Because a percentile is a property of a distribution, not a quantity that adds. If nine instances are idle with a p99 of 50 ms and one is overloaded at 5 seconds, the mean of the p99s is about 545 ms, which describes nothing — the fleet's true p99 depends on how the traffic was distributed. The correct approach is to aggregate the underlying histogram buckets and compute the percentile from the combined distribution, which is exactly what histogram metric types are for.
  4. What does a trace tell you that metrics and logs cannot?
    Where the time went, across service boundaries, for one specific request. Metrics tell you the endpoint is slow but not which part; logs tell you what one service did but not how its time related to the others. A trace shows the whole causal tree with durations, so forty small sequential database spans reveal an N+1, four sequential service calls reveal a fan-out that should be parallel, and one dominant span points straight at the dependency. The value depends entirely on context propagation working everywhere.
  5. What is an error budget and what is it for?
    The amount of unreliability an SLO permits — 99.5% over thirty days allows about 3.6 hours of failure. Its purpose is to turn reliability from a matter of opinion into a shared number: while budget remains, shipping features is the right call; once it is exhausted, reliability work takes priority until it recovers. That converts an argument between product and engineering into a policy both agreed to in advance, which is most of the value.
  6. Why alert on burn rate rather than error rate?
    Because a raw error rate has no sense of proportion — the same threshold either pages constantly during normal noise or misses a slow burn that quietly consumes the month's budget. Burn rate measures consumption relative to the budget and the window, so a fast burn triggers a page and a slow one raises a ticket. Combining a short and a long window also filters brief spikes that resolve themselves, which is where most spurious pages come from.
