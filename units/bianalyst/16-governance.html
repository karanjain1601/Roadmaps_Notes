    Attributes multiply, they do not add

    Sex plus full birth date plus full postcode gives 1.2×10¹¹ combinations for 67 million people, so 99.95% of the population is unique. No name is needed and no name was kept.

    Which is why a column that looks harmless on its own is not harmless in a table. "Just the district" multiplies the space by 2,900, and the harm of a field depends entirely on what it sits next to.

    Two coarsening steps, and the analysis barely notices

    Birth date to birth year, and postcode to district. Uniqueness goes from 99.9% to 0.0000% and the cell size from under one person to 116.

    The analytical cost is close to nothing. Almost no question anyone asks needs a birth date rather than a birth year, and the ones that do — cohort work by signup week, say — do not also need the postcode.

    Which reframes the privacy conversation usefully. It is not usually a trade between analysis and safety; it is a habit of keeping the finest grain available because it was there.

    A governance question anybody can answer

    With binary attributes you can carry twenty-one; with day-level ones, two. So the useful question is not "is this field sensitive" — which requires judgement and invites argument — but "how many levels does it have", which is a COUNT(DISTINCT).

    Multiply the distinct counts of every column in the table. If the product exceeds your population, the table identifies people, whatever it is called.

    INCENTIVES

    What a metric does once people are measured on it

    Real improvement has a ceiling. Gaming does not. Everything demanded past the ceiling arrives as gaming by construction.

    Goodhart's law as arithmetic rather than as a warning

    At a 2% target, 98% of the measured gain is real. At 40%, 25% — and the real gain has flatlined at 10.0%, which is the ceiling. Nothing about the people changed.

    The measured gain tracks the target almost exactly at every level, which is the part that makes this hard to see from inside. The metric always says the target was met.

    Two regimes, and only one of them is inversion

    At equal cost the correlation decays monotonically — 0.95, 0.83, 0.59, 0.39, 0.20, 0.09 — and lands near zero. The metric does not invert; it decays to noise. Everyone reaches the target and the ranking stops carrying information about who improved.

    Inversion needs one more condition: the shortcut has to be cheaper than the real thing. At 0.4× the cost, a 25% target gives r = −0.24. The agents scoring highest are then the ones who took the shortcut hardest, so the metric does not merely stop working — it starts promoting the wrong people.

    I had assumed inversion was the general case. It is not: it is what happens when governance is absent, and the ordinary case is a metric quietly becoming uninformative.

    The lever is the price of the shortcut, not the size of the target

    You cannot raise the ceiling on genuine improvement by asking for more — that is what a ceiling means. What you can change is the cost of the alternative.

    Three things do it. Audit, so gaming carries a chance of being caught. Definitions that are hard to satisfy accidentally — chapter 7's work, since a metric with five undefined choices offers five shortcuts. And a paired metric that moves the other way, so the cheapest route to the first number damages the second.

    ACCESS

    Who can see what, and why the default matters

    Four access models
    ModelDefaultFailure mode
    Open by defaultEveryone sees everythingThe re-identification arithmetic above, at scale
    Closed by defaultRequest per datasetShadow copies in spreadsheets
    TieredAggregates open, row-level requestedThe tier boundary is where the arguments happen
    Purpose-boundAccess tied to a stated usePurposes drift, and nobody revisits them
    Closed-by-default produces the copies it was meant to prevent

    Chapter 14 measured the queue effect: a rollout that removes 78% of items frees 19% of hours. Access requests behave the same way. If the wait for a dataset exceeds the patience of the person asking, they will use whatever they already have — an old export, a colleague's extract, a spreadsheet from March.

    Those copies are outside every control you built, do not expire, and are the actual privacy exposure. So an access policy has to be judged on what people do when it says no, not on what it forbids.

  1. Publish the distinct count of every column in shared datasets. It is the input to the arithmetic above and it costs one query.
  2. Generalise at the view, not the source. Birth year for the open tier, birth date behind a request, and the same underlying table.
  3. Expire exports. A dated file that stops opening is worth more than a policy nobody reads.
  4. Make the fast path the safe path. If the governed route is slower than the ungoverned one, governance is a tax on the conscientious.
  5. Pair every target metric with a guard metric. The cheapest route to the first should damage the second.
  6. Review metrics that stopped varying. A number where everyone now hits target has usually decayed to noise, and the flatness is the symptom.

    7. FAIRNESS

    Three things worth checking

    Who is missing from the data
    The people who did not complete signup, did not consent to tracking, or use a channel you do not instrument. They are absent from every segment cut and from every model, and their absence is invisible in the data by definition — you can only find it by counting against an external total.
    Whether the segments you cut by are proxies for something else
    Postcode is a proxy for income and often for ethnicity. A model or a targeting rule that uses it inherits that, whether or not anyone intended it. Chapter 11's question applies: who chose to be in this group?
    Whether the error rate is equal across groups
    An overall accuracy figure is an average, and chapter 4 showed what averages hide. A model that is 94% accurate overall can be 97% on the majority group and 71% on a small one, and the aggregate will never show it — the same margin arithmetic chapter 13 measured.
    The analyst's specific responsibility

    Most of this chapter is somebody else's policy. The part that is not: you are usually the only person who knows what is actually in the table. Whether the postcode is full or district, whether the "anonymous" export has a birth date in it, whether the segment with 71% accuracy exists at all.

    Nobody else can raise it, because nobody else can see it.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions before sharing a dataset

  7. What is the product of the distinct counts? Above your population, it identifies people.
  8. Does anything need day-level dates? Two such columns exhaust the budget on their own.
  9. What will people do if the answer is no? Shadow copies are the real exposure.
  10. Is anyone measured on this number? If so, expect the correlation with the objective to decay.
  11. Is the shortcut cheaper than the real improvement? That is the difference between decay and inversion.
