Slowly changing dimensions

    THE QUESTION

    What happens to last year when something changes today

    Two defensible answers, and you must pick one

    A customer was SMB in January and is Enterprise now. When you report January's revenue by segment, does their January spend appear under SMB or under Enterprise?

    Both are legitimate. As it was answers "how did the SMB segment perform last January". As it is answers "how much did today's Enterprise customers spend last January" — a cohort question, and a genuinely useful one.

    What is not legitimate is not knowing which one your warehouse does. That is the situation most organisations are in, and the rest of this chapter measures what it costs.

    The standard types
    TypeWhat it doesHistoryReproducible
    Type 1Overwrite the attributeNoneNo
    Type 2New row, with valid-from and valid-toFullYes
    Type 3Add a "previous value" columnOne step backNo
    Type 4Current row plus a separate history tableFullYes
    Type 6Type 2 rows carrying the current value tooBoth viewsYes
    Type 6 is the one worth knowing about

    It keeps Type 2 rows and adds a column holding today's value on every historical row. You get both answers from one join: segment_at_time and segment_current. Most questions want the first; cohort questions want the second; and nobody has to guess which the table gives them.

    History that moves

    The signs differ, so you cannot correct for it

    Two effects fight each other. Today's Enterprise cohort is larger than January's, which pushes its historical revenue up — but most of them were spending £90 back then rather than £2,600, which pushes it down much harder. For Mid the population effect wins instead, and the number goes up 27%.

    So the bias is not a direction you could adjust away. It is per segment, and it depends on the interaction between who moved and how much they were spending at the time.

    Overwriting exaggerates growth rather than hiding it

    Enterprise revenue grew 61.9% over the two years. Type 1 reports 81.6% — twenty points too high.

    The two agree exactly on the latest month, because today's segment is today's segment. The entire error sits in the base, which Type 1 back-fills with customers who were spending £90 at the time.

    Which is worth stating plainly because the instinct runs the other way: people expect overwriting to flatter the present. It flatters the trend, by deflating the past.

    This is the failure that destroys trust

    The same historical month reads 6.6% different when re-run two years on, with no new data, and the gap widens every month you wait: 2.0%, 3.9%, 6.6%.

    Someone quotes a figure from a report they ran in March. It no longer matches. Nothing is broken, nobody made a mistake, and there is no satisfying explanation available in the moment. After that happens twice, people start keeping their own spreadsheets — which is the real cost, and it does not appear on any storage bill.

    Type 2 gives the same number every time, because it stores what was true.

    The question is not "do we need history"

    At 2% annual migration the restatement is 2% and nobody would notice. At 30% it is 17%, and the growth figure is out by 49 percentage points.

    So the question to ask before choosing a type is how often does this attribute change — which is chapter 5's question again, arriving from a different direction. Rarely-changing attributes can be overwritten safely. Frequently-changing ones cannot, and the same attributes tend to be the ones people slice by.

    The range penalty is flat; the broadcast penalty is a cliff

    The as-of join costs 2.60× an equality join at every dimension size, because probing an interval is intrinsically dearer than hashing a key. Resolving the surrogate key once at load time and storing it on the fact removes that penalty entirely, and it is free at query time because the work moved to the load.

    The broadcast penalty is separate and the surrogate key cannot touch it. At three years the dimension still fits and Type 2 costs 1.03× Type 1. At four years it does not fit, and the same surrogate key leaves you at 4.35×.

    I had expected the surrogate key to settle the whole question. It settles half of it.

    The fourth year is not special

    Three years of history costs 1.03× Type 1; the fourth costs 4.35×. Not because that year contains anything different, but because it is the one that crosses the memory limit.

    Archive older versions to a cold table that only long-range queries touch, and the hot dimension stays on the cheap side of chapter 5's cliff. That is a partitioning decision, not a modelling one, and it is invisible to everyone writing queries.

    So it is not history against performance

    At a horizon that fits in memory, Type 2 with the key resolved at load costs 1.03× Type 1 and gives reproducible history. The naive as-of join costs 2.67×, which is where the folk belief that "Type 2 is slow" comes from.

    The trade is history against two specific costs, each with its own fix: resolve the surrogate key at load, and keep the hot dimension under the broadcast limit. Do both and the performance argument disappears. Do neither and it is real.

    PRACTICE

    Getting it right

  1. Decide per attribute, not per dimension. A customer's name can be overwritten; their segment, tier and owning region cannot. Mixing both in one Type 2 table means a new row every time somebody fixes a typo.
  2. Resolve the surrogate key at load. One lookup when the fact row is written, and every downstream query becomes an equality join.
  3. Use a far-future end date rather than NULL. 9999-12-31 keeps the range predicate simple and stops the current row from being silently excluded by a BETWEEN.
  4. Add an is_current flag. Most queries want the current row and should not have to express that as a date comparison.
  5. Watch the hot dimension against the broadcast limit, and archive beyond it. That is the fourth-year cliff.
  6. Type 6 if you can afford the column. Both the as-was and as-is answers from one join, and nobody has to guess which the table gives them.
    7. The late-arriving fact

    A transaction dated last Tuesday arrives today, after the customer changed segment on Wednesday. The as-of lookup must use the transaction date, not today's — which is chapter 4's time-basis question with real consequences.

    Resolving the surrogate key at load makes this correct automatically if the load looks up by transaction date. If it looks up by load date, you have quietly built Type 1 with extra steps, and the symptom is history that stops being reproducible for exactly the rows that arrived late.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a dimension

  7. How often does each attribute change? At 2% a year overwriting is fine; at 30% it misstates growth by 49 points.
  8. Does re-running last quarter's report give the same number? If not, you are on Type 1 whether or not anyone chose it.
  9. Which question does your segment column answer — as-was or as-is? Both are valid; not knowing is not.
  10. Is the surrogate key resolved at load or at query time? The difference is 2.60× on every query.
  11. How many rows is the hot dimension, against the broadcast limit? The fourth year was a 4.2× step.
  12. Does the load look up by transaction date or by load date? The second one is Type 1 in disguise.
