CHAPTER 3

Metrics, KPIs and definitions

Every lever inside a segment has elasticity exactly equal to that segment's revenue share — so a 3% site-wide improvement beats a 10% improvement on the segment with the most traffic. And a conversion rate can be raised 58% by cutting traffic, losing 1,600 conversions on the way.

    THE ARTEFACT

    Writing a definition that survives a meeting

    Chapter 1 measured what an undefined metric costs: four honest readings of the same thing, 4.0× apart, against a signal of 2.6%.

    The population
    Which rows count, and explicitly which do not. Internal accounts, test orders, refunded transactions, bot traffic. The exclusions are where two implementations diverge, and they are always the part nobody writes down.
    The grain
    One row per what? Per user, per session, per order line? Chapter 4 is about why this is the decision everything else follows from, and about the fan-out that silently doubles a total when it is wrong.
    The time basis
    Event time or processing time; which timezone; whether a refund on the 3rd reduces January or March. "Revenue" measured on order date and on settlement date will never reconcile, and both are correct.
    The window
    Trailing 28 days, calendar month, fiscal period. Calendar months differ in length by 11%, which is larger than most of the changes anyone reports.
    The owner
    One named person who decides when the definition changes. Not a team — a person, because "the data team owns it" resolves to nobody at the moment it matters.
    The decision it serves
    What someone does differently at different values. This is the field that most often reveals the metric should not exist.
    The test is reconstruction, not agreement

    A definition is finished when two analysts who have not spoken can implement it independently and get the same number. Agreement in a meeting is much weaker: everyone nods at "active users" because everyone is nodding at their own version.

    The cheap way to run that test is to hand the written definition to someone else and ask them to write the query. Where they ask a clarifying question, the definition has a hole — and it is faster to find it that way than in a quarterly review.

    Elasticity equals revenue share, exactly

    Every lever inside a segment — visitors, conversion, order value — has the same elasticity, and it is that segment's revenue share. Not its traffic share. Not its conversion rate. Not how much headroom it appears to have.

    A site-wide factor has elasticity 1.000, because it multiplies every branch.

    This falls straight out of the arithmetic once you see it, and almost nobody computes it before assigning a team. The tree gets drawn, the boxes look equally important, and the squad goes to the box with the most traffic in it.

    The comparison teams get wrong

    Three per cent everywhere beats ten per cent on the biggest segment

    New visitors are 2.3 times more numerous than returning ones and produce 17% of revenue. So the same ten per cent improvement is worth 3.8 times more on returning visitors — and a mere three per cent site-wide beats both.

    Traffic share is not revenue share, and the box on the whiteboard does not tell you which you are looking at.

    So the tree does not rank your options

    Elasticity is fixed by share, which means the structure alone can never tell you what to work on. Priority is elasticity multiplied by how far you can actually move the lever, and the second term is exactly the number a metric tree cannot give you.

    What the tree is good for: making the shares visible, so that nobody proposes a 10% conversion programme on a 16% segment believing it is the main event. Use it to eliminate, not to choose.

    A 58% improvement that costs 1,600 conversions

    Removing the display channel entirely takes the conversion rate from 1.921% to 3.027% and loses 1,600 conversions. The KPI goes up and the business goes down.

    Nobody has to be dishonest for this to happen. A team told to get conversion to 2.5% can reach it by cutting 66% of display traffic, report a 30% improvement, and destroy 1,048 conversions in the process. The metric asked for it.

    Never ship a ratio without its numerator beside it

    Pairing the rate with the count it is built from catches every one of these cases: rate up, numerator down is the pattern to escalate. It costs one extra line on the dashboard and needs no judgement from whoever reads it.

    And it distinguishes cleanly from the real thing. A genuine 12% lift takes the rate to 2.152% and conversions to 20,440 — both up. The guardrail does not need to be clever; it needs the second number to be on the page.

    The same shape appears everywhere

    Average order value improves by turning away small orders. Support satisfaction improves by closing hard tickets slowly enough that people give up. Cost per acquisition improves by abandoning expensive but profitable channels. Every ratio has a denominator someone can influence, and someone eventually will.

    The general form of the fix: publish a ratio with the numerator and the denominator, and set the target on the one you actually want to move. If you want more conversions, target conversions.

    CHOOSING

    North stars and the metrics around them

  1. A north star should be a count you want more of, not a rate. The demo above is why: a count has no denominator to game.
  2. It should be something the team can move within a quarter. Annual revenue is a result, not a north star — nobody can attribute this week's work to it.
  3. It should lead, not lag. If it only moves after the customer has already decided, it cannot steer anything.
  4. Pair it with two or three guardrails, chosen as the things that would get worse if someone optimised the north star carelessly. Write them down at the same time or they never get written.
  5. Publish the elasticities. Once a tree exists, the shares are free to compute, and they stop the 10%-on-a-16%-segment conversation before it starts.
  6. Re-derive the tree when the shares move. A tree built when returning traffic was 40% of revenue gives the wrong priorities at 66%.

    7. JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about your metric set

  7. Could a stranger implement your top metric from the written definition? Hand it over and count the clarifying questions.
  8. Do you know each segment's revenue share? That number is every lever's elasticity, and it is usually not the one people assume.
  9. Is your headline KPI a rate? If so, is the numerator on the same page?
  10. Which guardrails did you write down when the target was set? Ones added after a problem are a post-mortem, not a guardrail.
  11. Who is the named owner of each definition? If the answer is a team, it is nobody.
