    THE JOB

    What business intelligence is for

    A number is worth what it changes

    Business intelligence is the practice of getting a decision made on evidence rather than on the loudest opinion in the room. Everything else — the warehouse, the semantic layer, the dashboards, the charts — is machinery in service of that.

    Which gives a hard test that most BI work fails: name the decision this changes, and name what a different number would have made someone do differently. If nobody can, you have built a report, not intelligence.

  1. The output is a decision, not an artefact. A dashboard that is beautiful, correct, fast and unopened has produced nothing.
  2. Being right is necessary and not sufficient. A correct number delivered after the decision, or in a form nobody can act on, is indistinguishable from no number.
  3. Ambiguity is more dangerous than error. An error gets found. A metric that means four things gets used for years, and this chapter measures what that costs.
  4. Your credibility is the asset. One number that turns out to mean something other than what the room assumed will cost you more than a dozen slow queries.

    The spread is 113 times the signal

    The widest reading is 4.0 times the narrowest. The best month-on-month change any definition reports is 2.6%. Changing which definition you use moves the number 113 times further than a very good quarter does.

    So an undefined metric cannot carry the argument it is being used for. Whoever picks the definition has more influence on the answer than whatever the business actually did — and in most organisations nobody picked it, four people each assumed a different one, and all four are quoting "MAU" in the same meeting.

    Note also that the definitions disagree on magnitude even where they agree on direction: one reports 2.6% growth and another 1.2%, so the same month reads as twice the progress depending on which query someone ran. That is enough to decide a budget the wrong way while everyone believes they agree.

    They are not nested, which is why the argument never resolves

    If the definitions were nested — each a stricter subset of the last — then two people quoting different ones would at least be describing the same users more or less generously. They are not. 36,047 users have a long session and no core action; 34,679 have both.

    Persistent mobile sessions produce activity without a login. Integrations fire core actions with no interactive session at all. Every real system has these paths, so the definitions carve out different overlapping groups rather than concentric ones.

    Which means the resolution is never "let's use the stricter one". It is to write down which group the decision is actually about, and then pick.

    What a defined metric looks like

    A name, a precise population, a time window, a grain, an owner, and the decision it exists to support. "Weekly active accounts: distinct account IDs with at least one completed order-create event in the trailing 7 days, excluding internal and test accounts, owned by Growth, used to set fulfilment staffing."

    That is four times as long as "WAU" and it is the entire difference between a number and an argument. Chapter 3 turns this into a working practice; chapter 7 puts it somewhere the tools can enforce.

    Value saturates and cost does not

    Going from 10 dashboards to 200 raises decisions from 8.9 to 26.0 a week — 2.9× — while maintenance rises 20×. That is the whole problem in one line.

    Note what is not claimed: total decisions do rise. "Delete dashboards" is not automatically right, and a portfolio review that argues from the total will lose. What falls is the return per hour of upkeep.

    There is no portfolio size that maximises average return

    Average return falls monotonically from the very first dashboard. Optimising it always says "fewer", right down to one — which is why that framing never wins an argument with the people who want another dashboard.

    The rule that gives a defensible answer is marginal: at a bar of one decision per maintenance hour you stop at 24 dashboards; at half that bar, 41; at a quarter, 58.

    What makes this usable is that it names the bar rather than the answer. A stakeholder can argue with "is one decision per maintenance hour the right threshold" in a way they cannot argue with "we have too many dashboards".

    The number to take to a review

    At 200 dashboards the busiest twenty carry 73% of all views and 103 are never opened in a week. That second figure is the one that moves a meeting — not "we have too many", but "these 103 were not opened once and someone maintains every one of them".

    CONSEQUENCE

    What ambiguity actually costs

    Arguments that cannot be settled
    Two people with different definitions produce different numbers, both correct. The meeting resolves it by seniority rather than evidence, which is precisely the failure BI exists to prevent.
    Decisions made on the wrong population
    The definitions here were not nested: 36,047 users had a long session and no core action. A retention programme aimed at "actives" will target a different group depending on which query someone ran.
    Trends that are definition changes
    A metric quietly redefined during a migration produces a step change that gets attributed to the business. Chapter 16 covers the lineage that makes this visible; without it, the step is indistinguishable from a result.
    Reconciliation work forever
    Once two systems report different numbers for the same name, someone spends a day a month explaining the gap. That cost recurs and never appears on any plan.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions to ask on your first week

  5. Pick your top metric and ask three people to define it. If you get three answers, you have found the most valuable work available to you.
  6. Which decision does each dashboard support? The ones with no answer are the ones to propose retiring first.
  7. How many dashboards were opened at all last month? At 200, half were not.
  8. Who owns each metric definition? Not the dashboard, the definition. Usually nobody.
  9. What is your bar for a new dashboard? Naming it turns every future request into a conversation about the bar rather than about you.
