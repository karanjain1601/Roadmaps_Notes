    It manufactures certainty exactly where there is none

    Three of six findings are genuinely undetermined: the range spans the bar, so the analysis does not decide the question. A point estimate returns a definite verdict for all six.

    Note where the extra three verdicts come from. They are not spread evenly — they are precisely the cases where the honest answer was "we do not know", so the point estimate adds confidence only where confidence is absent.

    A coin flip reported as a decision

    For the price rise, 53.3% of the range agrees with the verdict the point estimate gives. That is very nearly a coin flip, and the summary contains no trace of the 47% of plausible answers pointing the other way.

    The first row is worth noticing too. The point estimate sits at the top of its range because the naive comparison is the estimate people quote — chapter 11's 9.7× overstatement. So the summary is confident, and confident at the extreme end of what the data allows.

    The analysis is least decisive at intermediate bars

    Go falls monotonically (4, 2, 0, 0, 0) and no rises monotonically (0, 1, 2, 3, 4). But undetermined peaks in the middle: 2, 3, 4, 3, 2.

    That has to happen, because findings move go → undetermined → no as the bar rises. And it means the analysis is least decisive at intermediate thresholds — which is exactly where most real decision bars sit. Very lenient and very demanding bars are easy to answer; the ones people actually use are not.

    Which gives the sentence worth saying out loud: "this is a go if your bar is 5%, undetermined at 15%, and a no above 25%." It returns the decision to the person whose bar it is, without pretending the range is narrower than it is.

    What each reporting style delivers
    StyleDefinite verdictsVerdicts the analysis supports

    Half the service, the same knowledge

    Stating the range answers three questions and admits three. It feels like less service and it is the same amount of knowledge.

    The difference is entirely in who carries the uncertainty — the analyst who hides it, or the decision-maker who owns it. And the second arrangement is the one where a bad outcome does not arrive with the words "but the report said".

    A one-page summary loses far less than people fear

    The three most valuable facts carry 68% of the value — the same concentration chapter 1 found in dashboard readership, and the same reason it matters.

    But the first three in discovery order carry 13% — five times less. Because what you learn first is the setup: how many customers, how many months, which sources needed reconciling. All true, all necessary to have done, and none of it is the finding.

    Not a style preference about busy executives

    Six facts in value order reach 90% fidelity; eleven are needed in discovery order. Half the length for the same content.

    Which reframes the usual advice. "Put the finding first" is not a courtesy to people who will not read past the first paragraph — it is that the same page carries several times more when the ordering is right. It would still be correct for a reader who read every word.

    The structure this implies
    #SectionWhy here
    1The finding, with its rangeThe only fact that must survive any cut
    2What it means for the decisionThe bar, and which side the range falls
    3The one number that would change itWhat makes this fragile
    4How to settle itThe holdout, the pre-period check, the cost
    5Method and caveatsNecessary, and it goes last
    This is discovery order reversed

    Which is why writing it feels wrong and reading it feels obvious. You spent three days on the method and forty minutes on the conclusion, and the document asks you to lead with the forty minutes.

    The practical trick is to write it in discovery order, then move the last section to the top and delete what has become redundant. Trying to write it in the final order from a blank page tends to produce a conclusion you have not yet earned.

    CRAFT

    Four sentences worth having ready

    "This is within normal variation and I would not act on it."
    Chapter 10's finding, said out loud. Two-thirds of weeks move more than 5% on a stable process, so this is the correct answer most weeks and it takes practice to say, because it sounds like not having an answer.
    "The honest range is X to Y, and your bar sits inside it."
    Names the uncertainty and hands back the decision in one move. It also pre-empts the follow-up, because the obvious next question — "so what would narrow it?" — is the one you want asked.
    "That number answers a different question from the one you asked."
    For the case chapter 11 measured: members spend 58% more, which is true and is not a statement about the programme. Saying which question a number answers is more useful than caveating it.
    "A two-week holdout on 5% of customers would settle this permanently."
    Turns an unanswerable question into a scheduled one. It is also the cheapest thing in the report and the item most likely to be cut, so it belongs in the summary rather than the appendix.
    The one to avoid

    "The data suggests..." followed by a point estimate. It reads as appropriately hedged and commits to a single number, which is the worst combination: the caution is in the tone and the confidence is in the content.

    If the finding is uncertain, the number has to carry the uncertainty. No adverb can do it.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a summary

  1. Does the headline number carry a range? If not, it is asserting certainty the analysis may not have.
  2. Where does the decision bar sit relative to that range? Inside it means undetermined, and that is a finding.
  3. If you cut to three facts, are they the three most valuable? By value that is 68%; by discovery order 13%.
  4. Is the method before or after the finding? Before is discovery order, and it halves what the page carries.
  5. Is there a route to settling it? A 5% holdout belongs in the summary, not the appendix.
