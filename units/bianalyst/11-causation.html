    The famous failure and the common one

    Attenuation is the common damage; reversal is the famous one

    At 60% confounding the result is attenuated 100% of the time and reversed 0%. The reported lift is +10.4% against a true 35% — wrong by a factor of three, with the right sign.

    Reversal only becomes common at extreme confounding: 30% of runs at 85%, and 84% at 100%.

    Which matters because of what "check your segments" does and does not do. It catches a reversal instantly — the segments disagree with the total, and that is impossible to miss. It tells you nothing about the attenuated case, because every segment agrees with the overall sign and the only problem is a magnitude nobody can see.

    The number that survives review

    A reported +10.4% where the truth is +35% will be used to decide whether the treatment is worth rolling out. If the threshold for rollout is 15%, the decision goes the wrong way, and every check anyone would run comes back clean.

    Nothing about the treatment changed across this whole table. Only who received it.

    THE REAL QUESTION

    "Does the loyalty programme increase spend?"

    The programme genuinely lifts spend 6%. Members spend far more than that, because of who joins.

    The sign is right and the magnitude is out by a factor of ten

    The naive comparison reports +58.2% against a true +6%. Nobody will argue about the direction, so the number goes into a business case unchallenged and the programme gets credited with roughly ten times what it did.

    This is the same failure shape as the attenuated lift above: a plausible number, the correct sign, and a magnitude that changes the decision.

    The residual is exactly the part you cannot see

    Matching on observables gets to 2.3× when almost all the selection is observable and only 8.4× when most of it is not. The naive figure barely moves across the whole table, because the total selection is the same — only how much of it you can control for changes.

    So the quality of an observational answer is set by something you cannot measure. Which is why the honest version of it comes with a caveat rather than a tighter number, and why "we controlled for the obvious things" is not reassurance — it is a statement about what was easy to collect.

    Parallel trends is an assumption, not a property of the method

    With parallel trends, difference in differences recovers +6.0% exactly — it removes both the selection and the time trend, which is why it is the best observational tool here.

    Give members a 6% trend of their own and it reads +12.4%, out by 2.1×. And the assumption is exactly as unverifiable as the thing it stands in for: if you could observe why members trend differently, you would not have needed DiD.

    What you can do is check the pre-period. If the two groups moved in parallel for the six months before the programme launched, the assumption is at least consistent with the data — which is weaker than proof and much better than nothing.

    THE OTHER HALF

    Answering the decision rather than the query

    What people ask, and what they need
    The requestThe decision behind itWhat actually answers it
    "Do members spend more?"Should we expand the programme?The causal effect, which needs a design
    "What's our churn rate?"Where should retention spend go?Churn by segment, and the addressable part
    "Which channel converts best?"Where should the next pound go?Marginal return, not average conversion
    "Why did last week dip?"Do we need to act?Chapter 10: usually nothing needs acting on
    "Can I get this by region?"Something they have not said yetAsk what they will do with it
    "Which channel converts best" is the one worth catching

    Average conversion by channel answers a different question from where the next pound should go. A channel converting at 4% may be saturated and a 1% channel may have room, and the request as phrased cannot distinguish them.

    It is also chapter 3's ratio trap waiting to happen: the highest-converting channel is often the smallest, and expanding it moves it toward the others.

  1. Ask what they will do differently at different answers. If nothing changes either way, the query is not worth running well.
  2. Say which question your number answers. "Members spend 58% more" is true and is not an answer about the programme.
  3. Offer the design when the question is causal. A two-week holdout on 5% of eligible customers costs almost nothing and settles it permanently.
  4. Report a range when the answer is observational. "Somewhere between 6% and 40%, depending on how much of the joining decision we can see" is more useful than a false point estimate.
  5. Name the confound out loud. Doing so once, specifically, buys more credibility than any amount of general caution.

    6. JUDGEMENT

    Five questions before answering a "does X cause Y"

  6. Who chose to be in the treated group? If they chose, the naive gap was 9.7× the truth here.
  7. What would make someone both treated and high-outcome? That is the confound, and naming it is most of the work.
  8. Is there a pre-period? It converts an untestable assumption into a checkable one.
  9. Could you hold out 5%? Almost always yes, and it removes the whole problem.
  10. If the answer is attenuated rather than reversed, would anyone notice? At 60% confounding, no check catches it.
