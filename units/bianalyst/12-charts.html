CHAPTER 12

Chart choice and what the eye can do

Encoding a value as circle radius makes a 4:1 ratio look like 16:1, and the chart looks identical either way. Meanwhile the axis multiplies signal and noise by exactly the same factor — so no axis setting can show a 3% change against 8% weekly noise.

    GEOMETRY

    What each encoding does to a ratio

    Not a claim about perception. Given a value ratio, this is the ratio that appears in the visual dimension the reader actually compares.

    The two circle mistakes are exact inverses

    Encoding value as area is correct, and gives a radius ratio of √r. Encoding it as radius is a bug, and gives an area ratio of r². At 4:1 those are 2:1 and 16:1.

    Both are geometry rather than opinion, and both are invisible in the finished chart — a bubble chart looks entirely plausible whichever one you did. The only tell is arithmetic.

    A dataset spanning 100:1, drawn as bubbles with the largest at 60px

    How to spot it without reading the code

    A bubble chart where the small values have vanished is usually a radius-encoding bug rather than a data problem. At 100:1 the correct encoding leaves the smallest bubble at 6 pixels — legible — and the bug leaves it at 0.6, which is nothing.

    The same test works in reverse: if the largest bubble is swallowing the plot area, check whether someone passed the value straight to r.

    The pie's encoding is exact; the problem is elsewhere

    A two-point difference in share is exactly 7.2° of arc. The angle carries the value faithfully — pies are not "lying" about anything.

    What they lack is a shared baseline. Two bars 2% apart sit against a common axis and the difference is a readable offset; two slices 7.2° apart, at different rotations, have nothing to line up against. So the pie is fine for "this is about a third" and poor for "which of these two is bigger", and those are different jobs.

    THE AXIS

    Amplification, and what it cannot do

    Amplification is exactly mean ÷ plotted range. It applies to everything on the chart.

    The axis cannot improve signal-to-noise, because it scales both

    At every axis setting the ratio of a 3% change to ordinary 8% weekly noise is 0.38 — invariant. Widening or narrowing the window multiplies both by the same factor.

    Which means there is no axis range that shows a 3% change while hiding 8% noise. I had assumed one existed and went looking for it; the arithmetic says it cannot, because the noise is simply larger than the signal.

    The axis decides how big both look. It cannot decide which one you see.

    Aggregation is the only lever, and it is chapter 10's again

    At weekly, the 3% change is 0.38× the ordinary noise, so noise wins on the chart at every axis setting. Aggregating to quarterly takes it to 1.35×, and only then does the change become the dominant visual feature.

    Chapter 10 found the same thing about detection: monthly aggregation is quieter by exactly √(52/12) and that beats looking more often. Here it is again as a charting problem — a chart cannot rescue a series you are sampling too finely.

    The zero-baseline rule, stated honestly

    On a 300-pixel chart a 3% change spans 9 pixels. Readable, and not salient — so the real objection to a zero baseline is not that it hides the change but that it makes the change indistinguishable from everything else on the page.

    Which gives the rule that survives the argument: bars start at zero because their length is the encoding, and a truncated bar encodes a false ratio — the 4:1 geometry from the first demo, applied to a baseline. Lines need not, because their slope is the encoding, and slope is unaffected by where the axis starts.

    PRACTICE

    Choosing a chart

    The question, and what answers it
    QuestionChartWhy
    How has this changed over time?LineSlope is the encoding; baseline is free
    Which of these is biggest?Horizontal barLength on a common baseline, and labels fit
    How do two variables relate?ScatterPosition on two common scales
    What is the distribution?Histogram or boxPosition, and it shows the shape a mean hides
    Is this roughly a third?Pie is fineAngle is exact; one comparison against the whole
    Which of these two shares is bigger?Not a pie7.2° with no shared baseline to read it against
    Three variables at once?Scatter with colourTwo positions plus a categorical — not bubble size
    One number against a target?The number, largeA gauge encodes one value as an angle and wastes the space
  1. Sort bars by value, not alphabetically. The ordering is free information and alphabetical ordering discards it.
  2. Label directly rather than with a legend. A legend makes the reader hold a colour in memory and look it up; a label at the end of the line does not.
  3. Use colour for categories, position for quantities. Colour has a small usable range and is unreliable for magnitude.
  4. Never encode a quantity as bubble radius. The first demo is why: a 4:1 ratio becomes 16:1 and nothing on the chart says so.
  5. Put the noise band on the chart. Chapter 10's control limits, drawn, remove the "why did it dip" conversation before it starts.
  6. Choose the aggregation before the axis. The axis cannot fix a signal smaller than the noise; only aggregation can.

    7. JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a chart

  7. What is the reader comparing — position, length, angle or area? Only the first three preserve the ratio.
  8. If it has bubbles, is value mapped to area or radius? The difference at 4:1 is 2:1 against 16:1.
  9. Is the change worth seeing larger than the noise? If not, no axis choice helps.
  10. Is it a bar with a truncated baseline? That encodes a false ratio; a line does not.
  11. Could you read it without the legend? If not, the labels are in the wrong place.

    12. Recap

    What to take from this chapter

    1. Position, length and angle carry a ratio through unchanged.
    2. Value → area is correct and gives a radius ratio of √r — 2:1 for a 4:1 value.
    3. Value → radius is a bug and gives an area ratio of r² — 16:1 for the same data.
    4. The two are exact inverses, and the chart looks identical either way.
    5. At 100:1 the correct encoding leaves the smallest bubble at 6px; the bug leaves 0.60px.
    6. So vanished small bubbles are usually an encoding bug, not a data problem.
    7. A pie's angle is exact: a two-point share difference is 7.2°.
    8. What it lacks is a shared baseline, which is why bars win for comparison.
    9. Axis amplification is exactly mean ÷ plotted range.
    10. It scales signal and noise identically, so the ratio is invariant at 0.38.
    11. There is no axis range that shows a 3% change against 8% noise.
    12. The axis decides how big both look, not which one you see.
    13. Aggregation is the only lever: weekly 0.38, monthly 0.78, quarterly 1.35.
    14. A chart cannot rescue a series you are sampling too finely.
    15. On a 300px chart a 3% change spans 9 pixels — readable, not salient.
    16. Bars start at zero because length is the encoding; lines need not, because slope is.
