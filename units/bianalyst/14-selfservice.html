CHAPTER 14

Self-service and what it costs

A tool that answers 78% of questions covers 36% of the value and removes 19% of the hours. And free self-service goes net negative as soon as a wrong answer costs more than 0.25× what a right one is worth.

    Coverage by ticket count flatters self-service

    A tool handling up to a filtered comparison answers 78% of questions and covers 36% of the value. Those are the same tool measured two ways, and the first number is the one that appears in the business case.

    The gap is the whole argument. The questions self-service cannot reach are the ones closest to a decision, so they carry most of the value — which is chapter 1's point about which questions are worth answering, arriving as a procurement problem.

    The queue gets shorter and every item gets slower

    Level-3 self-service removes 78% of items and only 19% of hours. Mean cost per remaining item goes from 1.1 to 4.1 hours — 3.8 times.

    Which explains a conversation that happens after every rollout: the ticket count has collapsed and the analysts report no relief. Both observations are correct. Self-service removed the cheap items, and what it left behind is the expensive tail.

    It also predicts a second-order effect. An analyst whose queue is entirely level 5–7 work has no quick wins left, and a day with no completions feels like a day of no progress even when it is the most valuable day of the week.

    THE REAL TRADE

    Coverage against correctness

    Self-service raises coverage and lowers per-answer correctness. The question is whether the product goes up.

    Free self-service wins the naive comparison, and it is the wrong comparison

    It more than doubles coverage, cuts correctness by 41%, and still delivers more correct answers: 47.3% against 39.5%. On those numbers it is worth doing.

    But governance with certified metrics beats it on both terms at once — 71.0% — because chapter 7's semantic layer raises correctness without giving back much coverage. The real choice was never between the first two rows.

    A usable test

    A rollout is worth it if per-answer correctness stays above (old coverage × old correctness) ÷ new coverage. At 86% coverage that break-even is 46%, and free self-service at 55% clears it.

    Two numbers, one division. Everything else in the debate is opinion, and this replaces most of it.

    The answer flips at 0.25×, which is lower than it has any right to be

    I expected this threshold to sit somewhere around 1–2× and assumed a wrong answer would have to be seriously costly before the arithmetic turned. It flips at 0.25×: free self-service goes net negative as soon as a confidently wrong number costs more than a quarter of what a right one is worth.

    Which is almost always. So the "it still delivers more correct answers" argument survives only under the assumption that wrong answers are free, and that assumption is doing all the work.

    A 28-fold difference in robustness, and that is what the semantic layer buys

    The certified-metrics configuration stays ahead of analysts-only until a wrong answer costs 7.05× a right one, against free self-service's 0.25×.

    That gap — not the coverage number — is the case for spending on definitions before tooling. A self-service rollout onto ungoverned metrics is a bet that nobody will act on a wrong number, and chapter 7 measured how often that bet loses: five choices at 85% gives 44% correct end to end.

    PRACTICE

    Making self-service work

  1. Buy the semantic layer before the tool. 0.25× against 7.05× robustness, and the tool is the cheaper half.
  2. Measure coverage by value, not tickets. 78% by count was 36% by value; only one of those belongs in a business case.
  3. Expect no capacity relief. 78% of items removed freed 19% of hours; plan the headcount case on the hours.
  4. Certify a small number of datasets and say so on the page. Uncertified is fine for exploring and should be visibly uncertified.
  5. Publish the metric definition next to the field name. A tooltip converts chapter 7's invisible assumption into a visible option.
  6. Watch for the level-4 questions arriving as level-2 ones. "Just revenue by region" is often a fan-out away from chapter 4's 6.6× error.
  7. Keep a route back to a human. The 22% of questions self-service cannot answer carry 64% of the value, and the failure mode is someone approximating rather than asking.
    8. The thing that actually raises correctness

    Not training, and not documentation. Removing the choice: a certified dataset where revenue means one thing, joins are pre-resolved as chapter 6's surrogate keys, and the grain is declared so the tool can refuse an invalid aggregation.

    Everything in this unit's Part 2 was, in retrospect, the self-service programme.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a self-service programme

  8. What share of the value does the tool cover? Not the ticket count.
  9. What is the break-even correctness? Old coverage × old correctness, over new coverage.
  10. What does a wrong answer cost here? Above 0.25× and ungoverned self-service is net negative.
  11. Are the metrics certified before the tool ships? That is the difference between 0.25× and 7.05×.
  12. Did analyst hours actually fall? Items and hours move at very different rates.
