CHAPTER 10

Variation against signal

A process where nothing is changing moves more than 5% in 66% of weeks and produces three consecutive declines in 31% of quarters. And for a sustained shift between 8% and 18%, monthly reporting is both quieter and faster than weekly — a loss on both axes.

    The mean never moves. The process is entirely stationary. And 66% of weeks show a move larger than five per cent, while 31% of quarters contain three consecutive declines.

    The three-declines pattern has nothing to do with the business at all — a fair coin flipped weekly produces runs of three the same way. What gets read as a trend is the arithmetic of runs, which is why it recurs roughly every third quarter forever.

    A percentage threshold is a rule about noise, not about the business

    A metric with 2% variation trips the five-per-cent rule in 7.8% of weeks; one with 15% trips it in 81.3%. Same rule, same underlying stability, ten times the alarm rate.

    Which is why the identical threshold produces a weekly crisis on the small-segment chart and silence on the group total, and why "flag anything over 5%" quietly becomes "flag the noisy metrics".

    Note the third column: the run rule fires at 31.7% regardless of noisiness, because runs depend only on the sequence of ups and downs. It is a rule about coin flips, and it is immune to how carefully you choose the threshold.

    The comparison that matters is against the rule you are actually using

    A ±3σ limit fires 0.14 times a year on a stable process and catches a 20% shift in 3.2 weeks. The five-per-cent rule fires 34 times a year on that same stable process.

    That is 244 times the false alarm rate, in exchange for nothing — the percentage rule has no better detection, it simply reacts to everything. The control limit's advantage is that it is scaled to the metric's own variation, so it means the same thing on every chart.

    Computing the limits honestly

    Use the variation of the process, not of the reporting period. The usual mistake is computing σ from the last twelve points, which includes any real shift and widens the limits to cover it — so the chart stops flagging the change it was built to find.

    The standard fix is a moving-range estimate from a period you have reason to call stable, recomputed only when you have deliberately decided the process changed.

    I expected a clean trade and there isn't one

    The obvious story is that weekly costs more false alarms and buys faster detection. It does cost more false alarms — 4.3× — and for a sustained shift between 8% and 18% it is also slower.

    A 10% shift is 2.6σ on monthly data and only 1.25σ on weekly, so the weekly chart mostly cannot see it at all. Averaging 4.3 weeks shrinks the standard error by √4.3, and that beats having 4.3 times as many chances to look.

    Above an 18% shift weekly wins, because the signal clears the wider weekly limits easily and you no longer have to wait for the month to close. That is the crossover, and it is where weekly reporting genuinely earns its place.

    And a floor, below which the comparison is meaningless

    Below an 8% shift neither cadence finds it within a year at ±3σ. What looks like detection down there is a false alarm arriving by luck — which weekly does more often precisely because it is noisier.

    So the honest summary has three regions: under 8%, neither works. Between 8% and 18%, monthly wins on both axes. Over 18%, weekly wins on speed and still costs 4.3× the false alarms.

    Weekly reporting is a tool for large abrupt changes. Using it for a gradual 10% erosion is choosing to be noisier and slower at the same time.

    Correct per metric, wrong for the page

    Twenty metrics at ±3σ give 2.8 false alarms a year — one every 19 weeks. Sixty give 8.4, one every six weeks, which is often enough that the flags stop meaning anything.

    This is MLOps chapter 6's alarm budget arriving on a dashboard, and the fix is the same: fewer metrics on the page, not wider limits on each. Widening the limits to fix a page-level problem destroys the per-metric sensitivity you built them for.

    PRACTICE

    Reporting that does not manufacture stories

  1. Put control limits on the chart, not a percentage threshold. The same threshold means different things on different metrics; limits scaled to each metric's own variation mean the same thing everywhere.
  2. Report at the cadence the decision is made at. If nobody acts weekly, weekly reporting buys 4.3× the false alarms and, below an 18% shift, arrives later too.
  3. Say "within normal variation" out loud. It is the most useful sentence available and it takes practice to say, because it sounds like not having an answer.
  4. Never explain a single point. Any explanation you construct for one week will be equally available next week when it moves the other way.
  5. Use runs rules deliberately or not at all. Three declines fires on 31% of quarters by chance; if you want a runs rule, use eight consecutive points on one side of the mean, which is genuinely rare.
  6. Keep the number of flagged metrics small. Sixty at ±3σ is a false alarm every six weeks.
    7. The sentence that changes a meeting

    "This week is inside the range we have seen for the last two years, so I would not act on it. Here is what would have to happen for me to say something has changed: two consecutive points outside the limits, or a sustained move of about a fifth."

    That commits you in advance, which is the part that makes it credible. It also means the next genuine signal arrives with an argument already agreed rather than one to be had.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a recurring report

  7. What is this metric's coefficient of variation? Without it you cannot tell a signal from a Tuesday.
  8. How often would your alert rule fire on a flat process? The five-per-cent rule fires 34 times a year.
  9. Is anyone acting at the reporting cadence? If not, the cadence is buying noise.
  10. How many metrics carry limits on the page? Sixty is a false alarm every six weeks.
  11. When did you last say a move was within normal variation? If never, some of those explanations were invented.

    ONE THING TO TRY

    Compute the standard deviation of your headline metric over the last two stable years. Divide by the mean. That number tells you which moves are worth a slide.
