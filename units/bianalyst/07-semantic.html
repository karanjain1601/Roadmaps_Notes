    THE PROBLEM

    Divergence is a conjunction, not carelessness

    0.85 to the power of five is 44.4%

    Nobody here is careless. Every single choice is made correctly six times in seven, and fewer than half the implementations are right end to end.

    This is the same conjunction that ran through chapter 4's grain problems: independent decisions multiply, and five of them at 85% is worse than one at 44%. Definitional divergence is a structural outcome of letting several people implement the same metric, not evidence about those people.

    The cost grows with the square of the organisation

    Reconciliation happens between pairs. Eight tools is 28 pairs, of which 21.8 disagree; sixteen tools is 120 pairs and 93.4 disagreements. At ninety minutes a pair that is 32.6 hours against 140.2.

    Quadrupling the number of tools multiplies the reconciliation work by 20. So the cost of an undefined metric does not grow with the size of the organisation — it grows with the square of it, which is why this problem seems to appear suddenly rather than gradually.

    You cannot get there by being careful

    At 95% per choice — which is very careful indeed — eight teams still all agree only 12.7% of the time.

    The failure is a conjunction across five choices and eight people, so care has to be near-perfect on both axes simultaneously. The way out is not better discipline. It is having one implementation, which is what a semantic layer is.

    Two measures, and they behave completely differently

    The share holding the right number follows c + (1−c)·0.444 exactly — the simulation matches the closed form at every coverage level — so it rises linearly. At 90% coverage, 94.5% of consumers have the canonical figure, which looks like a solved problem.

    Numbers in circulation does not behave linearly at all. At 90% coverage there are still 4.0 distinct numbers, because the six people off the layer are each 56% likely to differ and likely to differ from each other.

    One person with a different number is enough to start the meeting. Which is why the two measures diverge: the share is a linear win and the disagreement is not.

    Why "we built a metrics layer" and "the numbers still disagree" are compatible

    Getting to two numbers in circulation needs 98% coverage. Getting to one needs all of it.

    So the exports and the ad-hoc SQL are not an edge case to tidy up later. They are the part that decides whether the layer worked, and a layer at 90% coverage is doing real good — 94.5% of people have the right number — while still producing a disagreement most weeks.

    The practical consequence is that adoption work is not a rollout afterthought. It is the majority of the project's value, and it is the part that gets cut when the deadline moves.

    What a layer can and cannot enforce

    The seven things that go wrong, and which are governable
    ChoiceEnforceable by a layer?
    Exclude internal accountsYes — a filter in the definition
    Exclude refunded ordersYes — a filter
    Order date or settlement dateYes — the time basis is part of the metric
    TimezoneYes — if the layer sets it; otherwise the client does
    Trailing 28 or calendar monthPartly — the layer offers both, the user still picks
    Which metric to use at allNo
    What happens after an exportNo
    Four of five become impossible to get wrong

    The fifth becomes a visible option rather than an invisible assumption — which is nearly as good, because a visible disagreement gets settled in one conversation and an invisible one does not.

    The last two are not definitional problems and no layer will fix them. Someone choosing the wrong metric is chapter 3's question, and someone editing an exported spreadsheet is chapter 14's.

    What actually goes in the layer

    Metric definitions — the expression, the filters, the time basis, the aggregation rule per dimension that chapter 4 showed no tool can infer.

    Join paths — so nobody has to know that orders join customers on the surrogate key rather than the natural one, which is chapter 6's whole apparatus made invisible.

    Grain declarations — so the layer can refuse to sum an order-grain measure at line grain rather than silently returning 6.63× the truth.

    Ownership and description — the fields from chapter 3 that make the definition readable by someone who did not write it.

    PRACTICE

    Making one work

  1. Start with the metrics people argue about, not the easy ones. The value is concentrated in a handful of contested numbers, and a layer that covers forty uncontested ones has produced nothing.
  2. Measure coverage explicitly. Share of queries, or share of consumers, hitting the layer. Without it you cannot tell 70% from 95%, and those are very different places.
  3. Make the layer the fastest path. Adoption follows convenience far more reliably than it follows policy, and every off-layer consumer is 56% likely to produce a different number.
  4. Version definitions and record when they change. A metric that quietly changed meaning in March produces a step that gets attributed to the business — the same failure chapter 6 measured for dimensions.
  5. Expose the definition next to the number. A tooltip with the filters and time basis settles most disagreements without a meeting.
  6. Accept that exports exist. Stamp them with the metric version and the run time, so a stale spreadsheet identifies itself rather than starting an argument.

    7. JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a semantic layer

  7. What share of queries go through it? Below 98% you still have several numbers in circulation.
  8. How many independent implementations of your top metric exist? Eight gives 21.8 disagreeing pairs.
  9. Is the layer the fastest way to get an answer? If not, coverage will not hold.
  10. Can a consumer see the definition from the number? That converts an invisible assumption into a visible option.
  11. Are metric versions recorded? A silent redefinition looks exactly like a business result.
