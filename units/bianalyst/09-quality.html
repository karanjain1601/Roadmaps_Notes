Data quality, in dimensions

    VOCABULARY

    Six dimensions, and what each actually asks

    The standard list, stated as a question you can test
    DimensionThe questionHow you test it
    CompletenessAre all the rows here?Row counts against expectation, per source
    TimelinessAre they here yet?Age of the newest row, per source
    ValidityDoes each value fit its type and range?Constraints, enum membership, bounds
    ConsistencyDo two places agree?Reconciliation against a second system
    UniquenessIs the declared grain actually unique?Chapter 4's key assertion
    AccuracyIs it true?Nothing automated. This is the hard one.
    Only the last one matters, and only the first five are testable

    Accuracy is what anyone actually cares about, and no check can establish it — a perfectly complete, timely, valid, consistent, unique table of wrong numbers passes every test.

    Which is why the useful question is not "is our data quality good" but which testable failures move the numbers people act on. That is measurable, and it is what the rest of this chapter does.

    The biggest distortion is not the biggest problem

    A unit change upstream is the largest single distortion at 31%, and it does 14.30 points of damage a year. The worst offender is a 4%-short partial load at 21.35 points — a much smaller distortion that happens more often and escapes half the time.

    Damage is frequency × escape rate × size, and only the last of those three is what people rank defects by. The same shape appeared in MLOps chapter 16, where detection time did the work that severity was assumed to do.

    Detectability is a property of the defect's shape, not its size

    The correlation between how much a defect distorts and how reliably it is caught is −0.07. Essentially unrelated.

    A 31% unit change and a 0.6% null spike are both single-column events. The checks can see that something moved; they cannot see which one moved the business number. So you cannot infer priority from detectability, and you cannot infer detectability from priority.

    These are marginal values, which is the number that should decide what to build

    Each row is what you lose by removing that one check while keeping the rest. Distribution monitoring avoids 48.43 points a year; null checking avoids 4.07 — a factor of twelve.

    Null checking is usually the first thing anyone builds, because it is the easiest to express. It is worth the least here, and that ordering is a coincidence of implementation difficulty rather than of value.

    Running nothing costs 324.20 points against 42.40 with all five — a 7.6-fold reduction, and not to zero. Five independent checks still leave 42.40 points a year escaping, which is the honest ceiling on what testing can do.

    Each further nine costs more than the last

    90% to 95% costs 3.0 hours. 95% to 99% costs another 15.9. And 99.9% is not reached at all within 48 hours.

    The tail is almost entirely one slow source, which means a completeness SLA above about 99% is a promise about that source rather than about your pipeline. Writing it into a service level you do not control is a way of guaranteeing you will breach it.

    A completeness figure alone cannot tell you whether the report is usable

    The total is identical under both causes — 5.0% short either way. The partner segment is 32% short when one source is slow, against 5.0% if the loss were random.

    So 95% complete is fine for the total and destroys the segment view, and the single aggregate number cannot distinguish the two situations. Only a per-source completeness check can.

    The scheduling consequence is uncomfortable: the total reaches 95% at 08:36, and the partner source not until 33:12 — over a day later. That is the number a report schedule should actually be set by, and it is invisible in any aggregate freshness metric.

    PRACTICE

    Checks that earn their place

  1. Check completeness per source, not in aggregate. The aggregate cannot distinguish "5% missing everywhere" from "one segment gutted", and those need different responses.
  2. Build distribution monitoring before null monitoring. 48.43 points against 4.07, and the ordering is usually the other way round because nulls are easier to express.
  3. Assert the declared grain is unique. One line, and it catches chapter 4's whole class.
  4. Reconcile against a second system for the top metric. This is the only check that touches accuracy, and it is worth running monthly even if it is manual.
  5. Show freshness on the report itself. "Data through 08:36, partner source 65% loaded" turns an invisible caveat into a visible one, and chapter 7's argument applies: a visible disagreement gets settled.
  6. Keep the alarm budget in mind. MLOps chapter 6 measured 50 columns at z>3 firing on 12.6% of clean days. More checks stop helping well before you run out of things to check.
    7. The check nobody writes

    A row count that is too high. Every quality suite tests for missing rows; almost none tests for a fan-out, a double load, or a backfill that ran twice — and "rows loaded twice" was 5.01 points of annual damage here.

    The assertion is symmetric and costs the same to write: expect the count within a band, not above a floor.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about a quality programme

  7. Which defect does the most annual damage? It is unlikely to be the one with the biggest distortion.
  8. What is each check worth marginally? Distribution was twelve times null checking here.
  9. Do you measure completeness per source? Otherwise 5% and 32% look the same.
  10. What completeness does your report actually run at? And is the SLA a promise about a source you do not control?
  11. Do any checks test for too many rows? Most suites only test for too few.
