CHAPTER 2

Where the BI analyst sits

A question asked monthly does not justify building a dashboard — 36 hours of answering against 48 hours of building and keeping it. The break-even is 16 times a year, and it moves eight times further on how long an answer takes than on how long a build takes.

    The distinguishing question is not the tool

    All four write SQL. All four touch the warehouse. What separates them is what they are accountable for when it goes wrong: the engineer for the pipeline being late, the analytics engineer for the model being wrong, the analyst for the decision being wrong or unmade.

    Which puts the BI analyst in an awkward and useful place: you are accountable for an outcome you do not fully control. Most of this chapter is about what that costs and which parts of it you can buy back.

  1. Only this role owns the definition. Chapter 1 measured four honest definitions of one metric differing by 4.0×. Nobody upstream will pick one for you.
  2. Only this role sits in the meeting. Knowing which decision is being made is not recoverable from the ticket, and it changes the answer.
  3. Only this role notices the number is odd. A pipeline that runs green and a model that compiles will both happily produce a figure that is 40% off.
  4. The overlap with analytics engineering is real and worth having. The second demo puts a number on it.

    5. THE DAILY DECISION

    Answer it again, or build it?

    Answering costs hours each time. Building costs hours once, plus the 0.4 hours a week of maintenance chapter 1 measured — charged whether anyone opens it or not.

    Monthly does not justify a dashboard

    The break-even is 16 times a year — between fortnightly and weekly. A monthly request costs 36 hours to keep answering and 48 hours to build and maintain.

    That is the opposite of most people's instinct, and the reason is that maintenance is charged per week whether anyone opens the thing or not. Six hours of building is trivial; forty-two hours of keeping it correct for two years is not.

    This is also where chapter 1's 103 never-opened dashboards come from. Every one of them was somebody sensibly deciding that a recurring question deserved a permanent answer, without pricing the permanence.

    Ask how long the answer takes, not how long the build takes

    An eightfold change in the answer cost moves the break-even 8.0×. A thirtyfold change in the build cost moves it 2.3×.

    So the two things worth knowing are how long answering takes and how often they will ask — and the second is the one nobody records. Logging the question, not just the answer, is the cheapest process change available to a BI team.

    The real argument for a shared model

    Cutting maintenance from 0.4 to 0.05 hours a week takes the break-even from 16 to 4 times a year, which flips a monthly request from "answer it again" to "build it".

    So a semantic layer does not earn its keep by making building faster. It earns it by making things cheaper to keep — and that is what decides the long tail of requests, which is most of them. Chapter 7 is about building that layer; this is the number that pays for it.

    The queue beats the spec, which is uncomfortable

    I expected spec quality to dominate — it is the part you control, and "write a better ticket" is satisfying advice. It does not. Halving the upstream queue saves 1.2 days; a perfect spec saves 0.8.

    At a 30% chance of another round you are simply not doing enough round-trips for round-trips to be the problem. Expected rounds is 1.43, so eliminating the extra ones removes 30% of the handoff cost, while halving the queue removes 50% of it.

    The awkward implication: the thing that would help most belongs to another team.

    The crossover is at 46%, and it tells you what to measure

    Below a 46% chance of another round, chase the queue. Above it, chase the spec.

    Which means the first thing to measure is not how long the other team takes — it is how often their first delivery answers the question. Almost nobody records that, and it decides which of two very different improvement programmes is worth running.

    Or remove the boundary for the easy cases

    Dropping the handoff rate from 35% to 12% — the BI analyst able to write their own models for straightforward cases — takes the calendar from 3.1 to 1.3 days, a 59% cut. That beats halving the queue and beats a perfect spec, because it eliminates the crossing rather than making it cheaper.

    This is the practical case for the analytics-engineering overlap: not ownership for its own sake, but 59% off the calendar for every question that would otherwise have crossed a team boundary. It is also why the boundary should sit at complexity rather than at tooling.

    THE OTHER HALF

    The part that is not analysis

    Finding the decision behind the request
    "Can you pull last month's signups by channel" is rarely the question. Asking what they will do differently depending on the answer changes the query about half the time, and occasionally reveals that no decision is pending at all.
    Saying no, with a number
    "That would be a dashboard we maintain for two years for a monthly question" is a refusal a stakeholder can evaluate. "We don't have capacity" is one they can only escalate.
    Owning the number in the room
    When two figures disagree, someone has to reconcile them publicly and quickly. Doing that well once buys more credibility than a quarter of correct dashboards.
    Knowing when the answer is "we cannot tell"
    Chapter 10 is about telling variation from signal. The professional move is often reporting that last week's dip is inside the normal range, which is unsatisfying and correct.

    JUDGEMENT

    Five questions about how your role is set up

  5. Do you record how often each question gets asked? The break-even depends on it and almost nobody logs it.
  6. What does one dashboard cost you per week to keep? At 0.4 hours the break-even is 16×/yr; at 0.05 it is 4×.
  7. What share of your questions need upstream work? At 35% the calendar was 5.3× the work content.
  8. How often does the first upstream delivery answer the question? Above roughly half, fix specs; below, fix the queue.
  9. Where does the boundary sit — at complexity or at tooling? If it is at tooling, you are paying handoff cost on questions that do not need one.
