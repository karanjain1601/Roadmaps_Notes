Chapter 13 · Infrastructure

Cloud providers and their primitives

Underneath several hundred product names there are about ten things: compute, block storage, object storage, a managed database, a private network, an identity system, a queue, a load balancer, DNS and a secret store. Learning the ten and then mapping the names is far faster than learning the names — and it makes the two questions that actually matter tractable: how much availability am I buying, and what is this bill made of.

What you are and are not buying

The shared responsibility model is usually drawn as a diagram with a diagonal line, which makes it look like a philosophy. It is not — it is a precise statement about who is liable for what, and it moves depending on which service you picked.

The bottom four rows never move

Whatever level of managed service you buy, configuration, identity, data and cost stay yours. Essentially every well-known "cloud breach" is a row from the bottom of that table — a public storage bucket, an over-broad IAM policy, a security group open to the world — rather than a provider failure. Moving up the stack buys you fewer things to patch, not fewer things to get right. And note the row that catches people even when everything else is managed: a backup that has never been restored is a hypothesis, not a backup.

Regions, zones and what redundancy actually buys

An availability zone is one or more datacentres with independent power, cooling and networking, a few milliseconds from its siblings. A region is a group of zones, hundreds of milliseconds apart. The distinction exists because failures are correlated at each level: an instance can fail alone, a zone can fail taking every instance in it, and a region can fail taking every zone.

A  =  region × ( 1 − ( 1 − zone × (1 − (1−instance)n) )k )   ·   n per zone, k zones

Redundancy only defends against the layer you applied it to. Adding instances defends against instance failure and does nothing about the zone; adding zones defends against zone failure and does nothing about the region. So each layer becomes a hard ceiling for the one beneath it, and the ceiling is reached quickly.

Buying nines, and what each one costs
Push instances-per-zone to 10 in one zone, then move to three zones instead
Instances inside one zone stop helping almost immediately
Going from three to ten instances in a single zone gains 0.0001 percentage points. The zone's own 99.95% is the ceiling and nothing you run inside it raises that. You are paying for seven more machines to buy nothing.
One instance in each of three zones beats ten in one
57 minutes a year against 5.3 hours, with 70% fewer instances. This is the single highest-return availability decision available and it is a placement choice rather than a spend.
Then the region becomes the ceiling
Two instances across three zones reaches 99.995% — the region's own SLA — and the sixth instance buys zero additional nines. Beyond that point the only lever is a second region, which roughly doubles cost and complexity.
And past four nines, you are the ceiling
Two regions gives 8.6 nines of infrastructure availability and about 4.24 in practice, because two bad changes a year at fifteen minutes each takes every region down simultaneously. No redundancy addresses a bad deploy, a config push, an expired certificate or a DNS mistake.
Which is chapter 1, arriving from the other direction

The arithmetic says that beyond roughly four nines, further investment in infrastructure redundancy is close to wasted and the binding constraint is your own change process. That is precisely the DORA argument: smaller batches, automated release mechanics and faster recovery. An organisation that wants a fifth nine and responds by adding a third region has misread its own numbers; the same money spent on progressive delivery, automated rollback and better testing moves the term that is actually limiting. Set the "bad changes per year" control to zero in the demo and watch the infrastructure nines suddenly become worth having.

Identity, which is the actual security boundary

Networks used to be the boundary. In a cloud account, the boundary is IAM: what a given principal is permitted to do to a given resource. Four ideas cover most of it.

Roles, not keys
A long-lived access key on an instance or in a CI job is a credential with no expiry that will eventually appear in a log, an image layer or a git history. A role is assumed to obtain short-lived credentials that rotate automatically — an instance profile on a VM, a service account on a pod via IRSA or Workload Identity, OIDC federation from a CI provider. Anywhere you currently hold a static key, there is usually a role that removes it.
Identity-based and resource-based policies both apply
An identity policy says what this principal may do; a resource policy — a bucket policy, a KMS key policy — says who may act on this resource. Access requires both to allow, and any explicit deny anywhere wins. This is why "the role has s3:GetObject and it still fails" is nearly always the bucket policy or the KMS key policy.
Least privilege is discovered, not designed
Nobody writes a minimal policy correctly from scratch. The workable method is to start broad in a non-production account, read the access analyser or the last-accessed data after a few weeks of real traffic, and narrow to what was actually used. Guessing produces either a policy that breaks in production or Action: "*".
Blast radius is an account boundary
IAM mistakes are contained by separate accounts far more reliably than by careful policy within one. Separate accounts per environment, with a service control policy at the organisation level that no role inside can override, is the control that survives a mistake in a policy document.
The confused deputy, and why ExternalId exists

You grant a third-party SaaS monitoring tool a role in your account so it can read your metrics. Its account can now assume that role — and so, if the vendor's policy is written loosely, can any other customer of that vendor, because the trust policy names the vendor's account rather than your relationship with it. The attacker does not need to compromise anything; they ask the vendor's system to do it on their behalf. The fix is a condition requiring a unique ExternalId that only you and the vendor know, so assuming the role requires knowing your specific value. Any cross-account trust you grant without one deserves a second look, and the same reasoning applies to OIDC trust from a CI provider: constrain the sub claim to your specific repository and branch, or every repository on that platform can assume your role.

Where the bill actually goes

Discussions about cloud cost are almost always about instance counts and instance types, because those are the things with obvious names. Decompose a real bill and a large fraction of it is somewhere else entirely — and that fraction responds to configuration changes rather than to architecture.

A monthly bill, itemised
Turn on the S3 gateway endpoint, then drag the instance count down to 4
Four charges that are invisible until you look

Cross-AZ traffic is billed in both directions. 5,000 GB of chatter between services in different zones costs twice the per-gigabyte rate, not once. Multi-AZ is right for availability and it has a running cost proportional to how chatty your services are — which is one more argument against a deep synchronous call chain.

NAT gateways charge per hour and per gigabyte. In the default workload above, NAT costs more than every EBS volume in active use. Traffic to S3 or DynamoDB routed out through NAT is paying a processing fee for something a gateway VPC endpoint handles for nothing — a configuration change worth $135 a month here, and far more at scale. Check this on any account you inherit.

Idle resources have no owner. Unattached volumes from terminated instances, NAT gateways in subnets nothing uses any more, load balancers with no targets, old snapshots. Roughly 6% of this bill is things that do nothing, and none of it will ever appear in a capacity review.

Network cost scales with traffic, not fleet size. At twenty instances compute is 63% of the bill; at four instances the same network and overhead makes compute only 25%. The crossover is around twelve instances. So a small deployment is mostly paying for things that are not compute, while a large one hides them — which is why the percentage view matters more than the total.

Moving the whole fleet to spot saves 70% of the compute line — which is 44% of this bill, not 70%. Commitments and spot are the levers everyone reaches for first because they are a purchasing decision rather than an engineering one, and they leave the other 37% untouched. The cheapest wins in this chapter are the opposite: a VPC endpoint, deleting unattached volumes, and co-locating two chatty services in the same zone. None of them require a negotiation, and together they are worth more here than the difference between on-demand and a one-year commitment.

Check yourself

Self-check

  1. Your service runs ten instances in one availability zone and the team proposes going to fifteen to improve availability. Evaluate the proposal.
    It buys essentially nothing. With instance availability around 99%, the chance that all ten fail independently is already about 10⁻²⁰ — the probability that the zone fails is what dominates, and no number of instances inside a zone changes that. In the model, going from three to ten instances in one zone gains 0.0001 percentage points, so the last seven machines bought nothing and the next five will too. Moving one instance into each of three zones takes annual downtime from 5.3 hours to 57 minutes with 70% fewer instances. The general rule is that redundancy only defends against the layer you applied it to, so each layer becomes the ceiling for the one below: check which layer is currently binding before adding capacity at a layer that is not.
  2. Management wants five nines. You currently run across three zones in one region. What do you tell them?
    That infrastructure is not the constraint. Three zones already reaches about 99.995% on infrastructure — the region's own SLA — so the next infrastructure nine requires a second region, roughly doubling cost and adding data-replication and failover complexity. But the model shows that two regions gives 8.6 nines of infrastructure availability and only about 4.24 in practice, because two bad changes a year at fifteen minutes each takes every region down at once. Redundancy does not protect you from your own deploys, config pushes, expired certificates or DNS mistakes. So the honest answer is that past roughly four nines the work is chapter 1's: smaller batches, automated release mechanics, progressive delivery and faster recovery. Also worth asking what the five nines is for — 26 minutes a year is a very expensive promise, and an error budget against a realistic SLO is usually the more productive conversation.
  3. Compute is 63% of your bill. Someone proposes moving everything to spot instances to cut costs by 70%. What actually happens?
    You save 70% of 63%, which is about 44% of the bill — a real saving, but not the one that was promised, and it comes with a workload constraint: spot capacity is reclaimed with roughly two minutes' notice, so it suits batch jobs, CI runners and stateless replicas with surplus capacity, not a stateful primary or a fleet already running at its minimum. Meanwhile the other 37% is untouched, and that is where the cheap wins are: in the model, a gateway VPC endpoint removes $135/month of NAT data processing with a configuration change, unattached volumes and idle NAT gateways account for another $130, and cross-AZ chatter is billed in both directions so co-locating two talkative services halves it. None of those require a purchasing decision or a change in workload tolerance. Do them first, then have the spot conversation on the remainder.
  4. Why does traffic from an EC2 instance to S3 cost money, and how do you stop it?
    If the instance is in a private subnet, its route to S3 goes out through a NAT gateway, which charges both an hourly rate and a per-gigabyte data-processing fee. The traffic never leaves the provider's network and you are paying a processing charge for it anyway — 3,000 GB a month is $135 in the model, and it scales linearly with a workload nobody thinks of as network-heavy. A gateway VPC endpoint for S3 and DynamoDB adds a route so the traffic bypasses NAT entirely, and it is free. Interface endpoints for other services are not free but are usually cheaper than NAT processing plus more secure, since traffic never traverses a public path. This is one of the highest-return things to check on an inherited account: look at the NAT gateway's processed-bytes metric and ask what is on the other end.
  5. A vendor asks you to create an IAM role their account can assume so they can read your monitoring data. What must the trust policy contain?
    A condition on a unique ExternalId agreed between you and the vendor. Without it, the trust policy names the vendor's AWS account, and anything running in that account can assume your role — including, if the vendor's own access control is loose, another of their customers. That is the confused deputy problem: the attacker never compromises anything, they simply ask the vendor's system to act on your behalf. With an ExternalId condition, assuming the role requires knowing your specific value, which the vendor stores against your tenant. Alongside that, the role should carry the minimum permissions the integration actually needs and be scoped by resource rather than *. The same reasoning applies to OIDC federation from a CI provider: constrain the sub claim to your specific repository and branch, or any repository on that platform can assume the role.
  6. Your organisation runs everything in one AWS account "for simplicity". What is the argument against it, in terms of the material in this chapter?
    Blast radius. IAM policy is difficult to get exactly right, and a single over-broad policy, a compromised credential or a mistaken terraform destroy reaches everything in the account. Separate accounts per environment give a boundary that does not depend on any policy document being correct, enforced by service control policies at the organisation level that no role inside the account can override. Several other things follow from the same split: cost attribution becomes exact rather than a tagging exercise, service quotas are per-account so production is not throttled by a load test, and an incident in one environment cannot exhaust the API rate limits of another. The cost is real — cross-account access, a shared network design and more IaC to manage — but "simplicity" here means a single failure domain for identity, cost and quotas simultaneously.
  7. You inherit an account with a $12,000 monthly bill and no context. What do you look at first?
    The itemised breakdown by charge type rather than by service, because the instinct is to count instances and the model shows compute is often a minority — 25% at four instances in the demo, and it only overtakes everything else at around twelve. Specifically: unattached EBS volumes and old snapshots, which are pure waste with no owner; NAT gateways, both their hourly cost in subnets that no longer need them and their data-processing charge, which usually indicates missing VPC endpoints; load balancers with no healthy targets; cross-AZ transfer, which is charged in both directions and points at chatty services that could be co-located; and internet egress, which points at content that should be behind a CDN per chapter 8. Only after that is the compute conversation worth having, and it starts with right-sizing against observed usage rather than with spot or commitments — because a commitment on an oversized fleet locks in the mistake for a year.
