Monitoring and observability

Chapter 1 counted incidents and recovery time; every chapter since has assumed you can tell when something is wrong. This is how. The mechanics are three data types with very different costs, and the judgement is almost entirely about two things: which labels you attach, and what you agree to be woken up for.

The practical consequence is an order of operations during an incident: metrics say something is wrong and roughly where, traces say which hop, logs say why. Starting with logs is the common mistake — it is searching a haystack before establishing which field the needle is in, and it is why an incident with good metrics takes ten minutes and the same incident with only logs takes two hours.

Structured logs, sampled, with a trace id

Three properties turn logs from a cost centre into a usable tool. Structured — JSON with consistent field names — so they can be queried rather than grepped. Carrying the trace id, so a slow trace leads directly to the log lines for that request, which is what makes the three types one system rather than three. And sampled: keep every error and a small fraction of successes, which typically removes 90% of the volume and almost none of the value, because a thousand identical successful requests tell you nothing the metric did not already.

Cardinality is the whole cost model for metrics

A time series is a metric name plus one specific combination of label values. The number of series is the product of the label cardinalities, not the sum — so adding one label multiplies your entire storage, memory and query cost by that label's cardinality. This is the single most expensive mistake available in observability, and it looks like a one-word change in a code review.

Add one label, multiply everything
Add customer_id — a reasonable-sounding request from a product manager
The safe set is small and cheap
Endpoint, method, status and instance give 12,000 series — about 3 GB retained for a month and a rounding error in memory. This covers essentially every question you will ask of a service.
One unbounded label ends it
Adding customer_id takes it to 60 million series, 16 TB retained and 172 GB of RAM in the ingester. The metric is the same metric; the label is the entire cost.
Churn is a separate problem with the same symptom
Twenty live instances is fine, but if pods are replaced daily the index accumulates 360,000 distinct series over a month — 30× the live count — even though only 12,000 exist at any moment. A dashboard showing current series looks healthy while the index grows without bound.
The rule
A label may only take values from a set bounded by something you control: your route table, your status codes, your replica count. Anything that grows with users, requests or time belongs in a log or a trace, both of which are designed for high cardinality and neither of which multiplies.
"Can we break this down by customer?"

The request is reasonable and the naive implementation is not. Three ways to satisfy it without a label. Put customer on the trace or the log, which handle high cardinality natively and support exactly this filtering. Add a bounded tier label — enterprise, business, free — which is usually what the question really means and has a cardinality of three. Or keep a separate, deliberately small metric for the top twenty customers by revenue, with everything else bucketed as "other", which is a hundred series rather than five thousand. The general move is to ask what decision the breakdown supports; it is rarely "look at all five thousand".

What to measure

RED — for anything serving requests

Rate, Errors, Duration, per endpoint. Three metrics answer most questions about a service, and they are the ones a proxy or a mesh emits for free (chapters 6 and 12). Duration as a histogram, never as an average — the mean of a bimodal distribution describes nothing that happened.

USE — for anything with a resource

Utilisation, Saturation, Errors, per resource: CPU, memory, disk, network. Chapter 2 introduced this as a triage order. Saturation is the one usually missing and usually the most informative — queue depth, run queue length, CFS throttled periods.

Percentiles, and the trap in aggregating them
You cannot average percentiles across instances. The mean of three p99s is not the fleet p99 and can be wildly wrong. Either compute from histogram buckets that can be summed — which is what Prometheus histograms are for — or accept that per-instance percentiles are per-instance.
Measure where the user is
A server-side p99 of 80 ms is compatible with a user experience of two seconds, because it excludes DNS, TLS, queueing at the load balancer and the network. Chapter 4's curl -w breakdown is the same argument: measure the phase you are claiming to have improved.
Instrument once, at the boundary
OpenTelemetry gives one vendor-neutral SDK and wire format for all three data types, so switching backend is a collector configuration change rather than a re-instrumentation project. Given how often observability vendors are switched for cost reasons, that portability is worth having from the start.

Service level objectives and error budgets

An alert should mean "a person needs to act now". Threshold alerts on internal signals — CPU above 80%, disk above 90% — do not mean that; they mean a number moved. The alternative is to define what "working" means for users, measure it, and alert when you are consuming your allowance for not working faster than you can afford.

An SLI is a measurement
The proportion of requests served successfully in under 300 ms, say. Chosen because a user would notice it changing.
An SLO is a target on that measurement
99.9% over a rolling thirty days. Deliberately below 100%, because 100% is unachievable and pursuing it stops all change.
The error budget is what is left over
0.1% of thirty days is 43.2 minutes. That is the entire month's allowance for being broken, and it is a budget in the real sense — spending it on a risky deploy is a legitimate choice, and running out means the choice is made for you.
Burn rate is the ratio, not the raw number
Current error rate divided by the allowance. A 1% error rate against a 99.9% objective is a 10× burn: the month's budget in three days.
Burn rate, and which alert should fire
Set the error rate to 0.15% — harmless-looking, and the budget is gone in 20 days
Multi-window burn-rate alerting

A single threshold is either too noisy or too slow. The standard answer uses several tiers, each defined by how much of the budget it would consume: 14.4× over an hour (2% of the budget) pages, 6× over six hours (5%) pages, 3× over a day (10%) raises a ticket, and 1× over three days raises a ticket. Each is paired with a short confirmation window — a fifth of the long one — so a momentary spike does not fire the page. The result is that a catastrophic burn pages within minutes and a slow leak is caught before it silently eats the month, and neither needs a human to have guessed a threshold.

The failure a fixed threshold cannot catch

A 0.15% error rate looks like nothing. No sensible threshold fires on it. It burns at 1.5×, which consumes a thirty-day budget in twenty days — so you miss the objective every month while every dashboard stays green and nobody is ever paged. Burn-rate alerting catches it as a ticket, which is the correct response: not urgent, definitely needs doing. And note what one more nine costs: moving from 99.9% to 99.99% cuts the budget from 43.2 minutes to 4.3, and the same 0.15% error rate goes from a 1.5× burn to 15×. An objective is a commitment about how much work you will do, and chapter 13 showed that past about four nines the binding constraint is your own change process.

Alerts that are worth waking up for

Page on symptoms, not causes
"Error budget burning at 14×" is actionable. "CPU above 80%" is not — it may be entirely fine. Cause-based alerts multiply with your architecture and never quite cover the failure that actually happens; symptom-based alerts are few and cover everything by construction.
Every page needs a runbook link and an owner
If the responder has to work out what the alert means at 3am, the alert is unfinished. If nobody owns it, it will be ignored and then deleted after it has been ignored for long enough.
Measure the alerts themselves
Track pages per week, the share actioned versus acknowledged-and-ignored, and how many arrive outside working hours. An alert that has never once required action is worse than no alert, because it trains people to dismiss the channel — the same trust-budget argument as chapter 17's flaky tests.
Alert on the absence of data too
A metric that stops arriving is indistinguishable from a metric reading zero, and a crashed exporter looks exactly like a healthy service with no traffic. Dead-man's switches — an alert that fires when a heartbeat stops — are how you find out that the monitoring broke rather than the service.
yaml — burn-rate rules, and the two that people forget
groups:
- name: slo
  rules:
  # page: 14.4× burn over 1h consumes 2% of a 30-day budget.
  # the 5m clause is a confirmation window — without it a single scrape pages you
  - alert: ErrorBudgetBurnFast
    expr: |
      job:slo_error_ratio:rate1h{job="orders"}  > (14.4 * 0.001)
      and
      job:slo_error_ratio:rate5m{job="orders"}  > (14.4 * 0.001)
    for: 2m
    labels: { severity: page }
    annotations:
      runbook: https://wiki/runbooks/orders-error-budget
      summary: "Burning the month's budget in ~5 hours"

  # ticket: 3× over a day. catches the slow leak that no threshold would see
  - alert: ErrorBudgetBurnSlow
    expr: |
      job:slo_error_ratio:rate1d{job="orders"}  > (3 * 0.001)
      and
      job:slo_error_ratio:rate2h{job="orders"}  > (3 * 0.001)
    labels: { severity: ticket }

  # the metric has STOPPED. a crashed exporter looks identical to zero traffic,
  # and every rule above silently evaluates to nothing
  - alert: NoDataFromOrders
    expr: absent(job:slo_error_ratio:rate5m{job="orders"})
    for: 10m
    labels: { severity: page }

  # and the dead-man's switch: this fires ALWAYS, and an external service pages
  # you when it stops arriving — which is how you learn the alerting itself broke
  - alert: AlertingPipelineAlive
    expr: vector(1)
    labels: { severity: heartbeat }

Check yourself

Self-check

  1. A product manager asks to break down request rate by customer. You have 5,000 customers. What happens if you add the label, and what do you do instead?
    The number of series is the product of label cardinalities, so adding one label multiplies everything by 5,000: from 12,000 series to 60 million, about 3 GB retained for a month to 16 TB, and a rounding error of memory to 172 GB in the ingester. The metric is unchanged; the label is the entire cost, and it arrives as a one-word change in a code review. Three alternatives. Put the customer on the trace or the structured log, both of which are designed for high cardinality and support exactly this filtering. Add a bounded tier label — enterprise, business, free — which is usually what the question actually means and has a cardinality of three. Or keep a separate deliberately small metric for the top twenty customers with everything else bucketed as "other". The general move is to ask what decision the breakdown supports, because it is rarely "look at all five thousand".
  2. Your series count is stable at 12,000 but Prometheus memory grows every day. Explain.
    Churn, which is a different problem from cardinality with the same symptom. The number of series alive at any instant is 12,000, but the index has to hold every series that has existed within the retention window. If pods are replaced daily and the instance or pod label carries their identity, each day creates twenty entirely new series per combination — so over thirty days the index holds around 360,000 distinct series, thirty times the live count, and it grows with every deployment. A dashboard showing current series looks perfectly healthy throughout. The fixes are to drop or rewrite labels whose values change on every restart — a pod name is rarely worth a series, whereas a stable node or deployment name is — and to watch the head-series and index-size metrics rather than only the active count. Any highly dynamic identifier belongs on a log line, not on a metric.
  3. Why can you not average p99 latency across ten instances?
    Because a percentile is a property of a distribution, not a quantity that adds. The 99th percentile of the combined traffic depends on how requests were distributed and on the shape of each instance's distribution, and the mean of ten p99s can be far from it in either direction — badly wrong when load is uneven, which chapter 6 showed is the normal case with round-robin balancing and one slow backend. The correct approach is to record a histogram with fixed buckets, sum the bucket counts across instances, and compute the percentile from the summed histogram, which is exactly what Prometheus histograms and histogram_quantile exist for. The accuracy is then limited by bucket boundaries rather than by an invalid aggregation. If you only have per-instance summaries, per-instance percentiles are the only honest thing to report — and worth flagging, since a dashboard labelled "p99" that is really a mean of p99s misleads at exactly the moment it matters.
  4. Your service has a 99.9% objective over 30 days and is running at a 0.15% error rate. Nothing alerts. Is that right?
    No — nothing alerts under a fixed threshold, which is the problem. The budget is 0.1% of thirty days, or 43.2 minutes. A 0.15% error rate is a burn rate of 1.5×, so a full month's budget is consumed in twenty days: you will miss the objective every month while every dashboard stays green and no threshold on error rate would sensibly fire on 0.15%. Burn-rate alerting catches this as a ticket rather than a page, which is the right response — not urgent, definitely needs doing — and it does so without anybody having to guess a threshold. The general structure is several tiers defined by how much of the budget each would consume: 14.4× over an hour and 6× over six hours page, 3× over a day and 1× over three days raise tickets, each paired with a short confirmation window so a momentary spike does not wake anyone.
  5. What does moving an objective from 99.9% to 99.99% actually commit you to?
    Cutting the monthly error budget from 43.2 minutes to 4.3, and multiplying every burn rate by ten — the same 0.15% error rate goes from a 1.5× burn to 15×, which now pages. Practically that means a single bad deploy consumes the month, so it commits you to progressive delivery with automated abort (chapter 18), sub-minute detection and rollback, and enough redundancy that no single component failure is user-visible. Chapter 13 showed the arithmetic behind the ceiling: infrastructure redundancy reaches roughly four nines and past that the binding constraint is your own change process, because two bad changes a year at fifteen minutes each already exceeds a four-minute monthly budget. So the honest framing is that an extra nine is a commitment about how the team works rather than about what to buy, and the question to ask first is whether users would notice the difference between 43 minutes and 4.
  6. Why do metrics cost nothing per request while logs cost linearly, and what follows?
    A metric is an aggregate held in memory: incrementing a counter is the same work whether it happens ten times a second or ten million, and the cost is set by how many distinct series exist rather than how many events they summarise. A log line is a record per event, so ten times the traffic is ten times the bytes, ten times the ingestion and ten times the bill. What follows is a division of labour. Use metrics for anything you want continuously, for alerting and for long retention — a year of metrics is cheap and trends matter. Use logs for investigating individual cases, keep them for days rather than months, and sample them: retain every error and a small fraction of successes, which typically removes 90% of the volume and almost none of the value, since a thousand identical successful requests tell you nothing the metric did not. And ensure every log line carries the trace id, which is what makes the three data types one system rather than three.
  7. Your monitoring shows no errors because the exporter crashed. Which alert catches that?
    Two, at different levels. An absent() rule on the service's own metric fires when the series stops arriving, which distinguishes "no errors" from "no data" — these are identical on a graph and opposite in meaning, and every threshold rule silently evaluates to nothing when the data stops. Above that sits a dead-man's switch: a rule that always fires, routed to an external service that pages you when the heartbeat stops arriving. That is what catches the case where Prometheus itself, the alertmanager, or the notification path is broken — because no internal alert can report on the failure of the system that would deliver it. Both are cheap and both are routinely missing, which is why "we had no alerts" is a common line in postmortems where the truth is that there was no monitoring at all for several hours.
