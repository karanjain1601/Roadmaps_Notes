Chapter 08 · Source and servers

Caching and the edge

A cache is a second copy of an answer, kept somewhere cheaper to reach. That is the whole idea, and it is the highest-leverage performance work available — a 90% hit ratio means your origin handles a tenth of the traffic. It is also where the two hardest problems in the discipline live: knowing when a copy has stopped being true, and surviving the moment a popular copy expires.

The layers, and what each is actually good for

Caching happens at half a dozen places between a user and your database, and they are not interchangeable. The rule that orders them: the closer to the user, the cheaper the hit and the harder the invalidation. A browser cache costs you nothing and you cannot reach into it; a database query cache is fully under your control and saves you the least.

One-second micro-caching is absurdly effective and almost nobody uses it

Take an endpoint receiving 500 requests a second that is "uncacheable" because the data changes constantly. Cache it at the proxy for one second. The data is now at most one second stale — which for a dashboard, a feed or a product listing is invisible — and the origin goes from 500 requests a second to one. That is a 99.8% reduction for a single line of nginx configuration, and it applies to exactly the endpoints people assume caching cannot help with. The test is not "does this change often" but "does a one-second-old answer harm anyone".

The HTTP semantics that control all of it

Caches in the browser and at the edge are driven entirely by response headers. Getting these right is what makes the layers above work; getting them wrong is why so many sites either re-download everything or serve two-year-old JavaScript.

The two cache-key failures that become incidents

Caching a personalised response publicly. A response containing one user's name is stored by the CDN under a key that does not include their identity, and every subsequent visitor is served it. This is a data breach caused by a missing private, and it is depressingly common on pages that are public except for a logged-in header. The defence is to make personalisation structurally impossible to cache — render the shell publicly and fetch the personal part separately — rather than relying on getting the header right on every endpoint.

Vary cardinality explosion. Each header named in Vary multiplies the number of stored variants. Vary: User-Agent is the notorious one: there are effectively unlimited distinct user-agent strings, so the hit ratio collapses to near zero while storage fills with near-identical copies. Vary: Accept-Encoding is fine — two or three values. Vary: Accept-Language is fine if you normalise to a short list first. Never vary on anything high-cardinality, and check what your CDN adds to the key by default: cookies and query strings are frequently included whole, so one tracking parameter can make every URL unique.

Hit ratio, and why the last few points matter most

Request popularity is not uniform. It follows something close to a Zipf distribution — the nth most popular object gets roughly 1/ns of the traffic — which has two consequences that pull in opposite directions and are both important.

origin load  =  1 − hit ratio

Which looks trivial and is not, because it is a reciprocal relationship. Going from a 90% hit ratio to 95% does not improve things by 5% — it halves the origin load, from 10% of traffic to 5%. From 95% to 99% halves it twice more. The hard-won last points are worth more than all the easy early ones put together, and this is why CDN work that looks like diminishing returns on the hit-ratio chart is nothing of the sort on the origin-capacity chart.

Cache size against hit ratio, and what the origin sees
Drop the Zipf exponent to 0.6 — the shape of demand beats the size of the cache

The other consequence runs the other way. With a million objects and a Zipf exponent of 0.9, a cache holding the thousand most popular gets a 34.6% hit ratio; doubling it to two thousand adds only 4.7 points, and going all the way to ten thousand adds 17. Cache capacity has logarithmic returns, so buying your way to a high hit ratio is expensive. Meanwhile the same thousand-object cache against a flatter distribution — exponent 0.6 — manages only 6.0%. The shape of your demand matters far more than the size of your cache, which is why the productive work is usually making more requests cacheable rather than making the cache bigger.

Where the hit ratio actually comes from

In practice you rarely move the number by adding storage. You move it by removing the things that make otherwise identical requests miss: a tracking query parameter that makes every URL unique, a Set-Cookie on a static asset that makes the CDN refuse to store it, a Vary on something high-cardinality, an Authorization header sent on public requests, a missing trailing-slash normalisation. Every one of those turns one cacheable object into thousands of uncacheable ones. Before sizing anything, look at the requests that missed and ask why they were considered distinct.

The stampede

A cached entry expires. The next request misses and goes to the origin, which takes some time to answer. Every request arriving during that window also misses, because the entry is still not there — so they all go to the origin too. One expiry becomes a burst.

requests reaching the origin per expiry  =  1  +  request rate for that key  ×  origin latency

At 2,000 requests a second for one hot key and a 200 ms origin fetch, that is 401 simultaneous origin requests where you expected one. Note what is not in the formula: the TTL. Making the TTL longer does not make the burst smaller, only rarer — and rarer bursts are harder to diagnose, because the origin looks fine between them.

One expired key against your origin
Switch on coalescing, then compare stale-while-revalidate
Request coalescing collapses it to one
Also called single-flight or request collapsing. The cache notices that a fetch for this key is already in progress and makes the other 400 requests wait for that result instead of starting their own. nginx calls it proxy_cache_lock, Varnish does it by default, every serious CDN implements it, and in application code it is singleflight.Group in Go or a promise cache in Node. This is the fix, and it usually costs one configuration line.
Stale-while-revalidate removes the wait too
Coalescing still makes 401 clients wait 200 ms for the refresh. With stale-while-revalidate the cache serves the slightly stale copy instantly and refreshes in the background, so nobody waits at all and the origin still sees one request. The cost is bounded staleness, which for most content is a trade you would take every time.
Stampedes are a hot-key problem
Spread the same 2,000 requests a second across 5,000 keys and each key sees 0.32 requests a second, so amplification falls to 1.06× — essentially nothing. Bursts come from concentration, so the endpoint worth protecting is the one on the front page, not the long tail.
But synchronised expiry creates a fleet-wide one
If 5,000 keys were all populated at the same instant — a deploy, a cache flush, a cold start — their TTLs expire together and the origin gets 5,400 requests in one moment even though no individual key is hot. Adding ±20% jitter to each TTL spreads that same load to about 450 per second over twelve seconds, which the origin absorbs without noticing. Jitter costs nothing and should be the default on any TTL you set programmatically.
Cold start is the same failure wearing a different hat

Flushing the cache, deploying a change that alters the cache key, or bringing up a new CDN region all produce the same event: a full-traffic burst against an origin sized for a fraction of it. An origin comfortably serving 10% of 20,000 requests a second is being asked for all of it, and it will not survive. The mitigations are to warm the cache before shifting traffic, to shift traffic gradually rather than at once (chapter 18), and to have a concurrency limit at the origin that sheds load rather than collapsing — because an origin that returns 503 for the excess recovers, and one that accepts everything and thrashes does not.

Invalidation, and the strategy that avoids it

Purging is unreliable in a way that is easy to underestimate: it is a best-effort operation against systems you do not control, it takes seconds to minutes to reach every point of presence, and it cannot touch a browser cache at all. Every approach below is really a way of needing it less.

StrategyHowVerdict
Content-hashed URLs/app.4f2b91c.js — the URL changes when the bytes doThe best answer available. Nothing is ever invalidated because nothing is ever overwritten. Cache for a year
Short TTLLet it expireSimple, predictable, needs no API. Combine with stale-while-revalidate and it costs nothing in latency
Surrogate keysTag responses (product-42, category-shoes) and purge by tagThe right tool when one write invalidates many URLs. Fastly and Cloudflare both support it
Purge by URLCall the CDN's API per pathWorks, but you must know every affected URL, and a page can be reachable at several
Purge everythingFlush the whole cacheConverts a small correctness problem into the cold-start incident above. Reserve it for emergencies and expect the origin to feel it
Cache-aside, and the write pattern that avoids a class of bugs

For an application cache the standard pattern is cache-aside: on read, check the cache and populate on miss; on write, update the database and then delete the cache entry. Deleting rather than updating matters more than it looks. If two concurrent writers both update the cache, they can land in the opposite order to the way they hit the database, leaving the cache permanently holding the older value with nothing to correct it. Deleting has no such ordering hazard: whoever reads next repopulates from the current truth. The residual race — a reader repopulating with a stale value it read just before a write committed — is narrow, and a short TTL bounds how long it can persist.

At the CDN layer two more things are worth knowing. Tiered caching or an origin shield puts a second cache layer between the edge points of presence and your origin, so a miss at forty PoPs becomes one origin request rather than forty — the same coalescing idea applied geographically, and it often improves origin load more than any TTL change. And the edge can serve through an origin outage: stale-if-error plus a generous stale window means your CDN keeps answering with slightly old content while the origin is down, which converts a total outage into a degraded one for free.

Check yourself

Self-check

  1. Your CDN hit ratio is 90% and someone proposes work to reach 95%, arguing it is only a five per cent improvement. Why is that framing wrong?
    Because the number that matters is what reaches the origin, and that is one minus the hit ratio. At 90% the origin handles 10% of traffic; at 95% it handles 5%. The change halves origin load, and therefore halves the capacity you need, the database work you do and the size of the burst if the cache is ever flushed. Going on to 99% halves it twice more. The relationship is reciprocal, so the hard-won last points are worth far more than the easy early ones — the opposite of the diminishing-returns intuition the hit-ratio chart suggests. The corollary is that the same reasoning runs backwards during an incident: a hit ratio quietly dropping from 95% to 90% doubles origin load without any change in user traffic, which is why hit ratio deserves to be an alerting metric rather than a dashboard curiosity.
  2. An endpoint serving 500 requests a second is declared uncacheable because its data changes constantly. Is that reasoning sound?
    Usually not. The question is not how often the data changes but whether a slightly old answer harms anyone. Caching for one second takes the origin from 500 requests a second to one — a 99.8% reduction — while making the data at most one second stale, which for a dashboard, a feed, a product listing or a search result is imperceptible. This micro-caching applies precisely to the endpoints people assume caching cannot help with, and it costs one line of proxy configuration. The genuine exceptions are narrow: responses that differ per user, anything where a stale read enables an incorrect action such as a balance check immediately before a transfer, and anything legally required not to be stored. For those, private or no-store is right — but they are a much smaller set than "the data changes often" suggests.
  3. A hot key at 2,000 requests a second expires and the origin receives about 400 simultaneous requests. Explain, and give two fixes with their trade-offs.
    During the 200 ms the origin takes to answer the first miss, the entry is still absent, so every request arriving in that window also misses and is forwarded — 1 + 2000 × 0.2 = 401 origin requests from one expiry. Note that the TTL does not appear in that calculation: a longer TTL makes the burst rarer, not smaller, and rarer bursts are harder to diagnose because everything looks healthy in between. The first fix is request coalescing, where the cache recognises an in-flight fetch for the key and makes the other 400 requests wait on its result — nginx's proxy_cache_lock, Varnish by default, singleflight in Go. The origin sees one request; the cost is that 401 clients still wait 200 ms. The second is stale-while-revalidate, where the cache serves the expiring copy immediately and refreshes in the background: the origin still sees one request and nobody waits at all, at the cost of serving content that is briefly stale. Where the staleness is acceptable, the second is strictly better, and the two compose.
  4. No individual key is hot, yet the origin gets a large burst every few minutes. What is happening?
    Synchronised expiry. If many entries were populated at the same instant — after a deploy, a cache flush, a scale-up, or simply because the TTL is set from a fixed configuration value — then they all expire at the same instant too, and the origin receives one request per key simultaneously even though each key individually sees almost no traffic. In the demo, 5,000 keys populated together produce about 5,400 origin requests in a single moment. The fix is jitter: randomise each TTL by ±20% so expiries spread out. That same 5,400 becomes roughly 450 per second over twelve seconds, which the origin absorbs without noticing. Jitter costs nothing, and it should be the default anywhere a TTL is set programmatically — the same reasoning as adding jitter to retry backoff in chapter 6, and for exactly the same reason: synchronisation turns a manageable average into an unmanageable peak.
  5. Support reports that a logged-in user briefly saw another user's name on a page. What is the most likely cause?
    A personalised response was cached by a shared cache under a key that does not include the user's identity. Most likely the response was missing Cache-Control: private — or had public explicitly — so the CDN or reverse proxy stored one user's rendered page and served it to everyone requesting the same URL until it expired. It is a data breach rather than a performance bug, and the usual context is a page that is public except for a logged-in header. Adding private fixes the immediate case, and Vary: Authorization or Vary: Cookie makes the key identity-dependent — though varying on a whole cookie header is high-cardinality and destroys the hit ratio, so it is a poor general answer. The structural fix is to make it impossible: render the shell as a genuinely public, identical document and fetch the personalised fragment in a separate request marked private, no-store. Then no header mistake on any endpoint can produce this outcome.
  6. Why is Vary: User-Agent almost always a mistake while Vary: Accept-Encoding is fine?
    Because Vary adds the named header to the cache key, so the number of stored variants multiplies by that header's cardinality. Accept-Encoding has effectively three values — gzip, brotli, none — so you store three copies and each still gets a third of the requests: harmless, and necessary for correctness or you serve brotli to a client that cannot decode it. User-Agent has effectively unlimited distinct values, since every browser build, operating system version and bot has its own string. The cache stores a near-identical copy for each, the hit ratio collapses towards zero, and storage fills with duplicates. If you genuinely need to serve different content by device class, normalise first — have the edge compute a small derived value such as mobile/tablet/desktop and vary on that instead. The same reasoning applies to whatever your CDN includes in the key by default: query strings and cookies are frequently included whole, so a single tracking parameter can make every URL unique.
  7. You flush the entire CDN cache during business hours to fix one incorrect page. Predict what happens.
    Every subsequent request misses, so the origin — which was sized to handle perhaps 10% of total traffic — is suddenly asked for 100% of it. At 20,000 requests a second that is a tenfold overload arriving instantly, and the likely outcome is that the origin saturates, response times climb, and the cache repopulates slowly with responses that are themselves slow, extending the overload. It is the same event as a cold start, and it can easily be worse than the original incorrect page. The proportionate action is to purge the specific URL, or better to purge by surrogate key so that related URLs go together. If a full flush is genuinely necessary, do it during low traffic, warm the important paths first, and make sure the origin has a concurrency limit that sheds excess load with a 503 rather than accepting everything and thrashing — an origin that rejects the surplus recovers, and one that queues it does not.
