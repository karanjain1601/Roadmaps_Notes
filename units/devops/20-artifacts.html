Chapter 20 · Delivery

Artefacts, registries and supply chain

Chapter 17 built something and chapter 19 pointed a cluster at it. This chapter is about the thing in the middle: what it is, whether the name you deployed still refers to what you tested, how much of it you did not write, and whether you can prove where any of it came from. Most of the answers are cheap; the reason they are not universal is that none of them is visible until it matters.

A tag is a pointer; a digest is the thing

Chapter 10 established that an image is a manifest naming layers by content hash, and that a tag is a mutable pointer to a manifest digest. Everything in this section follows from taking that seriously.

ReferenceMeansVerdict
app:latestWhatever was pushed most recentlyTwo replicas started an hour apart can be running different code with no record of it
app:v1.4.2Whatever currently has that tagBetter, and still mutable. A retag is invisible to every manifest and every diff
app:abc1234 (commit sha)Whatever currently has that tagTraceable to a commit, still mutable, and it is the practical middle ground
app@sha256:…Exactly these bytes, or nothingThe only reference that cannot change under you. Deploy this

The usual objection is that digests are unreadable. The usual answer is to write both: app:v1.4.2@sha256:… is valid, the tag documents intent and the digest is what is resolved. Chapter 19's manifest bump can maintain it automatically.

Immutable tags at the registry, if it supports them
Most registries can reject a push to an existing tag. That turns retagging from a silent operation into an error, which is the cheapest possible fix for a whole class of problem.
Retention has to be deliberate
Every build pushes an image and nothing deletes them, so registries grow without bound. A policy that keeps the last N per branch, everything referenced by a running deployment, and everything tagged as a release covers it — with the second clause being the one people forget, and the one that turns a cleanup job into an outage.
Rollback depth is a retention setting
You can roll back exactly as far as the registry still has images. A thirty-day policy means a thirty-day rollback horizon, which is fine until the regression turns out to have shipped six weeks ago.

How much of this did you write?

Almost none of it. A modest application declares a few dozen direct dependencies, each of which brings its own, and the transitive closure is larger than most people would guess. That closure is the actual attack surface, and its size changes what "keeping up with vulnerabilities" can possibly mean.

Your dependency tree, and the cost of answering one question
Halve the direct dependencies and watch what happens to the total
The packages you chose are a rounding error
Forty direct dependencies become about 1,200 distinct packages after deduplication, so the ones you actually evaluated are 3.3% of the surface. Every review of "should we add this library" is a decision about roughly thirty packages, not one.
At that size, a critical vulnerability every year is a certainty
At 0.5% per package per year, the probability of at least one is 99.76% and the expected count is 6. So the operational question is never whether one will happen; it is how long it takes to find out whether it affects you.
And that cost varies by three orders of magnitude
Auditing forty repositories by hand costs about 480 hours a year. Grepping lockfiles costs 32. Querying an SBOM index costs half an hour — a 960× difference for the same six incidents.
Removing a direct dependency removes a subtree
Halving direct dependencies from forty to twenty removes 600 packages, not twenty. This is the only lever that reduces the surface rather than the response cost, and it is why "we could write this ourselves in forty lines" is sometimes the right answer.
An SBOM does not make you safer; it makes you fast

A software bill of materials is a list of every component in an artefact, in a standard format — SPDX or CycloneDX — generated at build time and stored alongside the image. It fixes nothing on its own. What it changes is the shape of a bad morning: when a widely-used library turns out to be compromised, the organisations that answer "which of our 340 services contain it, and at what version" in ten minutes are the ones with a queryable index of their SBOMs, and the ones taking three days are doing it by hand. Generate them in CI — docker buildx --sbom=true or Syft — attach them to the image, and index them centrally. The indexing is the part that gets skipped and the part that does the work.

Provenance: proving where an artefact came from

An SBOM says what is inside. Provenance says how it got there: which source revision, built by which system, with which parameters, at what time. It is a signed statement attached to the artefact, and it is the only control that detects a build that was compromised while it ran — because nothing in the source is wrong in that case.

SLSA levelRequiresDefends against
1Provenance exists and is availableNothing by itself; it makes the rest possible
2Built by a hosted service, provenance signed by itSomeone forging the provenance by hand
3Hardened, isolated builds; the builder's identity is non-forgeableA compromised build tenant tampering with another's output
Hermetic builds are what make provenance meaningful
A build that reaches out to the network mid-run can pull anything, so its provenance describes a process whose inputs are not fully known. Declaring all inputs up front — a lockfile, a pinned base image digest, no network in the build step — is what lets the statement "this artefact came from that source" be true.
Sign artefacts, and keyless is now the practical option
Cosign with OIDC signs using a short-lived certificate bound to the CI workload's identity, with the signature recorded in a public transparency log. No key to store, rotate or leak — chapter 21's problem avoided entirely rather than solved.
Signatures only matter if something checks them
An unverified signature is decoration. The check belongs at admission — Kyverno, Gatekeeper or a policy controller — rejecting any image not signed by your build identity and not carrying provenance for the expected source repository. Chapter 19's controller does not verify anything by itself.
Six attacks, seven controls
Try review plus scanning — then find what still gets through
No single control covers more than half

The best individual control catches 3 of 6 modelled attacks — admission verification, because it is the last gate and everything passes through it. Review plus dependency scanning, which is what most organisations have, catches 2 of 6 and specifically cannot see a compromised build: nothing in the source is wrong, so no amount of source review or dependency scanning detects a backdoor injected while the artefact was being produced. Only provenance answers that, because only provenance makes a claim about the build itself.

The pattern is that each control covers one link, and an attacker only needs the weakest. That is what makes the full chain — reviewed source, pinned and scanned dependencies, hermetic build with signed provenance, signature verified at admission, deployment by digest through a reconciler nobody can bypass — worth more than the sum of its parts. It is also why partial adoption plateaus: adding a fourth scanner does nothing for the links that have no control at all.

bash — the chain, end to end
# in CI: build with provenance and an SBOM attached to the image
docker buildx build --push \
  --provenance=mode=max --sbom=true \
  -t ghcr.io/org/app:${GITHUB_SHA} .

# sign it with the workflow's own OIDC identity — no key to store or rotate
cosign sign --yes ghcr.io/org/app@${DIGEST}

# anyone can verify, without a shared secret
cosign verify \
  --certificate-identity-regexp '^https://github.com/org/app/.github/workflows/.+' \
  --certificate-oidc-issuer https://token.actions.githubusercontent.com \
  ghcr.io/org/app@${DIGEST}

# and the provenance: which source, which builder, which parameters
cosign verify-attestation --type slsaprovenance \
  --certificate-identity-regexp '^https://github.com/org/app/.*' \
  --certificate-oidc-issuer https://token.actions.githubusercontent.com \
  ghcr.io/org/app@${DIGEST}

# the part that makes all of the above load-bearing: enforce it at admission.
# without this the signatures exist and nothing consults them

Registries, promotion and what to keep

Promote by reference, never by rebuild
Chapter 17's build-once rule ends here: staging and production reference the same digest, and promotion is a one-line change to a manifest. A "production build" that recompiles the same commit is a different artefact with a different set of transitive resolutions, so the test results do not transfer.
A pull-through cache is worth more than it looks
Mirroring upstream registries locally removes a hard dependency on Docker Hub being available and unthrottled during a scale-up — chapter 15's argument about upstream repositories, applied to images. It also gives you a chokepoint for scanning and for blocking a compromised upstream tag.
Registries hold more than images now
Helm charts, SBOMs, signatures, attestations and Terraform modules are all OCI artefacts. One store, one authentication model, one retention policy — and one place to apply the verification above.
Write the retention policy before you need it
Keep the last N per branch, everything a running deployment references, and every release tag indefinitely. The second clause is the one that gets omitted, and omitting it means a cleanup job can delete an image a running pod will need on its next restart.
The gate that is always red teaches everyone to override it

Chapter 10 made this point about scanners and it applies to the whole chapter. A policy that fails builds on any finding, in a dependency tree of 1,200 packages with six critical vulnerabilities a year and a great many low-severity ones, will be bypassed within a month — and the bypass will then be used for the finding that mattered. Gate on high and critical severity with a known fix, allow documented time-bounded exceptions that expire loudly, and reduce the surface so the remaining findings are plausibly relevant. A minimal base image is a supply-chain control precisely because it removes packages you were never going to patch.

Check yourself

Self-check

  1. Your deployment references app:v1.4.2 and someone reports that two replicas are behaving differently. How is that possible?
    A tag is a mutable pointer to a manifest digest, so v1.4.2 means "whatever currently carries that tag" rather than a fixed set of bytes. If anyone pushed to that tag after the first replica pulled, a replica started later pulls something different — and because the tag is unchanged, no manifest, no diff and no audit log shows it. The same mechanism means a rollback to v1.4.2 may not return what was there before. Deploy by digest, app@sha256:…, which is content-addressed and cannot change; write both if you want the tag for readability, since app:v1.4.2@sha256:… is valid and the digest is what is resolved. Enable immutable tags at the registry as well, which turns a retag from a silent operation into an error, and is the cheapest available fix for the whole class.
  2. How many packages does an application with forty direct dependencies actually depend on, and what follows?
    Roughly 1,200 after deduplication, so the packages anyone actually evaluated are about 3.3% of the surface. Two things follow. First, at a 0.5% annual probability of a critical vulnerability per package, the chance of at least one is 99.76% and the expected count is six a year — so a critical CVE is not an event to be prevented but a recurring operational task to be made cheap. Second, adding a direct dependency is a decision about roughly thirty packages rather than one, which is why "we could write this in forty lines" is occasionally the right answer: halving direct dependencies from forty to twenty removes six hundred packages, not twenty. That is the only lever that shrinks the surface itself; everything else in the chapter shrinks the cost of responding to it.
  3. What does an SBOM actually buy, given that it fixes no vulnerabilities?
    Speed of answering one question: which of our artefacts contain this component, at what version. In the model, six critical CVEs a year across forty repositories cost about 480 hours to answer by reading code, 32 hours by grepping lockfiles, and half an hour by querying an index of SBOMs — a 960× difference for identical incidents. That difference is the whole value, and it is entirely realised at the moment a widely-used library turns out to be compromised, when the organisations answering in ten minutes and the ones taking three days are distinguished by whether they built the index beforehand. Generate SBOMs in CI and attach them to the image, which is one flag; then index them centrally, which is the step that usually gets skipped and the step that does the work. An SBOM sitting unqueried next to an image is a compliance artefact rather than an operational one.
  4. You have branch protection, mandatory review and dependency scanning. Which supply chain attack do you still have no defence against?
    A compromised build. If an attacker controls the CI runner and injects a backdoor while the artefact is being produced, nothing in the source is wrong — so review sees a clean diff and dependency scanning sees a clean tree, because the malicious code was never in either. In the model those controls catch two of six attacks and specifically miss this one, along with an unsigned image pushed straight to the registry and a tag repointed at a bad image. The only control that addresses a compromised build is provenance: a signed statement that this artefact was produced from that source revision by that builder with those parameters, which is checkable and which a tampering build cannot forge if the builder identity is non-forgeable — SLSA level 3. And provenance only helps if something verifies it, which means an admission policy rejecting images whose attestation does not match the expected source.
  5. Why is admission verification the single highest-value control, and why is it still not sufficient?
    Highest value because it is the last gate and everything passes through it: an unsigned image, an image signed by the wrong identity, and an image applied by hand outside the normal path are all rejected at the same point, which is three of six attacks in the model. It is the only place where the properties established earlier are actually enforced rather than merely recorded. Not sufficient because it can only check what the artefact claims about itself — it cannot tell that a reviewed commit was malicious, that a dependency in the tree is compromised, or that the source it attests to was itself tampered with. Each control covers one link and an attacker needs only the weakest, which is why the chain is worth more than the sum of its parts and why adding a fourth scanner does nothing for links that have no control at all. It is also why the check must be enforcing rather than auditing; a policy in warn mode is a signature nobody consults.
  6. Your registry cleanup job deletes images older than thirty days and a pod fails to start. What went wrong?
    The policy did not exclude images that a running deployment still references. A pod that has been running for two months has its image cached on its node and needs no pull — until it is rescheduled, the node is replaced, or it crash-loops, at which point the pull fails with a manifest-unknown error and the workload cannot start. The failure appears long after the deletion and looks unrelated to it. A retention policy needs three clauses: keep the last N per branch, keep every release tag indefinitely, and keep anything referenced by a live deployment — the third being the one routinely omitted. It is also worth noting the related consequence: retention sets your rollback horizon, so a thirty-day policy means you cannot roll back to something that shipped six weeks ago, which is exactly when you discover a regression that has been latent.
  7. Your vulnerability gate fails builds on any finding and engineers have started using the override. What went wrong and what would you change?
    The gate stopped carrying information. With 1,200 transitive packages, a policy that fails on every finding is red essentially always, so the override becomes routine — and once it is routine it is used for the finding that mattered too. That is a worse position than not scanning, because it creates the appearance of a control while removing the signal. Three changes. Gate only on high and critical severity with a known fix available, so a red build means an action exists. Allow documented, time-bounded exceptions that expire loudly rather than a silent override, so the decision is recorded and revisited. And reduce the surface: a distroless or minimal base image removes hundreds of packages you were never going to patch, which makes the remaining findings plausibly relevant — a minimal base is a supply-chain control for exactly this reason, not only a size optimisation.
