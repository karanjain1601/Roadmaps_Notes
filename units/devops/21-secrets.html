Secrets management

A secret is a credential that grants access, and every practice around it exists to answer two questions: how long is it useful to whoever finds it, and how much does it reach. Both have numbers attached, and both are decided less by the tool you pick than by whether rotation is something a human does.

Where they actually leak

Almost nobody loses a secret to cryptanalysis. They lose it to a place nobody thought of as storage.

The hierarchy that follows

Ranked by how much they help: do not have a secret at all — OIDC federation, workload identity, IAM roles, keyless signing from chapter 20 — then issue it dynamically with a short lifetime, then store it centrally and inject it at runtime, then encrypt it at rest wherever it must sit. Most effort goes into the fourth rung. Most of the benefit is in the first two, and the first one is available far more often than people assume: chapters 13, 17 and 20 each removed a static credential entirely by replacing it with an identity.

Rotation interval and exposure window are the same dial

A leaked static credential is useful to whoever has it until it is rotated. Since a leak can happen at any point in the rotation period, the average exposure is half the interval — so the interval you can sustain determines the damage. And what determines the interval is how much work a rotation is.

effort ∝ secrets ÷ interval   ·   exposure ∝ interval

Exactly reciprocal, so a manual process trades one for the other one-for-one and there is no setting that is good at both. That is not a discipline problem; it is the shape of the equation, and the only way out is to make the numerator zero.

What a rotation policy costs and what it buys
Try weekly rotation, then switch to dynamic credentials
Ninety-day rotation is cheap and leaves a 45-day window
About 101 hours a year for sixty secrets, and an expected 54 secret-days of live exposure. Comfortable, and it means a credential leaked in January is still working in February.
Weekly rotation closes the window and costs 0.81 of a person
1,304 hours a year doing nothing but rotating secrets. This is why nobody does it, and why the policy that gets written down is ninety days — the exposure is a consequence of the effort, not of anyone's risk appetite.
Dynamic credentials break the trade rather than balancing it
A credential issued on demand with a one-hour lifetime gives a 30-minute average window at zero rotation effort — 2,160× less exposure than ninety-day manual rotation, while costing less. That is not a better point on the curve; it is a different curve.

Envelope encryption, which is why a KMS key is not a secret you hold

Encrypting a large amount of data directly with a key held in a hardware module would mean sending all of it there. Instead a random data key is generated per object, used to encrypt the payload locally, and then itself encrypted by the KMS key; the encrypted data key is stored next to the ciphertext. To decrypt, you send only the small encrypted data key to the KMS and get the plaintext key back. The consequences are what matter operationally: the master key never leaves the module, so it cannot be exfiltrated; every decryption is an API call, so it is logged and can be denied by policy; and revoking access to the KMS key instantly renders every object encrypted under it unreadable, without touching the objects. This is what sits under SOPS with a cloud key, S3 encryption and encryption at rest in etcd.

How much does one credential reach

The second question. A credential shared across the estate is convenient to issue, impossible to rotate — because rotation means coordinating every consumer — and it is copied into every place that uses it, which is what actually determines how likely it is to leak at all.

One shared credential against many scoped ones
Compare the expected loss, then look at the last row
A shared credential is not one copy, it is a hundred and twenty
Forty services across three environments means the same string is in 120 deployment targets, 120 config stores and 120 CI pipelines. At 0.5% leak probability per copy per year, its own annual leak probability is 45.2% — against 0.5% for one scoped to a single deployment. Sharing multiplies the exposure before blast radius is even considered.
Partitioning cuts the expected loss by an order of magnitude
Expected share of the estate compromised in a year: 45.2% shared, 1.5% per service, 0.5% per service per environment.
But the real change is the tail
The probability of losing everything at once is 45.2% with one shared credential and effectively zero once partitioned. Partitioning does not just lower the average — it removes the outcome you cannot recover from. This is chapter 13's account-boundary argument and chapter 9's blast-radius argument, in a third setting.
The price is 120 credentials instead of one
Which is 203 hours a year to rotate manually and therefore not going to happen. Fine granularity is only reachable when something issues credentials automatically — the two halves of this chapter are not independent choices.
The shared credential nobody can rotate

The specific failure worth recognising: a database password used by every service. Rotating it means changing it in every consumer simultaneously, because the old one stops working the moment it changes — so a rotation is a coordinated outage, so it never happens, so the credential is years old and in every backup, laptop and CI log accumulated since. The exit is not a heroic coordinated rotation. It is to add a second credential per service, migrate consumers one at a time, and retire the shared one when the last consumer has moved — chapter 18's expand-and-contract applied to credentials, with the same property that every intermediate state works.

Detection, and what to do in the first ten minutes

Scan continuously, in three places
A pre-commit hook for speed, a required CI check for enforcement, and a scan of the whole history — because chapter 5's point is that a secret committed two years ago is still live today. gitleaks and trufflehog do all three.
Assume automated exploitation is fast
Public repositories are scanned continuously by people who are not you, and a leaked cloud key is typically used within minutes. There is no window in which a quiet cleanup is a reasonable plan.
Canary tokens tell you it happened
A credential that does nothing except alert when used, placed in the repository, the image and the config store. It costs nothing and it is the only control here that detects use rather than presence.
Alert on the credential's own audit log
Every managed store records reads. An access from an unexpected principal, region or time is the signal, and it is available without any additional tooling.
  1. Revoke first. Not rotate — revoke. Chapter 5 put rotation first because it was discussing history rewriting; here the distinction matters, because issuing a new credential does not stop the old one working unless you explicitly invalidate it.
  2. Check what it did. The audit log for that credential, over its whole life, not just since the leak was noticed. You do not know when it leaked, only when you found out.
  3. Then clean up the artefact. Rewrite the history, rebuild the image, purge the log. This is remediation of the evidence, and it comes after the credential is dead.
  4. Ask the forge to garbage-collect. GitHub and GitLab keep unreachable objects fetchable by SHA after a force-push until support purges them — chapter 5.
  5. Fix the class. Why was a secret in a position to be committed? The structural answer is that application configuration comes from the environment or a store at runtime, so there is nothing sensitive in the repository to leak.
Kubernetes Secrets are not encrypted

They are base64-encoded, which is an encoding chosen so binary values survive YAML. Without EncryptionConfiguration they sit in etcd in plaintext, so anyone with an etcd backup has every secret in the cluster. Three things are worth checking on any cluster you inherit: encryption at rest is enabled with a KMS provider rather than a local key; get secrets is treated as a privileged verb in RBAC, since a role that can read secrets in a namespace can read every secret in it including other teams'; and secrets are mounted as files rather than environment variables, because a file has permissions and an environment variable is readable at /proc/<pid>/environ by anything sharing the PID namespace and is inherited by every child process the container spawns.

Check yourself

Self-check

  1. Your policy says rotate every ninety days. Someone proposes weekly. What does the arithmetic say?
    It halves-and-then-some the exposure and it will not happen. Rotation effort scales as secrets divided by interval and exposure scales with interval, so the two are exactly reciprocal and a manual process trades one for the other one-for-one. For sixty secrets at twenty-five minutes each, ninety-day rotation costs about 101 hours a year and leaves an expected 54 secret-days of live exposure; weekly costs about 1,304 hours — 0.81 of a full-time person doing nothing else — and leaves 4.2. Nobody will sustain the second, which is why the written policy is ninety days: the exposure is a consequence of the effort, not of anyone's risk appetite. The productive move is not to argue about the interval but to remove the numerator. A dynamic credential with a one-hour lifetime gives a thirty-minute average window at zero rotation effort, which is 2,160× less exposure than ninety-day rotation while costing less.
  2. Why is a credential shared across forty services more than forty times worse than one per service?
    Two effects compound. First, the shared credential is copied into every place that uses it — forty services across three environments is 120 deployment targets, config stores and pipelines — so at 0.5% leak probability per copy per year its own annual leak probability is 45.2%, against 0.5% for one scoped to a single deployment. Sharing multiplies how likely a leak is before blast radius is considered at all. Second, when it does leak it reaches 100% of the estate rather than 2.5% or 0.8%. Together, the expected share of the estate compromised in a year goes from 45.2% to 1.5% to 0.5%. But the more important number is the tail: the probability of losing everything simultaneously is 45.2% with one shared credential and effectively zero once partitioned. Partitioning does not mainly reduce the average — it removes the outcome you cannot recover from, which is the same argument as separate cloud accounts in chapter 13 and container blast radius in chapter 9.
  3. Your database password is shared by every service and has not been rotated in three years. How do you get out?
    Not with a coordinated rotation, which is precisely why it has not happened: changing it invalidates the old one immediately, so every consumer must switch at the same instant, so a rotation is a planned outage. Use expand and contract from chapter 18 instead. Create a second credential per service, alongside the existing one — every intermediate state works because both are valid. Migrate consumers one at a time, verifying each. When the audit log shows the shared credential has had no use for a safe interval, revoke it. That last step is the contract, and it is the irreversible one, so it comes last and on its own. Having done that, the per-service credentials are the right shape to be issued dynamically by a store, which is where you actually wanted to end up — and the migration is also the point at which you can scope each one to only the tables its service uses.
  4. A colleague says Kubernetes Secrets are encrypted because the values look scrambled. Correct them.
    That is base64, an encoding chosen so binary values survive YAML, and it is trivially reversible. Without an EncryptionConfiguration, secrets are stored in etcd in plaintext, so anyone holding an etcd backup holds every secret in the cluster. Three checks follow. Enable encryption at rest with a KMS provider rather than a locally-held key, so the master key never sits next to the data. Treat get secrets as a privileged RBAC verb, since a role that can read secrets in a namespace can read every secret in it — including ones belonging to other teams sharing that namespace. And mount secrets as files rather than environment variables: a file has permissions and can be re-read after rotation, while an environment variable is readable at /proc/<pid>/environ by anything sharing the PID namespace, is inherited by every child process, and frequently ends up in a crash dump or an error report.
  5. What is envelope encryption for, given that you could encrypt the data with the KMS key directly?
    You largely could not — a hardware security module is not designed to have gigabytes streamed through it, and doing so would be slow and expensive. Envelope encryption generates a random data key per object, encrypts the payload locally with it, and then encrypts only that small data key with the KMS key, storing the result alongside the ciphertext. Decryption sends just the encrypted data key to the KMS. Three operational consequences make it worth understanding rather than just using. The master key never leaves the module, so it cannot be exfiltrated even by someone with full access to your storage. Every decryption is an API call, so it is logged, rate-limited and deniable by policy — which is how you detect an attacker reading data they should not. And revoking access to the KMS key instantly makes every object encrypted under it unreadable without touching a single object, which is the fastest containment action available in a breach.
  6. You find an AWS key in a public repository committed six months ago. What is the first action, and why not history rewriting?
    Revoke the key. Not rotate — revoke, because issuing a replacement does not stop the old one working unless it is explicitly invalidated. Public repositories are scanned continuously by automation, and a leaked cloud key is typically used within minutes, so after six months you should assume it has been used and the question is what it did. History rewriting comes third: it removes the evidence, takes time, and is worthless while the credential is live. So the order is revoke, then review the audit log across the credential's whole lifetime rather than since you noticed, then rewrite history and rebuild any image containing it, then ask the forge to garbage-collect the unreachable objects it will otherwise keep serving by SHA. Then fix the class: application configuration comes from the environment or a store at runtime, so nothing sensitive is in the repository to leak, plus a scanner as both a pre-commit hook and a required CI check.
  7. Which control tells you that a leaked secret has actually been used, rather than that one exists?
    Two, and they are complementary. A canary token is a credential that grants nothing and alerts when anyone attempts to use it — placed in the repository, the image and the config store, it is silent until somebody who should not have it tries it, at which point you know both that a leak occurred and roughly where from. It costs nothing and it is the only control in the chapter that detects use rather than presence. The other is the audit log of the real credential: every managed secret store and every cloud IAM system records reads and authentications, so an access from an unexpected principal, region or time of day is a direct signal. Both matter because scanners only tell you that a secret is present somewhere you can see; neither tells you whether it has been copied. Alerting on unusual use is what closes that gap, and it is available in most estates without buying anything.
