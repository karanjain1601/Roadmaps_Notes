Chapter 07 · Source and servers

DNS, TLS and certificates

Two systems sit in front of every request, both mostly invisible until they are not. DNS decides where the request goes and caches that decision in more places than you control. TLS decides whether the connection is allowed at all, and fails on a schedule you set months earlier and then forgot. Between them they account for a startling share of outages, and almost all of those are avoidable with the material in this chapter.

How a name becomes an address

Resolution is a chain of caches with an authority at the end of it. Your application asks a stub resolver, which asks a recursive resolver, which — if nobody in the chain already knows — walks down from the root to the TLD to the authoritative server for the zone. Every step along the way is allowed to remember the answer for as long as the record's TTL says, and this is where all the operational surprises live.

  1. The application asks. Usually through getaddrinfo(), which consults /etc/nsswitch.conf, then /etc/hosts, then DNS. Some runtimes bypass this entirely and speak DNS themselves, which is why /etc/hosts sometimes does not work for a Go binary or a JVM.
  2. The stub resolver — systemd-resolved, dnsmasq, or nothing at all — may answer from its own cache. In a container this is normally the cluster's DNS service instead.
  3. The recursive resolver answers from cache if it can. This is the layer whose cache you have the least visibility into and the most dependence on.
  4. Otherwise it walks the tree. Root servers say who handles .com; the .com servers say who is authoritative for example.com; that server gives the answer. Each of those referrals is itself cached, usually for a long time.
  5. Everyone on the path caches the answer for its TTL — and, critically, they cache negative answers too, governed by the SOA record's minimum field rather than by the TTL of the record that does not exist.
"DNS propagation" is not a thing

Nothing is pushed anywhere. When you change a record, the authoritative server has the new answer immediately and every cache in the world keeps serving the old one until its own entry expires. So the delay you experience is not propagation, it is expiry — and that has a very different practical consequence: the fix is not to wait, it is to have lowered the TTL beforehand. Once you have made the change with a one-hour TTL in place, there is nothing you can do to speed it up.

The cutover procedure that actually works

Lower the TTL to 60 seconds at least one old-TTL period before the change — 24 hours ahead if the record is currently on a day-long TTL, because the caches have to expire once at the old value before they pick up the short one. Make the change. Verify against several public resolvers, not just your own. Keep the old target serving for well beyond the worst case, since a resolver that ignores TTLs is common and a client that caches forever is not rare. Then raise the TTL back once traffic on the old target has stopped. The demo shows the payoff: with a 3600 s TTL the worst-case client is stale for two hours; with 60 s it is under three minutes.

Three DNS traps worth recognising by sight

Negative caching outlives your fix. Someone typos a hostname, the resolver caches NXDOMAIN for the SOA minimum — often an hour or more — and the correction appears to do nothing. This is why a broken deploy that referenced a wrong name keeps failing after you have fixed the name, and why the SOA minimum is worth setting to something modest.

Split horizon disagreeing with itself. The same name resolves to a private address internally and a public one externally. It works until a workload runs somewhere that gets the wrong view — a CI runner, a VPN client, a pod using the cluster resolver instead of the corporate one — and you get a connection to the wrong environment or a mystifying timeout. Always check resolution from the host that is failing, never from your laptop.

The ndots amplifier in Kubernetes. The default ndots:5 means any name with fewer than five dots is first tried against every entry in the search path. So api.example.com — two dots — produces lookups for api.example.com.default.svc.cluster.local, then .svc.cluster.local, then .cluster.local, and only then the real name: four or five queries where one was needed, and for external names all but the last are NXDOMAIN. It shows up as inexplicable DNS load and added latency on every outbound call. Fix it with a trailing dot on the name to make it fully qualified, or by setting ndots:2 in the pod's dnsConfig.

bash — the DNS questions worth being able to answer quickly
dig +short api.example.com              ← just the answer
dig +trace api.example.com              ← the full walk from the root. shows WHICH server is wrong
dig @1.1.1.1 api.example.com            ← ask a specific resolver. compare several before
dig @8.8.8.8 api.example.com              concluding a change has "not propagated"
dig +nocmd api.example.com +noall +answer   ← shows the REMAINING TTL, counting down.
                                              that number tells you how long this cache stays stale
dig SOA example.com +short              ← last field is the negative-cache duration
dig CAA example.com +short              ← why your certificate issuance is failing

# what the machine will ACTUALLY do — which is not always what dig does
getent hosts api.example.com            ← goes through nsswitch, so it sees /etc/hosts too
resolvectl query api.example.com        ← systemd-resolved's view, including which link answered
resolvectl statistics                   ← cache hit rate; a sudden miss rate spike is the ndots problem

What TLS actually establishes

A TLS handshake does three things: agrees a cipher suite and protocol version, proves the server's identity, and derives session keys. TLS 1.3 completes it in one round trip — down from two in 1.2 — which is why it is worth insisting on: over a 60 ms path that is 60 ms saved on every new connection, and chapter 4 showed how many new connections a busy service makes.

ClientHello
Supported versions and cipher suites, and — importantly — SNI: the hostname the client wants. SNI is sent in the clear, and it is how one IP address serves certificates for hundreds of names. A client that omits it gets whatever default certificate the server chooses.
ServerHello and Certificate
The chosen suite plus the server's certificate and the intermediates it wants you to use. The root is not sent; it must already be in your trust store.
Key exchange
Ephemeral Diffie-Hellman, which gives forward secrecy: recording the traffic and stealing the private key later does not decrypt it. Non-ephemeral suites are gone from TLS 1.3 for exactly this reason.
Validation, on the client
Build a chain from the leaf to something in the trust store; check every certificate's validity window against the local clock; check the hostname against the leaf's SAN list — the Common Name has been ignored by browsers for years; check revocation, weakly.

Nearly every certificate incident is one of a handful of specific failures in that last step, and they are distinguishable by which clients break.

Break the chain, and see who notices
Try "intermediate not served" — the browser is fine and curl is not
"It works in my browser but not from the server"

This is almost always a missing intermediate. Browsers implement AIA chasing: when the chain is incomplete they read the Authority Information Access extension and fetch the missing certificate themselves, so the page loads and you conclude the certificate is fine. curl, OpenSSL, Java and Go do not do this by default, so every server-to-server call fails with unable to get local issuer certificate. The server is misconfigured in both cases; only one of the two clients is forgiving enough to hide it. Check with openssl s_client -connect host:443 -servername host and count the certificates in the chain it prints — if there is only one, the server is not sending the intermediate.

bash — certificate triage
# the whole chain, as the server actually sends it
openssl s_client -connect api.example.com:443 -servername api.example.com -showcerts </dev/null

# just the dates and names — the two things that are usually wrong
openssl s_client -connect api.example.com:443 -servername api.example.com </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -subject -issuer -dates -ext subjectAltName

# does the chain verify WITHOUT the browser's help?
curl -vI https://api.example.com 2>&1 | grep -E 'SSL|subject|issuer|expire'

# is SNI what makes it work? compare these two
openssl s_client -connect 203.0.113.10:443 -servername api.example.com </dev/null
openssl s_client -connect 203.0.113.10:443 </dev/null      ← no SNI: default vhost

# days remaining, for a monitor
echo | openssl s_client -connect api.example.com:443 -servername api.example.com 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -enddate

ACME, renewal and the failure that is always the same

ACME automates issuance: you prove control of the domain, the CA issues a certificate, and a client renews it before expiry without anyone involved. Let's Encrypt issues for 90 days and clients renew at 30 days remaining, which gives a full month of failed attempts before anything breaks — the design is deliberately forgiving. Certificates still expire in production regularly, and the causes are a short list.

ChallengeProves control bySuitsFails when
HTTP-01Serving a token at /.well-known/acme-challenge/ on port 80A single public web serverPort 80 is closed, a redirect to HTTPS breaks the path, or the load balancer sends the challenge to a different instance than the one holding the token
DNS-01Publishing a TXT recordWildcards, internal hosts with no public port, and anything behind a firewallThe DNS provider's API credentials expire, or a long TTL on the challenge record slows validation
TLS-ALPN-01A special certificate on port 443Servers that cannot use port 80Anything terminating TLS in front of you
The five reasons certificates expire despite automation

Renewal succeeded and nothing reloaded. The new file is on disk and the process is still serving the old one from memory. Every renewal hook must reload the server — systemctl reload nginx — and the monitoring must check what is served on port 443, not what is in the file.

The renewal timer stopped. A host rebuilt without re-enabling it, a cron removed, a container that renews at start-up and then runs for six months. Alert on the timer's last success, not only on the expiry date.

A CAA record was tightened. Someone adds CAA naming one CA and every other issuer starts refusing. It fails silently for 30 days and then the site goes down.

Rate limits during an incident. Let's Encrypt allows 5 duplicate certificates per week. A crash-looping container that requests on every start burns through that in minutes, and then cannot renew for a week.

The one certificate nobody automated. A partner's mutual-TLS client certificate, an internal CA-issued certificate on a database, a code-signing key. Automation covers the public web endpoint and the outage comes from somewhere else. Inventory every certificate and alert on all of them at 30, 14 and 7 days — and make the alert page someone, because an email to a departed employee is the classic ending to this story.

On the private side, mutual TLS reverses the check: the client presents a certificate too, and the server validates it against its own CA. That gives cryptographic service identity rather than "whoever can reach this port", which is the foundation of zero-trust networking between services. It also multiplies the certificate problem by the number of workloads, which is precisely why it is only practical when something issues and rotates them automatically — chapter 12 covers the service mesh doing exactly that, with certificate lifetimes measured in hours rather than months.

Check yourself

Self-check

  1. You changed an A record forty minutes ago. The TTL is 3600. Some users reach the new server, most do not, and a colleague says it is "still propagating". What is actually happening, and what should you have done?
    Nothing is propagating, because nothing is pushed. The authoritative server has had the new answer since the moment you saved it; every cache between your users and it is serving the old one until its own entry expires. Because caches populate at random moments, the remaining life of any given entry is roughly uniform between zero and the TTL, so after forty minutes of a 3600 s TTL about a third of resolvers have picked up the change — and the chain of caches below them adds more delay on top, so the worst case is closer to two hours than to one. There is no action that speeds this up. The fix belongs earlier: lower the TTL to 60 s at least one full old-TTL period before the change, so that every cache is already holding a short-lived entry when you make it. Keep the old target serving well past the worst case, because some resolvers ignore TTLs and some clients cache forever.
  2. An old Java service still connects to the decommissioned database an hour after the DNS change, while everything else moved immediately. Why?
    The JVM has its own DNS cache, and historically networkaddress.cache.ttl defaulted to caching successful lookups forever when a security manager was installed — and to 30 seconds otherwise, which is fine. A long-running process that resolved the name at start-up therefore never asks again, and no TTL you set on the record has any effect on it at all. The general lesson is that any cache in the chain which ignores TTLs makes the record's TTL irrelevant for that client: your control ends at the last layer that honours it. Fix by setting networkaddress.cache.ttl explicitly to something small, restarting the process, or — structurally — putting a load balancer in front so the address never needs to change. The demo makes this visible: enable the caching client and the "fully switched over" time becomes never.
  3. Your certificate works in every browser and every server-to-server call fails with "unable to get local issuer certificate". Diagnose it.
    The server is not sending the intermediate certificate, so the chain from the leaf to the trusted root is incomplete. Browsers hide this: they read the Authority Information Access extension on the leaf and fetch the missing intermediate themselves, a behaviour called AIA chasing. curl, OpenSSL, Java and Go do not do that by default, so they cannot build a path to anything in their trust store and reject the connection. Confirm with openssl s_client -connect host:443 -servername host -showcerts and count the certificates returned — one means the intermediate is missing. The fix is on the server: serve the full chain file rather than the leaf alone. Note the direction of the error: it is a server misconfiguration that only some clients are strict enough to notice, so "it works in Chrome" is not evidence that it is correct.
  4. A client reports certificate errors from one machine only. Everything else is fine and the certificate has 45 days left. What do you check first?
    The clock. If the machine's time is significantly ahead, a perfectly valid certificate reads as expired; if significantly behind, it reads as not yet valid. Both present as certificate errors with no hint that time is involved, and both are common on machines that have lost NTP, on virtual machines resumed from a snapshot, and on hardware with a dead RTC battery. Check with timedatectl and compare against a known-good source. Two other single-machine causes are worth ruling out next: an outdated or damaged trust store missing the issuing root, and a corporate TLS-inspecting proxy substituting its own certificate — which produces an unknown-issuer error on any machine whose trust store lacks the proxy's CA. Chapter 4 makes the same point about NTP: clock skew silently breaks TLS validation, token expiry and log correlation together.
  5. Why is a CNAME illegal at the zone apex, and what do providers offer instead?
    A CNAME says "this name is an alias; look up the target instead", and the standard requires that a name with a CNAME have no other records at all. The apex — example.com itself — necessarily has an SOA record and NS records, so a CNAME there would contradict the requirement, and resolvers behave unpredictably if you force one in. This matters because most managed load balancers and CDNs give you a hostname rather than a fixed IP, so the obvious configuration is exactly the illegal one. Providers solve it with a non-standard record type — ALIAS, ANAME or "CNAME flattening" — where the authoritative server resolves the target itself and returns the resulting A records, so what the client sees is a valid apex A record. It works well; just be aware it is provider-specific behaviour rather than something the protocol guarantees, and that it means your DNS provider is now doing a lookup on every query.
  6. Kubernetes pods are generating five times the expected DNS query volume and outbound calls have an extra 10 ms. Explain.
    The ndots:5 default in the pod's /etc/resolv.conf. Any name containing fewer than five dots is treated as possibly relative, so the resolver tries it against every entry in the search path before trying it as absolute. For api.example.com — two dots — that means queries for api.example.com.<namespace>.svc.cluster.local, then .svc.cluster.local, then .cluster.local, each returning NXDOMAIN, before the real query is finally sent. Four or five round trips where one was needed, on every resolution, multiplied by every pod: it shows up as load on the cluster DNS service and as latency on every external call. Two fixes: append a trailing dot to make the name fully qualified so the search path is skipped entirely, or set dnsConfig.options ndots:2 on the pod. Also worth checking is whether negative caching is enabled in the cluster resolver, since without it those NXDOMAIN answers are re-fetched every time.
  7. Certificate renewal is automated and has been running for a year. The site goes down on expiry day anyway. Give three plausible causes.
    First and most common: renewal succeeded but nothing reloaded. The new certificate is on disk while the running process continues serving the old one from memory, so file-based monitoring reports everything as fine right up to the moment it expires. Every renewal hook must reload the server, and the monitoring must check what is served on port 443 rather than what is in the file. Second: the timer stopped some months ago — a rebuilt host, a removed cron entry, or a container that only renews at start-up and has been running since. The failure is thirty days old and nobody alerted on it, which is why you alert on the renewal's last success rather than only on the expiry date. Third: something started refusing issuance — a CAA record tightened to name a different CA, expired DNS provider credentials for the DNS-01 challenge, or a rate limit consumed by a crash-looping container requesting a certificate on every restart. A fourth worth adding is that the expiring certificate was never in the automation at all: a mutual-TLS client certificate or an internal CA-issued one that somebody installed by hand.
