Chapter 23 · Running it

Reliability, incidents and cost

Chapter 1 opened with recovery time as one of four numbers. This is the chapter about the people who produce it, at two in the morning, and about the question nobody asks before setting an availability target: what is the last nine actually worth. Both have arithmetic behind them, and in both cases the arithmetic contradicts the usual instinct.

On-call, priced honestly

The instinctive fix for a heavy rotation is to add people to it. That does help — it makes each shift less frequent — but it changes nothing about what a shift is like, because the person on call absorbs every page that week regardless of how many colleagues they have.

What a rotation costs the people in it
Double the rotation size, then instead halve the page rate
Rotation size changes frequency, not intensity
Going from six people to twelve halves annual pages — 217 to 108 — and leaves a single shift exactly as bad: twenty-five pages either way. If the complaint is "my on-call week is unbearable", more people is not the answer to it.
The page rate is the only thing that fixes the shift
Cutting from 25 pages a week to 5 takes broken nights from 43 to 13 a year and the shift from unsurvivable to routine. At 25 pages a week with 35% out of hours, 71% of on-call nights are interrupted.
Interrupted nights are a working-time cost, not just an unpleasantness
A broken night costs roughly a day of degraded output, so the heavy rotation is 43 degraded working days a year per person — 19% of the year — spread across everyone in it.
And it is self-reinforcing
Annual pages per person as the rotation shrinks: 217, 260, 325, 433. Each departure raises the load on those remaining by more than the share that left, which makes the next departure more likely. A rotation losing people is not a staffing problem to be solved by hiring; it is a page-rate problem that hiring temporarily masks.
The rules that make it survivable

Cap the page rate, and treat exceeding it as a defect. A widely used ceiling is about two pages per shift-day; above that the shift is not a shift, it is a job. Google's version is that if a service consistently exceeds its page budget, the team hands the pager back to the developers until it does not — which sounds punitive and is really just making the cost visible to whoever can reduce it.

Every page must be actionable, documented and owned (chapter 22). Time spent on-call is work, so it is compensated and it comes out of the sprint rather than on top of it. The week after a bad shift is lighter, deliberately. And a minimum of six or eight people, because below that a holiday or a departure breaks the rotation entirely — if you cannot staff that, follow-the-sun with another team is a better answer than a four-person rota.

The first ten minutes

Incidents go badly for organisational reasons far more often than technical ones: several people investigating the same hypothesis, nobody talking to the business, and no one person able to say what is happening. Incident command is a small amount of structure that removes those failures.

Incident commander

Owns the incident, not the fix. Decides what is tried, keeps a timeline, and explicitly does not debug — the moment the commander is in a terminal, nobody is coordinating. The first responder takes this role by default until they hand it over.

Operations lead

The person actually making changes. One person touches production; everyone else proposes. This is the rule that prevents two simultaneous fixes producing a third problem nobody can attribute.

Communications lead

Updates the status page and stakeholders on a fixed cadence, whether or not there is news. "Still investigating, next update in fifteen minutes" is a complete and useful update, and it stops five executives interrupting the commander to ask.

  1. Declare it. Early and cheaply. The cost of declaring an incident that turns out to be minor is a few minutes; the cost of not declaring one is that nobody joins for an hour. If you are wondering whether it is an incident, it is.
  2. Stop the bleeding before understanding it. Roll back, disable the feature flag, shed load, fail over. Diagnosis is what the postmortem is for. Chapter 18's rollback and chapter 19's caveat about a reconciler reverting your manual fix both belong at this step.
  3. Ask what changed. Overwhelmingly it is a deploy, a config change, a feature flag, a certificate expiry (chapter 7), a scheduled job, or a dependency's own incident. Check the deployment timeline first, every time.
  4. Work the layers, do not guess. Chapter 4's ladder and chapter 2's USE method exist so that a tired person can be systematic instead of clever. Chapter 22's order — metrics say where, traces say which hop, logs say why — is the same discipline for a distributed system.
  5. Keep a timeline as you go. In the incident channel, with timestamps. Reconstructing it afterwards produces a fiction, and the timeline is the single most valuable input to the postmortem.
  6. Hand over properly if it runs long. An explicit handover of the commander role with a written summary. Fatigue causes the second incident.
Metastable failure, and why the incident continues after the cause is gone

Chapter 6 introduced the shape: retries amplify load during a partial outage, so the retry traffic becomes the load and the system stays down after the original trigger has cleared. Chapter 8's cache stampede and cold-start problem is another instance, and chapter 11's crash-looping pod a third. The operational signature is that the thing you fixed is fixed and the symptoms persist — and the resolution is almost always to shed load: reduce traffic, disable retries, drain the queue, or restart with a cold-start plan. Adding capacity into a metastable failure usually feeds it. It is worth recognising by name during an incident, because the instinct is to keep looking for a cause that is no longer there.

Postmortems that find causes

Chapter 1 stated the technical argument for blamelessness: if naming a cause gets someone punished, causes stop being named and you lose the only data that would prevent recurrence. Blameless does not mean nobody is accountable — it means the question is what about the system made this action reasonable at the time rather than who did it.

A weak postmortem saysA useful one says
"Engineer ran the wrong command""The command's dry-run and destructive forms differ by one flag, and the confirmation prompt says the same thing for both"
"Should have been more careful""There was no staging environment where this could have been tried, so the first execution was in production"
"Root cause: human error""Four conditions had to coincide, and three of them had been true for months"
"Added a warning to the runbook""Made the destructive path require an explicit resource name, which cannot be satisfied by muscle memory"
There is rarely a root cause
Complex systems fail when several conditions coincide, most of which were present and harmless for a long time. Asking for the root cause forces the answer into a single box and the box is usually a person. Ask instead what would have had to be different, and expect several answers.
Counterfactuals are not analysis
"If only they had checked the dashboard" describes a world that did not happen. The useful question is why the dashboard was not checked — was it not on the runbook, did it not exist, was it showing a mean where a percentile was needed, chapter 22.
Action items need an owner, a date and a size
A postmortem with fourteen actions produces zero. Three that are actually done beat fourteen that are filed. And distinguish between preventing this exact failure and reducing the class — the second is usually worth more and is usually the one that gets dropped.
Publish them internally
The value of a postmortem is largely to people who were not in the incident. A searchable archive is also the fastest way for a new engineer to learn how the system really behaves, which no documentation achieves.
Error budgets turn this into a policy instead of an argument

Chapter 22's budget gives a mechanical answer to the recurring conflict between shipping and stability. Budget remaining, spend it: ship, take risks, run the experiment. Budget exhausted: feature work pauses and reliability work takes priority until it recovers. The value is that the decision is agreed in advance and made by a number rather than by whoever argues hardest during the week it matters. It also cuts both ways, which is the part usually forgotten — a team with budget consistently left over is being too cautious, and should be shipping more aggressively, since unspent budget is unused capacity for change.

What a nine is worth

Availability targets are usually set by aspiration. They can be set by arithmetic instead, because both sides of the trade are measurable: the money you spend pursuing reliability rises steeply with each nine, and the money lost to the remaining downtime falls by exactly a factor of ten. Two curves moving in opposite directions have a minimum.

total  =  reliability spend(n)  +  525,600 × 10−n × cost per minute

Each nine multiplies the engineering cost — more redundancy, more testing, more sophisticated delivery, more people — while dividing the downtime by ten. Where the two lines cross is the availability worth having, and it depends entirely on what a minute of downtime actually costs you.

The economically optimal availability
Move the cost of a minute from $50 to $50,000 and watch the optimum shift
The optimum moves with the cost of a minute, not with ambition
3.21 nines when a minute costs $500; 4.46 when it costs $50,000. Same engineering, same organisation — the right target is different because the thing being protected is different.
Over-shooting is quantifiable and large
Demanding five nines when a minute costs $500 spends $3.2M a year to avoid about $161k of downtime — roughly $2.8M worse than the optimum. That is a number you can take to the conversation, and it is far more persuasive than an opinion about feasibility.
At the optimum the two costs are balanced
Reliability spend $268k against residual downtime cost $161k. If your reliability spend is a hundred times your expected downtime cost, you are past the optimum and can say so precisely.
Making nines cheaper is the real lever
If each nine costs 2.5× the last instead of 4×, the optimum rises from 3.21 to 3.52. That is what better delivery practice buys: not reliability directly, but a lower price per nine, so more of them become worth purchasing. Which is chapter 1's argument, arriving one last time.
Two things the model deliberately leaves out

It treats downtime as uniformly costly, and it is not — an outage during a sale, a market open, or a regulatory window costs a multiple of one at 4am on a Sunday, which is an argument for weighting availability by time rather than for raising the target overall. And it ignores reputational and contractual costs, which are lumpy: an SLA with penalties, or a single outage that loses an enterprise customer, can dominate everything above. Both push the optimum up, and neither changes the structure — you still get a minimum, and the useful discipline is putting a number on the minute before arguing about the nines. Chapter 13's ceiling still applies on top: past about four nines the binding constraint is your own change process, so the money has to go there rather than into redundancy.

Finding out before it finds you

Chaos engineering is an experiment, not vandalism
State a hypothesis — "if one availability zone fails, error rate stays under 0.5%" — then inject the failure in a controlled way and see whether the hypothesis holds. If you cannot state the hypothesis, you are not ready to run the experiment.
Start in staging, with a blast radius you have chosen
And a stop button. The point is to learn something before production teaches you the same thing at a worse moment, not to prove that production can be broken.
Game days test the humans
Most of what fails during an incident is organisational: the runbook is wrong, the alert routes to someone who left, nobody can find the status page credentials, the failover procedure has never been executed by the person now executing it. A scheduled exercise finds all of that at a time of your choosing, and it is usually more valuable than the technical result.
Test the restore, not the backup
Chapter 13's shared-responsibility table put this in the row that never moves. A backup that has never been restored is a hypothesis. Restore it on a schedule, time it, and compare the elapsed time against what you have promised — because the recovery time objective you wrote down and the one you can actually achieve are different numbers until you measure.

Check yourself

Self-check

  1. Your on-call rotation is exhausting people and you get budget for two more engineers. Does that fix it?
    It halves the frequency and changes nothing about the experience. The person on call absorbs every page that week regardless of how many colleagues exist, so going from six to twelve people takes annual pages per person from 217 to 108 while leaving a single shift at twenty-five pages either way. If the complaint is "my week on call is unbearable" — which it usually is — more people does not address it. The page rate is the only lever that does: cutting from 25 a week to 5 takes broken nights from 43 a year to 13 and the shift from unsurvivable to routine. Adding people is worth doing if the rotation is below six or eight, because a holiday or a departure otherwise breaks it entirely. But treating a heavy rotation as a staffing problem masks the defect and the masking is temporary, since each departure raises the load on the rest by more than the share that left.
  2. Why does a shrinking rotation get worse faster than the headcount suggests?
    Because load per person scales as pages × 52 ÷ rotation size, which is a reciprocal. Going from six people to five is a 17% reduction in headcount and a 20% increase in annual pages each — 217 to 260. Five to four is 325, four to three is 433. So each departure makes the remaining shifts heavier, which makes the next departure more likely, which makes them heavier again. That is why a rotation losing people rarely stabilises on its own and why hiring into it buys time rather than a fix: the new person arrives into the same page rate. Breaking the loop needs the page rate reduced — every page actionable, documented and owned per chapter 22, and a hard ceiling treated as a defect rather than an aspiration. Google's version of that ceiling is to hand the pager back to the developing team until the service is under budget, which is less punitive than it sounds: it puts the cost in front of the people who can remove it.
  3. An executive asks for five nines. Downtime costs your business about $500 a minute. What do you say?
    That it costs about $3.2M a year to avoid roughly $161k of downtime, which is around $2.8M a year worse than the optimum. At $500 a minute the economically optimal target is about 3.2 nines, where reliability spend of roughly $268k and residual downtime cost of roughly $161k are balanced — which is what an optimum means. The useful move is to reverse the question: ask what a minute of downtime actually costs, because once that number exists the target follows from arithmetic rather than from ambition, and the conversation stops being about whether the team is committed enough. Two honest caveats to add. Downtime is not uniformly costly, so weighting availability by time of day or business period may be the real requirement. And chapter 13's ceiling applies regardless: past about four nines the binding constraint is your own change process, so the money would have to go into delivery practice rather than redundancy anyway.
  4. What does it mean that making each nine cheaper raises the optimal target?
    It means the target is a function of your engineering practice rather than a fixed property of the business. In the model, changing the cost multiplier from 4× per nine to 2.5× moves the optimum from 3.21 nines to 3.52 with everything else unchanged — because more nines have become worth purchasing. That is precisely what the rest of this unit buys: automated release mechanics lower the cost of the deploy, progressive delivery lowers the cost of a bad change, good observability lowers detection time, and each of those reduces the price of the next nine. So the answer to "how do we get more availability" is often not "spend more on redundancy" but "make change cheaper and safer", which is chapter 1's argument arriving from the cost side. It also explains why two organisations with identical infrastructure can rationally choose different targets.
  5. A postmortem concludes "root cause: engineer ran the wrong command". What is wrong with it?
    Three things. It is not a cause, it is a description of the trigger — the analysis stopped exactly where it should have started. It is a person, which means the next person with information about a near-miss will keep it to themselves, and you have traded away the only data that prevents recurrence. And it implies a single root cause, when complex systems fail because several conditions coincide, most of which had been true and harmless for months. Better questions: why do the safe and destructive forms of the command differ by one flag; why did the confirmation prompt not distinguish them; why was there no environment where this could be tried first; why did it take eleven minutes to notice. Each of those has a fix that removes a class of failure rather than this instance, and none requires anyone to be more careful. Blameless does not mean nobody is accountable — it means the question is what about the system made this action reasonable at the time.
  6. You fixed the cause of an incident and the symptoms continue. What is likely happening?
    A metastable failure: the system is now sustaining itself on the consequences of the original problem, so removing the trigger does not remove the condition. The usual mechanism is retry amplification from chapter 6 — every layer retrying turns a partial failure into a multiple of the original load, and that load persists after the trigger clears. Chapter 8's cache stampede is another instance, since a flushed or cold cache sends full traffic at an origin sized for a fraction of it, and chapter 11's crash-looping pods are a third. The resolution is almost always to shed load rather than add capacity: reduce traffic, disable retries, drain the queue, warm the cache before restoring traffic, or bring things back gradually. Adding capacity into a metastable failure often feeds it. It is worth naming during the incident, because the instinct is to keep hunting for a cause that is no longer there.
  7. Your disaster recovery plan says four-hour recovery. How do you know?
    You do not, until you have done it. A recovery time objective that has never been executed is an estimate, and the gap between the estimate and reality is routinely a factor of several — because the restore is slower than expected, the runbook references a system that was replaced, the person who wrote it has left, the credentials are in a password manager that is itself down, or the backup turns out to be of the wrong thing. Chapter 13's shared responsibility table put "backups actually restoring" in the row that never moves to the provider whatever service you buy. So restore on a schedule, into an isolated environment, time it end to end, and compare the measured number against the promised one. Do it as a game day with the people who would actually be doing it at 3am rather than the person who wrote the plan, because most of what fails in a recovery is organisational rather than technical — and that is exactly what a scheduled exercise is for.
