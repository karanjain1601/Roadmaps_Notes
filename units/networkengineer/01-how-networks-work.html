How a network actually works

A laptop on an office desk fetches a page from a server in another country. This chapter follows that one packet through every device it touches, and then accounts for the elapsed time — because knowing where the milliseconds go is what separates guessing from diagnosis.

1.2The four delays

Every millisecond a packet spends in transit belongs to one of four categories. They behave completely differently, and confusing them is the most common source of wrong conclusions about a network.

What each is, and what changes it
DelayCauseReduced byNot reduced by
PropagationDistance divided by the speed of light in the medium — about 200,000 km/s in fibre, or 5 µs per kilometreA shorter path. Nothing elseBandwidth. A 100 Gbps link to Sydney is exactly as far away as a 10 Mbps one
SerialisationTime to clock the bits onto the wire: packet size divided by link rateMore bandwidth, or smaller packetsDistance
QueueingWaiting behind other packets in a bufferLower utilisation, or better queue managementMore buffer — that makes it worse. See below
ProcessingHeader inspection, table lookup, any deep inspectionHardware forwarding, simpler policyUsually negligible until something inspects payloads
Bandwidth and latency are not the same resource

They are confused constantly, including by people who should know better, and the confusion produces expensive mistakes: upgrading a circuit to fix a latency complaint, then discovering nothing improved. Bandwidth is how many bits per second the link carries; latency is how long one bit takes to arrive. Adding bandwidth reduces serialisation delay and relieves queueing, and it does nothing whatsoever to propagation, which is set by distance and physics. A useful mental image: bandwidth is the number of lanes on the motorway, latency is its length. Widening it moves more cars per hour and does not shorten the journey. The corollary matters for design — if the application is slow because of round trips, the fix is fewer round trips or a closer server, never a bigger pipe.

1.3Queueing, and why buffers hurt

Queueing delay against utilisation

The relationship is not linear. It is the single most useful curve in network capacity planning.

Bufferbloat

Memory became cheap, so vendors fitted generous buffers, reasoning that a dropped packet is worse than a delayed one. For TCP that reasoning is backwards. TCP discovers the available capacity by increasing its rate until packets drop — the loss is the signal (Chapter 9). A deep buffer suppresses that signal: instead of dropping, the router accumulates a queue hundreds of milliseconds long, TCP keeps accelerating because nothing tells it to stop, and every flow through that link acquires the full queueing delay. The symptom is distinctive and widely experienced — a large upload makes an unrelated video call unusable, on a link with plenty of headroom. The fix is not a bigger buffer or a smaller one but active queue management, which drops or marks packets early and deliberately, keeping the queue short while still signalling. If you see multi-hundred-millisecond latency that appears only under load, this is almost always the cause.

1.4Bandwidth-delay product

Why a fast link to a distant place can be slow 
BDP = bandwidth × round-trip time
    = how many bits are "in flight" on the wire at once

  1 Gbps × 80 ms  =  80,000,000 bits  =  10 MB in flight

A sender can only have unacknowledged data up to its window.
If the window is smaller than the BDP, the sender STOPS and
waits for an acknowledgement — so the link sits idle and the
achievable throughput is:

    throughput = window / RTT

  64 KB window, 80 ms RTT  →  6.6 Mbps
  ...on a 1 Gbps link. The other 993 Mbps is unreachable
  regardless of what you pay for.

THIS IS THE "LONG FAT NETWORK" PROBLEM
  It is why a single TCP stream across an ocean underperforms
  so badly, why bulk transfer tools open many connections in
  parallel, and why window scaling — which lifts the 64 KB
  ceiling — is one of the more consequential options in the
  protocol.                                            ch 9

  And it is why moving data closer beats buying capacity:
  halving the RTT doubles the throughput of every window-
  limited flow, for free.

A user complains their connection is slow. You upgrade them from 100 Mbps to 1 Gbps and nothing improves. Why?

Because you increased bandwidth and their problem was latency, and the two are different resources. Bandwidth upgrades reduce exactly one component of delay — serialisation, the time to clock bits onto the wire — and at 100 Mbps that was already only 0.12 ms for a full-size packet, so removing nine-tenths of it saves about a tenth of a millisecond per packet. If the application is waiting on round trips to a distant server, the dominant term is propagation, which is distance divided by the speed of light in fibre and is completely unaffected by how wide the link is. A 1 Gbps circuit to Sydney has precisely the same 238 ms round trip as a 10 Mbps one. Three diagnoses to run before spending money. Decompose the delay: ping the default gateway, then a local server, then the actual destination — the first tells you about the local segment, the difference between the second and third is the wide-area component, and if that dominates, no local upgrade helps. Check utilisation on the path, because queueing delay explodes non-linearly past about 80% and a congested link three hops away will not appear in the user's own statistics. And count the round trips the application makes, since an application performing two hundred sequential requests at 80 ms each takes sixteen seconds no matter what the link does — that is an application architecture problem wearing a network costume. The productive fixes for a latency problem are a closer server, fewer round trips, caching, or a protocol with fewer handshakes; bandwidth is the fix for a throughput problem, which is a different complaint.

Why does a large upload make an unrelated video call unusable, even with spare capacity?

Bufferbloat. The upload is a TCP flow, and TCP finds the available capacity by increasing its sending rate until it sees loss — loss is the feedback signal that tells it to back off. When the bottleneck device has a very deep buffer, it does not drop anything; it queues. So TCP keeps accelerating, the queue keeps growing, and it can reach hundreds of milliseconds or even seconds deep before any packet is finally discarded. Every other flow crossing that same interface now sits behind that queue, and a video call — which needs consistent delay under about 150 ms and cares far more about jitter than about throughput — becomes unusable. The counterintuitive part is that the link is not out of capacity in the sense people mean: it is passing data at full rate. What is exhausted is the tolerable delay, and it was exhausted by a well-behaved flow doing exactly what the protocol tells it to. Note also that the direction matters and is often misdiagnosed: an upload congests the upstream queue, which on an asymmetric residential or branch link is much smaller than the downstream one, so a modest upload can ruin a connection whose download figure looks healthy. The fixes are all about the queue rather than the capacity. Active queue management — CoDel, FQ-CoDel, PIE — drops or marks packets early so TCP gets its signal while the queue stays short. Fair queueing gives each flow its own queue so a bulk transfer cannot monopolise the buffer. And shaping slightly below the link rate moves the queue from the provider's uncontrolled buffer into your own managed one, which is the single most effective thing you can do on a link whose far end you do not control.

Why are MAC addresses rewritten at every hop but IP addresses are not?

Because they answer different questions, and the distinction is the foundation of everything in Parts 1 and 2. An IP address identifies an endpoint in the global address space, and the whole point of routing is to deliver a packet to that endpoint no matter how many networks lie between — so it must survive the journey unchanged, or the destination could not be found and the reply could not be addressed. A MAC address identifies an interface on one physical segment, and it is meaningful only within that segment: it has no structure, no hierarchy and no way to be routed, which is precisely why we have IP addresses at all. So when a router forwards a packet it discards the incoming Ethernet frame entirely, consults its routing table to choose the next hop, resolves that neighbour's MAC address with ARP, and builds a brand-new frame addressed to it. The IP header travels through unchanged apart from the TTL being decremented and the checksum recomputed. Two practical consequences worth holding onto. When you capture traffic, the source MAC is always the previous hop, not the original sender — so a capture showing a frame from your router's MAC with a source IP from another continent is entirely normal, and confusing the two produces some memorably wrong conclusions. And it explains why a device with a correct IP configuration but a broken ARP entry cannot communicate at all despite everything looking right at layer 3: the packet is perfectly addressed and there is no frame to carry it. The NAT caveat from §1.1 is the one exception on the IP side, and Chapter 12 covers what that breaks.

Recap

  1. Layer 2 is local, layer 3 is end to end. MACs are rewritten at every hop; IP addresses are not.
  2. The host's first decision is a bitwise AND against its subnet mask: same segment, or default gateway.
  3. Four delays: propagation, serialisation, queueing, processing. They behave completely differently.
  4. Propagation is distance and physics — about 5 µs per kilometre of fibre, and no purchase changes it.
  5. Bandwidth and latency are different resources. Widening a motorway does not shorten it.
  6. Queueing delay explodes past roughly 80% utilisation, which is why capacity planning targets sit where they do.
  7. Deep buffers make things worse by suppressing the loss signal TCP depends on.
  8. Shape slightly below the link rate to move the queue somewhere you control.
  9. Throughput equals window over round-trip time when the window is smaller than the bandwidth-delay product.
  10. Halving the RTT doubles a window-limited flow, which is why moving data closer beats buying capacity.
