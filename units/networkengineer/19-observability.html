Seeing the network

Four sources of truth, each answering a different question and none answering all of them. Knowing which one to reach for is most of the skill, and knowing what your current one is hiding is the rest.

19.2What a polling interval hides

Averages and microbursts

A comfortable-looking graph and a saturated link are entirely compatible.

The drop counter is more honest than the utilisation graph

Utilisation is an average over the polling window, and averaging destroys exactly the information you need. A 1 Gbps link reporting 30% over five minutes may have been completely saturated for ninety seconds and idle for the rest — same average, entirely different experience for anything crossing it during those ninety seconds. Faster polling helps and cannot solve it: a burst shorter than the sampling interval is invisible at any interval you can afford, and genuine microbursts last milliseconds. What does not lie is the output drop and buffer counter, which increments the moment a queue overflows regardless of what the average was. So the rule is: when someone reports drops on an underutilised link, believe the counter rather than the graph, and stop looking for the missing traffic. The fixes are the ones from Chapter 15 — shaping, queue management, or more capacity — because a burst that exceeds the link rate is a burst regardless of the average around it.

19.3Flow records

The ledger of conversations 
A flow record summarises one conversation: source and
destination address, ports, protocol, interface, byte and
packet counts, start and end times, and often the marking
and the autonomous system.

  → the single most useful data source for "who is doing
    this", and it is generated by equipment you already own.

SAMPLING
  Full flow accounting on a busy link is expensive, so most
  deployments sample — 1 in 1,000 packets is common.

  WHAT SAMPLING PRESERVES: volume estimates for large flows,
  which is what capacity planning and top-talker reporting
  need. A heavy hitter is statistically certain to appear.

  WHAT IT DESTROYS: small flows, and any claim about a
  specific conversation. At 1 in 1,000, a ten-packet
  connection has about a 1% chance of appearing at all —
  so "this host never contacted that one" is a statement
  sampled flow data cannot support, which matters when an
  investigation depends on it.       → Cyber Security ch 15

ACTIVE AND INACTIVE TIMEOUTS
  A long-running flow is exported in chunks — typically
  every 60 seconds — so one download appears as many
  records. Anything counting records rather than bytes gets
  this wrong.

WHERE TO COLLECT
  Ingress on every edge and boundary interface, not on every
  interface everywhere. Collecting the same traffic at four
  hops multiplies the storage and tells you the same thing
  four times, and deduplication is harder than not
  collecting it.

19.5What to alert on

Alerts that earn their place 
ALERT ON SYMPTOMS THE USER WOULD NOTICE
  Synthetic reachability from the places users are. A probe
  that fails is a real problem; an interface at 85% might
  not be.

ALERT ON STATE THAT SHOULD NEVER CHANGE
  · a redundancy pair reporting degraded            → ch 18
  · a routing adjacency down
  · a spanning tree topology change on a stable network
  · an interface flapping
  · a configuration diff nobody expected            → ch 21
  These are cheap, specific, and almost never false.

ALERT ON COUNTERS THAT SHOULD BE ZERO
  CRC errors, late collisions, input drops. Nonzero and
  rising is a fault; the absolute number is meaningless
  without clearing them first.                       → ch 3

  It generates noise, it fires during backups every night,
  and people learn to ignore it. Alert on drops, which
  is the thing utilisation is a proxy for.

WHAT MOST NETWORKS ARE MISSING
  · degraded redundancy — a pair that quietly lost a member
  · certificate and licence expiry                   → ch 11
  · DHCP pool exhaustion approaching
  · the backup path, tested                          → ch 14
  Each of these fails silently and is discovered at the
  worst possible moment.
Drops are reported on a link showing 30% utilisation. Where has the traffic gone?

Nowhere — the traffic and the drops are both real, and the utilisation figure is an average that conceals them. Polling produces a mean over the sampling window, and a mean is exactly the wrong statistic for a queue, because a queue overflows on instantaneous rate rather than on average rate. A 1 Gbps link reporting 30% over five minutes is consistent with total saturation for ninety seconds and idle for the remaining two hundred and ten: same average, and everything crossing during those ninety seconds meets a full buffer. Shorter polling intervals narrow the window and cannot close it, because genuine microbursts last milliseconds and no affordable polling interval sees them — a one-second sample still averages away a fifty-millisecond burst that filled the queue. So the resolution is to stop trying to find the traffic in the utilisation graph and believe the counter instead: output drops and buffer overruns increment at the moment of overflow, regardless of what the surrounding average was, which makes them the honest signal here. What to do about it splits three ways. If the bursts come from a small number of sources, shape them so the burst is smoothed before it reaches the bottleneck. If the link is genuinely a chokepoint for bursty traffic, active queue management handles it far better than a bigger buffer, which just converts drops into latency — the bufferbloat argument from Chapter 1. And if the bursts are large and frequent, the honest answer is more capacity, sized for the burst rather than for the average. Worth noting the diagnostic habit this implies: when a graph and a counter disagree, the counter is measuring an event and the graph is measuring an average, and events are what users experience.

Why can sampled flow data not prove that two hosts never communicated?

Because sampling records a fraction of packets and infers the rest, which supports statements about volume and does not support statements about absence. At a common rate of one packet in a thousand, a conversation of ten packets — a DNS query and response, a short probe, a handful of beacons — has roughly a one per cent chance of contributing a single sampled packet, so its absence from the data is entirely expected whether or not it happened. The property sampling preserves is the opposite one: a flow large enough to matter for capacity is statistically certain to appear, and its byte count can be estimated accurately by scaling. That is why sampled flow records are excellent for top-talker reporting, capacity planning and understanding traffic mix, and unreliable for anything investigative. The distinction matters most in security work, where the question is frequently "did this host ever contact that address", and a confident "no" from sampled data is unfounded — the honest answer is that no evidence of it was captured, which is a different claim. Three practical consequences. Know your sampling rate before drawing conclusions, because it varies by platform and is often left at a vendor default nobody chose. Use unsampled collection at boundaries where completeness genuinely matters — the internet edge, sensitive segments — and accept the cost there rather than everywhere. And remember the active timeout issue when reading records at all: a long transfer is exported in chunks, typically every sixty seconds, so one download appears as many records and anything counting records rather than bytes will be wrong.

What should you alert on, and what should you not?

Alert on things a user would notice and on state that should never change; do not alert on utilisation. The reasoning is about signal quality rather than importance. Synthetic reachability probes from the places users actually are catch the failures that matter and produce almost no false positives, because a probe either completed or did not — and crucially they catch failures no device-side metric would reveal, like a routing problem three networks away. State that should never change makes excellent alerts for the same reason: a redundancy pair reporting degraded, a routing adjacency dropping, a spanning tree topology change on a stable network, an interface flapping, or an unexpected configuration diff are each specific, rare, and nearly always worth someone's attention. Counters that should be zero — CRC errors, late collisions, input drops — belong in the same category, with the caveat that the absolute figure is meaningless unless you cleared them first, so alert on the rate of increase. What not to alert on is utilisation, and the reason is that it trains people to ignore alerts. It fires every night during backups, it fires on links that are perfectly healthy at 85%, and it does not fire on the bursty link dropping packets at 30%. Alert on drops instead, which is the outcome utilisation is a proxy for and which is real when it happens. Finally, the alerts most networks are missing are all silent failures: degraded redundancy, approaching certificate and licence expiry, DHCP pools filling, and backup paths that have never been exercised. Each of those is discovered at the worst possible moment precisely because nothing was watching for it.

Recap

  1. Counters say how much, flows say who, capture says exactly what, and synthetics say whether it works.
  2. A utilisation average conceals bursts — 30% over five minutes can be full for ninety seconds.
  3. The drop counter is honest; the utilisation graph is an average.
  4. Microbursts last milliseconds and no affordable polling interval sees them.
  5. Sampled flow data supports volume claims, not absence claims.
  6. A long flow is exported in chunks, so count bytes rather than records.
  7. Collect flows at boundaries, not at every hop — the same traffic four times is not four times the insight.
  8. A mirror port can be oversubscribed and drop silently, which looks like network loss.
  9. Capture at both ends, with synchronised clocks, filtering at capture rather than display.
  10. Do not alert on utilisation. Alert on drops, on degraded redundancy, and on things that should never change.
