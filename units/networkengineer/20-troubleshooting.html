Troubleshooting

The difference between an engineer who fixes things in ten minutes and one who takes three hours is almost never knowledge. It is method: halving the search space with every test instead of checking things in the order they occur to you.

20.1Before touching anything

The four questions that resolve a large fraction of faults 
1  WHAT CHANGED?
   A network that worked yesterday has had something
   altered — a configuration, a certificate, a lease, a

   log finds this faster than any packet capture, and it is

   Ask it even when everyone says nothing changed.

2  WHO EXACTLY IS AFFECTED?
   One user, one subnet, one site, or everyone? The scope
   IS the diagnosis, most of the time — a fault affecting
   one VLAN and not another has already told you where to
   look, and one affecting everyone points at something
   shared.

3  WHAT IS THE ACTUAL SYMPTOM?
   "It's slow" and "it fails" are different faults with no
   overlap in their causes. Get a specific, reproducible
   observation: which operation, from where, to what, with
   what error, at what time.

4  DID IT EVER WORK?
   A thing that has never worked is a configuration
   problem. A thing that stopped working is a change or a
   failure. These lead in opposite directions and people
   routinely spend an hour on the wrong one.

THE ANSWERS SET THE SEARCH SPACE. Only then start testing.
Scope is diagnosis

Before any tool comes out, establish precisely who is affected — and just as importantly, who is not. A fault hitting one user and not their neighbour on the same switch is a host or a port; hitting one VLAN and not another on the same switch is layer 3 or policy; hitting one site is the wide area; hitting everyone at once is something shared — DNS, authentication, a core device, or time. Each of those conclusions eliminates the majority of the possible causes in one question, before you have logged into anything. The corollary is that the working case is as informative as the broken one. When something works from your laptop and not from a server, the difference between those two paths is the entire investigation, and enumerating that difference is faster than debugging either one in isolation.

Traceroute is the most misread output in networking

Four independent reasons a traceroute looks alarming and is fine. Middle hops showing high latency or asterisks are usually routers deprioritising the ICMP responses they must generate — that is control plane work, deliberately rate-limited, and it says nothing about how they forward transit traffic. What matters is the final hop; a middle hop at 300 ms with the destination at 40 ms is entirely normal. The return path is invisible: each hop's response takes its own route back, so an asymmetric or broken return path shows up as a latency spike attributed to the wrong device. Equal-cost paths mean successive probes take different routes, so the output can describe a topology no single packet ever traversed — Chapter 6. And latency that increases then decreases down the list is not a fault; it is just per-hop response variability. Use mtr instead where you can, since it sends many probes and shows loss and jitter per hop over time, which distinguishes rate-limited responses from genuine loss — the tell being that real loss persists at every hop after the affected one.

Why is bisection better than walking the path, and when is it not?

Because each test eliminates half the remaining candidates rather than one, so the number of tests grows with the logarithm of the path length rather than linearly. A sixteen-hop path is bounded at four tests by bisection and takes eight on average walking it from your end; at thirty-two hops it is five against sixteen. The bisection figure is a worst case rather than a fixed cost — a fault that happens to land on an early midpoint is found sooner — and the value is precisely that it is bounded. The saving compounds when tests are expensive, which they usually are — reaching a device, arranging access, coordinating with another team, or waiting for an intermittent fault to recur can make a single test cost far more than the minutes of typing. The mechanics are simple: test the midpoint, and whichever half contains the transition from working to broken becomes the new search space. It requires one property to work — the fault must be monotonic along the path, meaning everything before the bad hop works and everything after it does not. That holds for most connectivity faults and it is worth checking rather than assuming. Where bisection is not the right approach: when a strong prior exists, such as a change made to one specific device an hour before the fault appeared, in which case testing that device first is one test rather than four and you should take it. When the fault is not monotonic — intermittent, load-dependent, or affecting only some flows — bisection's assumption breaks and you need to characterise the pattern first. And when the path is short, three or four hops, where the difference between methods is a single test and the overhead of thinking about it is not worth it. The habit worth building is to start from scope rather than from the path at all: §20.1's questions frequently identify the segment before any test is run.

A traceroute shows 300 ms and asterisks at hop 7. Is that your problem?

Almost certainly not, and this is the most commonly misread output in networking. Intermediate hops respond to traceroute by generating ICMP messages, which is control plane work — handled by the device's CPU, deliberately rate-limited, and given far lower priority than forwarding transit traffic. So a router can be forwarding at line rate with no loss whatsoever while responding to traceroute slowly or not at all, and asterisks frequently mean "this device does not prioritise answering you" rather than "this device is dropping your traffic". The figure that matters is the final hop: if the destination responds in 40 ms, then packets are crossing hop 7 in a few milliseconds regardless of what hop 7 said about itself. Three further reasons the output misleads. The return path is invisible — each hop's reply takes its own route back, so a slow or broken return path appears as a spike attributed to the wrong device. Equal-cost paths mean successive probes may take different routes, producing a listing that no single packet ever experienced. And latency that rises and then falls down the list is normal per-hop variability, not a fault that healed. The genuine signal to look for is loss that persists at every hop after a given point, because that indicates traffic is actually being dropped rather than responses being deprioritised — and mtr is the better tool because it sends continuous probes and shows per-hop loss and jitter over time, which distinguishes the two cases immediately. If the final hop is fine and users report a problem, the fault is not on this path and the investigation should go back to scope.

What is the single highest-yield question in troubleshooting?

"What changed?" — and it is worth asking persistently, because the first answer is almost always "nothing". Networks are deterministic systems; something that worked yesterday and does not today has had something altered, and the exceptions are hardware failures and capacity crossing a threshold, both of which are less common than change. The reason the first answer is unreliable is not dishonesty but scope: the person you asked knows about their changes. A certificate expired, a DHCP lease pool filled, a licence lapsed, a scheduled job ran for the first time this month, another team pushed a firewall rule, a provider performed maintenance, or a software update installed overnight — none of those register as "a change" to the person reporting the fault, and several are invisible unless you go looking. So the productive version is specific rather than general: check the configuration diffs for the devices in scope, which is why version-controlled configuration from Chapter 21 pays for itself here; check the change record across every team, not just yours; check what expires, since certificates, licences and registrations fail on a schedule rather than in response to anything; and check the provider's status, because their maintenance window is a change you were not told about. Two supporting habits. Correlate the fault's start time against everything that happened around it — the timestamp is usually the strongest evidence available and it is why the clock synchronisation in Chapter 11 matters. And when the answer genuinely is that nothing changed, that is itself informative: it points at a threshold being crossed, an intermittent hardware fault, or something with a timer that has now expired.

Recap

  1. Ask what changed, who is affected, what the symptom is, and whether it ever worked — before any tool.
  2. Scope is diagnosis. Who is not affected narrows it as much as who is.
  3. Bisection is logarithmic; walking the path is linear. Sixteen hops is bounded at four tests against eight on average.
  4. Bisection needs the fault to be monotonic along the path. Check that rather than assuming it.
  5. Take a strong prior over a method — one test beats four when you have real evidence.
  6. Traceroute's middle hops are control plane responses, not forwarding behaviour. Read the final hop.
  7. Persistent loss at every hop after a point is the real signal; use mtr to see it.
  8. Filter at capture, follow one conversation, and read the gaps rather than the packets.
  9. A zero window is the receiver, not the network.
  10. Change one thing, write down what you tried, and revert what did not help.
