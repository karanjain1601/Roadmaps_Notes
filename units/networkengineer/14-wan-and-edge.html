The wide area

Inside a building you own the cable. Between buildings you rent a service from someone whose network you cannot see, cannot troubleshoot and cannot fix — so the engineering shifts from configuration to procurement, contract terms, and designing for a failure you will not be able to diagnose.

Read the service level agreement, and specifically what it pays

The headline availability figure is the least interesting part. What matters is what the agreement measures, how a fault is counted, and what a breach is worth. Availability is usually measured monthly, so 99.9% permits about forty-three minutes of downtime a month with no penalty at all. The clock frequently starts when you raise a ticket rather than when the fault began, so detection delay is your problem. Planned maintenance is normally excluded entirely, and providers use that generously. And the remedy is almost always a service credit proportional to the monthly fee — so an outage that costs your business a hundred thousand pounds is compensated with a few hundred pounds off the bill. The practical conclusion is not to negotiate harder but to design as though the circuit will fail, because the contract confirms that the provider's exposure is trivial compared to yours.

14.3Diversity, which is not the same as redundancy

Two circuits, and whether they actually help

Redundancy that shares a failure mode is expensive decoration.

Two providers is not two paths

The most expensive way to discover this is a digger. Two contracts with two companies frequently resolve to one physical route: the second provider resells the first one's last mile, both fibres run through the same duct into the building, both terminate in the same street cabinet, or both hand off to the same regional aggregation point ten miles away. Every one of those is a single failure that takes out a redundant pair. You cannot detect it from the configuration, and providers are often reluctant to disclose it because the wholesale arrangement is commercially sensitive. What to do: ask explicitly for a diverse route and get it in writing, request the circuit identifiers and check whether they share infrastructure, insist on separate physical entries to the building even though the civil work is expensive, and where genuine diversity is unavailable choose a different medium — a mobile or fixed-wireless backup shares no duct with anything.

14.4SD-WAN

What it actually is, under the marketing 
An overlay: encrypted tunnels between sites across whatever

WHAT IT GENUINELY CHANGES
  · TRANSPORT INDEPENDENCE. A site can use broadband, mobile
    and MPLS at once, with the overlay hiding the difference.
    That is what lets you replace expensive circuits with
    cheap ones without redesigning routing.
  · PER-APPLICATION STEERING. Voice over the low-jitter
    path, backups over the cheap one, and it can move a
    flow mid-session when a path degrades.
  · MEASURED PATH QUALITY. It probes loss, latency and
    jitter continuously and reacts in seconds — much faster
    than a routing protocol, and it detects the case where
    a link is up and performing badly, which routing does
    not.                                             → ch 7
  · CENTRAL CONFIGURATION. New sites come up from a
    template rather than from a runbook.

WHAT IT DOES NOT CHANGE
  · Physics. It cannot make an internet path have MPLS
    latency; it can only choose the better of what exists.
  · Capacity. Two bad links do not make a good one.
  · Your dependence on a provider — you have added one,
    since the controller and its cloud gateways are now on
    the critical path.

THE HONEST TRADE
  You are exchanging a provider-managed private network for
  a vendor-managed overlay across public transport. That is
  frequently cheaper and more flexible, and it moves the
  troubleshooting burden onto you: when a path is bad you
  own the diagnosis, and "the internet is congested
  somewhere" is a much harder conversation than a circuit
  fault ticket.

Your two circuits from different providers both failed at once. How?

Almost certainly a shared physical path that neither contract disclosed. There are four common ways two logically separate circuits turn out to be one, and all of them are invisible from your configuration. The second provider may resell the first one's last mile — wholesale arrangements are widespread, and the reseller has no obligation to tell you whose fibre is in the ground. Both fibres may enter the building through the same duct, so one contractor with a digger severs both; this is the classic case and it is why separate physical entry points matter even though the civil work is expensive. Both may terminate in the same street cabinet or exchange, which fails as a unit. Or both may hand off to the same regional aggregation point some distance away, so a fault miles from your site takes out both. Two further possibilities worth eliminating: a shared power feed to the comms room, and a single router with two WAN ports, which makes the router itself the single point of failure regardless of how diverse the circuits are. What to do about it. Ask for diverse routing in writing and request the circuit identifiers so you can check whether they share infrastructure — some providers will confirm diversity formally, and the ones that will not are telling you something. Insist on separate building entries. And where genuine diversity cannot be obtained or verified, choose a different medium entirely: a mobile or fixed-wireless backup shares no duct, no cabinet and no exchange with a fibre circuit, which makes it structurally diverse even though its performance is worse.

Should you replace MPLS with SD-WAN over internet circuits?

For most organisations, largely yes, and it is worth being precise about what you gain and lose rather than treating it as an obvious upgrade. The case for is strong: internet bandwidth costs a fraction of MPLS per megabit, so you can buy several times the capacity for less money; provisioning takes days rather than months; a site can use two or three cheap diverse transports instead of one expensive one, which frequently improves availability; and per-application steering with continuous path measurement reacts to degradation in seconds, catching the "link is up and performing badly" case that routing protocols miss entirely. Central templated configuration is a genuine operational improvement too. What you give up is the part that deserves care. MPLS provides contractual latency and jitter guarantees with class-of-service honoured end to end, and the internet provides none — for most traffic that does not matter, because measured internet paths are usually fine and SD-WAN can steer away from a bad one, but "usually fine with no recourse" is a different risk profile from "guaranteed with a penalty clause". Three cases where MPLS still earns its price: real-time traffic where jitter must be bounded rather than merely usually acceptable, regulated environments where a private network is a compliance requirement, and paths in regions where internet quality is genuinely poor and there is no better transport to steer toward. Two honest costs of the move: you have swapped a provider dependency for a vendor dependency on the SD-WAN controller and its gateways, which are now on your critical path; and the troubleshooting burden shifts to you, since diagnosing congestion somewhere in the middle of the internet is far harder than raising a circuit fault. A hybrid — MPLS for the traffic that needs it, internet for the rest — is a common and defensible landing point.

Why is a 99.9% availability SLA less reassuring than it sounds?

Because of how it is measured, what is excluded, and what a breach actually pays. The arithmetic first: 99.9% measured monthly permits about forty-three minutes of downtime every month with no breach at all, and a provider can be at exactly that figure indefinitely while being entirely within contract. Then the exclusions, which do more work than the number. Planned maintenance is normally excluded, and providers schedule generously against that clause. The clock frequently starts when you raise a ticket rather than when the fault began, so any delay in your own detection is deducted from the provider's account of the outage — which is a direct argument for reachability monitoring that alerts in seconds. Faults attributed to your equipment, your power, or the building are excluded. And in many contracts an outage must exceed a minimum duration before it counts at all, so repeated short failures may never register. Finally the remedy: it is almost always a service credit proportional to the monthly fee, so a full day of downtime might return a few percent of a bill that is small relative to what the outage cost you. No provider offers consequential loss, and none would price it if they did. The right conclusion is not to negotiate a better number — the marginal cost of 99.99% is high and the exclusions still apply — but to read the agreement as a statement of the provider's exposure, which is trivial, and design accordingly: diverse paths, fast independent detection, and a tested failover. The SLA is a procurement document, not an availability strategy.
