Addressing and address plans

Subnetting is arithmetic and can be learned in an hour. Designing an address plan is engineering, it takes judgement, and getting it wrong commits you to a larger routing table and a harder migration for as long as the network exists.

~30 min read CIDR · VLSM · summarisation · IPv6 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapters 1–3

4.1The arithmetic, briefly

THE ONE OPERATION THAT MATTERS
  A host decides "local or gateway?" with a bitwise AND:

    my address    10.20.30.40   00001010...00011110 00101000
    my mask       /24           11111111...11111111 00000000
    AND           10.20.30.0    ← my network

    destination   10.20.31.7
    AND same mask 10.20.31.0    ← different → send to gateway

  Every routing decision anywhere is a variation of this.

THE RANGES TO RECOGNISE ON SIGHT
  10.0.0.0/8        private, 16.7 M addresses
  172.16.0.0/12     private, 1 M          — the forgotten one
  192.168.0.0/16    private, 65 k
  100.64.0.0/10     carrier-grade NAT      → ch 12
  169.254.0.0/16    link-local — means DHCP failed
  127.0.0.0/8       loopback
  224.0.0.0/4       multicast
/24 everywhere is the most common planning mistake

It is comfortable because the boundary falls on a dot and the arithmetic is easy in your head, and it is wrong in both directions. A point-to-point link between two routers needs two usable addresses and gets 254 — waste that does not matter on its own and does matter when you have four hundred of them and a routing table to match. A user VLAN that grows past 254 hosts needs renumbering, which is the expensive operation this chapter exists to avoid. Size the subnet to the need plus growth, then round up to the next power of two, and use /30 or /31 for router-to-router links. The habit worth building is asking "how many hosts, in three years?" before typing a mask.

4.2Building a plan

VLSM allocation

Variable-length masks let each subnet be the size it needs. Allocate largest first, or you fragment the space.

The allocation procedure, and why order matters 
1  LIST every subnet and its host count, including growth.
2  SORT largest first.
3  ALLOCATE from the top of the block, rounding each up to a
   power of two and aligning it to its own size.
4  RESERVE the remainder rather than filling it — you will
   need it, and a plan with no spare space is a plan with a
   renumbering scheduled in it.

WHY LARGEST FIRST
  A /22 must start on a /22 boundary — an address divisible
  by 1024. Allocate a /28 first and the next /22 cannot begin
  until the following boundary, stranding everything between.
  Descending order makes each block land immediately after
  the last, with no gaps.

RESERVE PREDICTABLE RANGES
  Pick a convention and hold it everywhere:
    x.x.x.1     the gateway, always
    x.x.0.0/24  infrastructure and management
    top of range  static assignments, DHCP below
  A consistent convention means anyone can guess an address
  correctly, which is worth more than the addresses it costs.

AND LEAVE ROOM AT THE HIERARCHY LEVEL ABOVE
  Site 1 gets 10.1.0.0/16, site 2 gets 10.2.0.0/16 — not
  10.1.0.0/16 and 10.1.1.0/24. The gap between sites is what
  lets a site grow without breaking summarisation.

4.3Summarisation is the point

The reason to plan hierarchically is not tidiness. It is that a hierarchy can be described to the rest of the network in one line instead of hundreds, and that difference compounds at every level.

What the routing table looks like

The same networks, allocated two ways. Only one of them summarises.

Summarisation hides failures, and that is mostly good

When a site advertises one summary route instead of twelve specifics, a single subnet going down inside that site does not withdraw the summary — so the rest of the network keeps forwarding traffic toward it, and the site's own router drops it. That sounds like a defect and is usually the desired behaviour: it means a flapping subnet in Manchester does not cause routing recalculation in every other site, which is the whole point of containing instability. The cost is that reachability is now less precise, so you can black-hole traffic for a dead subnet rather than failing fast. Two mitigations: configure the summarising router to drop summarised traffic it cannot deliver into a null route, so it fails cleanly rather than looping, and be aware that if you summarise, your monitoring must watch the specifics — the routing protocol has stopped telling you about them by design.

Why allocate the largest subnet first?

Because every subnet must begin on an address boundary divisible by its own size, and allocating small blocks first strands the space between them. A /22 contains 1,024 addresses and can only start at an address that is a multiple of 1,024 — so if you have already placed a /28 at the start of your range, the next available /22 boundary is 1,024 addresses further on, and everything between the end of the /28 and that boundary is unusable for a /22. Do that a few times with mixed sizes and a block that mathematically holds all your subnets will not actually fit them, which is a genuinely confusing outcome when the totals say it should. Descending order avoids it entirely: each block is at least as large as the one after it, so the next allocation always lands immediately after the previous one with no gap, and the space packs perfectly. There are two related habits worth adopting at the same time. Round up to a power of two and add growth before you sort, because a subnet sized to today's host count is a renumbering scheduled for later, and renumbering is the operation this whole chapter exists to avoid. And reserve the remainder rather than distributing it — a plan that allocates every address is one where the next new VLAN has to go somewhere arbitrary, which is exactly how a summarisable hierarchy stops summarising. If the arithmetic gets tedious, note that it is entirely mechanical and worth scripting; the judgement is in the host counts and the growth assumption, not in the packing.

Why does an address plan determine your routing table size?

Because a router can only advertise a single summary route for a group of subnets if those subnets are contiguous and aligned — that is, if they collectively form one clean prefix. That property is decided entirely when addresses are handed out, and it cannot be recovered afterwards without renumbering. If site 3 holds 10.3.0.0/16 as one block, its router tells the rest of the network one thing: "10.3.0.0/16 is this way", regardless of whether the site contains twelve subnets or two hundred. If the same site was given 10.1.4.0/24, 10.7.9.0/24 and 10.12.30.0/24 as they were requested over the years, no summary exists — the numbers have no common prefix — and every one of those specifics must be carried by every router in the network. The effect compounds through the hierarchy: with contiguous per-site blocks grouped by region, a region can summarise its sites, and the core carries one route per region rather than one per subnet, so the table stops growing with the number of subnets and starts growing with the number of regions. The consequences of getting it wrong are not just memory. A larger table means slower convergence after a failure, more update traffic, more CPU on every recalculation, and instability in one corner propagating everywhere because there is no summary boundary to contain it. And the fix is renumbering, which touches DHCP scopes, firewall rules, access lists, monitoring, DNS and documentation — which is why the plan is worth an afternoon at the start and is nearly impossible to change at the end.

What is the biggest practical change when you deploy IPv6?

That address scarcity stops driving design, and a whole category of decisions and workarounds disappears with it. Every LAN is a /64 — not because it needs eighteen quintillion addresses but because SLAAC requires 64 interface bits — so subnet sizing ceases to be a question, VLSM arithmetic largely disappears, and a typical /48 site allocation gives 65,536 subnets, which means you can allocate them by meaning rather than by size. NAT becomes unnecessary, so end-to-end addressing is restored and the protocols that struggle with address translation stop struggling. The operational surprises are worth knowing in advance, though, because they are what actually consumes time. An interface has several addresses simultaneously — a link-local one always, a global one, often a temporary privacy address that rotates — so logs and access lists have to cope with a host that legitimately appears under different addresses over a week. Link-local addresses matter far more than in IPv4: neighbour discovery and routing protocol adjacencies run over them, they need a zone identifier to be usable, and they exist whether you configured anything or not. ICMPv6 cannot be filtered wholesale, because neighbour discovery, router advertisements and path MTU discovery all depend on it — blocking it does not harden the network, it breaks it. And the failure mode people meet first is a broken IPv6 path presenting as general slowness, because hosts prefer IPv6, attempt it, and fall back to IPv4 only after a timeout; the fix is either to make IPv6 work properly or to remove it, and half-working is the worst of the three states.

Recap

  1. The host's local-or-gateway decision is a bitwise AND against the mask. Everything else is a variation.
  2. /24 everywhere wastes point-to-point links and strands growing VLANs.
  3. Size to need plus growth, then round up to a power of two.
  4. Allocate largest first, or alignment boundaries strand the space between blocks.
  5. Reserve the remainder. A plan with no spare space has a renumbering built into it.
  6. Contiguous and aligned is what makes a summary possible, and it is decided at allocation time.
  7. Summarisation is why the table grows with regions rather than subnets.
  8. A summary hides a dead specific — usually desirable, and your monitoring must cover the gap.
  9. In IPv6 every LAN is a /64, and sizing stops being a design question.
  10. Never filter ICMPv6 wholesale. Neighbour discovery and path MTU depend on it.
