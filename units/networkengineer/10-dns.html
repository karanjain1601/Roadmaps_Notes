DNS

It is always DNS, according to the joke, and the reason the joke persists is that DNS sits in front of everything, fails in ways that look like other problems, and is operated by people who mostly hope not to think about it.

10.1How a name becomes an address

A resolution, step by step

Each cache hit removes a round trip. On a warm cache almost nothing happens at all.

The two kinds of server, which people conflate constantly 
RECURSIVE RESOLVER          "find this out for me"
  What a client is configured with. It does the work of
  walking the hierarchy, caches the results, and returns a
  final answer. Your internal resolver, or a public one.
  Caching lives here, and so does most of the performance.

AUTHORITATIVE SERVER        "this is the answer, from me"
  Holds the actual zone data for a domain. Does NOT recurse
  and does not cache other people's records. It answers only
  for the zones it is authoritative for, and refers you
  elsewhere otherwise.

  A server doing BOTH is a misconfiguration in almost every
  case — an open recursive resolver is abused for
  amplification attacks within days of being exposed.

THE WALK, WHEN NOTHING IS CACHED
  resolver → root server        "who handles .com?"
  resolver → .com server        "who handles example.com?"
  resolver → example.com server "what is www.example.com?"

  Three queries, three round trips, and only the last one
  goes to the domain owner. The first two are cached for a
  long time, which is why a cold cache is rare in practice.

STUB RESOLVER
  The tiny piece of the operating system that sends the
  query to the configured resolver. It does not recurse. It
  is also where the local cache and the hosts file live —
  and where "it works from my laptop but not the server"
  frequently originates.

10.3TTL is the only lever, and it cuts both ways

Choosing a TTL

Low TTLs make changes fast and queries expensive. There is no setting that is good at both.

Lower the TTL before the change, not during it

The mistake is symmetrical and extremely common. If a record has a 24-hour TTL and you drop it to 60 seconds an hour before a migration, the resolvers that already cached the old value are still holding it for up to 24 hours — they will not learn about the new TTL until the old entry expires. So the low TTL does nothing for exactly the caches you were worried about. The correct sequence is to lower the TTL at least one full old-TTL period before the change, verify from several external resolvers that the short value is being served, make the change, and then raise the TTL again once things are stable. Two related points: some resolvers enforce their own minimum and will not honour a very low TTL, so treat a low TTL as a request rather than a guarantee; and DNS-based failover is inherently slow and imprecise for this reason, which is why anycast or a load balancer is a better answer where seconds matter.

10.4Anycast, and why DNS uses it everywhere

The same address, announced from many places 
Several servers in different locations all announce the same
prefix into BGP. Every client reaches "the nearest one" by
routing, without knowing there is more than one.

WHY DNS IS THE PERFECT USE CASE
  · Queries are short, stateless, and usually a single
    packet each way — so it does not matter if consecutive
    queries land on different instances.
  · Latency matters a great deal, because a resolution sits
    in front of every connection.
  · Availability matters more than anything: withdraw the
    route from a failed site and traffic moves within BGP
    convergence, with no client change at all.

  This is why the root servers are thirteen ADDRESSES and
  well over a thousand physical instances.

WHY IT SUITS TCP LESS WELL
  A routing change mid-connection sends packets to a
  different instance, which has no state for that connection
  and resets it. For short queries that is invisible; for a
  long-lived session it is an outage. Modern practice
  anycasts TCP anyway — with careful routing stability — but
  the failure mode is worth knowing.

WHAT IT DOES NOT DO
  Anycast picks the topologically nearest instance, which is
  not always the geographically or latency-nearest one. BGP
  chooses by AS path length and policy, not by milliseconds,
  so a badly-placed peering relationship can send a client
  a very long way to a "near" instance.               → ch 8

You lowered the TTL an hour before a migration and half your users still hit the old server. Why?

Because a resolver that already had the record cached is holding it under the old TTL, and it will not see your new shorter value until that old entry expires. If the record had a 24-hour TTL, some caches will keep serving the old address for up to 24 hours after your change, entirely regardless of what you set an hour ago. The new TTL only applies to resolvers that fetch the record fresh — which is precisely the population that was never the problem. The correct sequence is to lower the TTL at least one full old-TTL period before the change: drop it a day early if it was set to a day, wait for the old value to age out everywhere, confirm from several external resolvers that the short TTL is actually being served, then make the change, then raise the TTL again once you are confident. Three complications worth knowing. Some resolvers enforce a minimum TTL of their own and will not honour thirty seconds, so a low TTL is a request rather than a guarantee. Some clients and application runtimes cache resolved addresses for the lifetime of the process and ignore TTL entirely, which is a very common cause of "the DNS is right but that server keeps connecting to the old address". And browsers maintain their own caches with their own rules. The broader lesson is that DNS is a poor failover mechanism — it is eventually consistent with an unpredictable tail, so where seconds matter the answer is anycast, a load balancer, or an address that does not need to change.

Why is DNS blamed for so many outages, and how often is that fair?

It is blamed because it sits in front of everything and its failures are indistinguishable from failures of the thing behind it: when resolution fails, the application reports that it cannot reach the server, so every DNS problem presents as a problem with something else. And it is genuinely at fault more often than its simplicity suggests, for structural reasons rather than because the protocol is bad. Caching makes failures inconsistent — some users are fine, some are not, and which group you are in depends on which resolver you use and when it last fetched the record, so the problem is difficult to reproduce and easy to misattribute. Changes take effect on a schedule you do not control, so a mistake propagates gradually and a rollback propagates just as gradually. It is a shared dependency with no obvious owner, frequently configured years ago, occasionally by someone who has left, and the registrar and the zone data and the resolvers are often managed by three different teams. And several of its failure modes are time bombs: a DNSSEC signature expires at a specific moment, a domain registration lapses, a secondary stops transferring and serves stale data until its expiry timer fires — none of which correlate with any change anyone made, which makes them baffling. The habits that help are unglamorous: monitor resolution from outside your network as a synthetic check, alert on zone serial mismatch between primary and every secondary and on approaching DNSSEC and registration expiry, keep at least two independent resolvers on separate infrastructure, and treat zone data as code with review and version history rather than as something edited in a web console.

Why is anycast such a good fit for DNS and a poorer one for long-lived connections?

Because anycast's core property is that which instance you reach can change at any moment, and DNS does not care while a session does. A DNS query over UDP is a single packet out and a single packet back, with no state held anywhere between queries — so if routing shifts and the next query lands on an instance in a different country, nothing notices. That statelessness is what lets anycast deliver its three benefits cleanly: latency drops because clients reach a nearby instance without any configuration; capacity scales by adding sites rather than by making one bigger; and failover is a BGP withdrawal, so a dead site stops receiving traffic within convergence time with no client change at all. It is also inherently resistant to volumetric attack, because the traffic is absorbed by whichever instances are near the sources rather than concentrating at one location. A long-lived TCP connection has the opposite requirement. Its state lives on one specific server, so a mid-connection routing change delivers packets to an instance with no knowledge of that connection, which responds with a reset. On a stable network that is rare, and networks are not always stable — a peering change, a link failure or a route flap anywhere along the path can move a flow. In practice anycast TCP is widely deployed and works, with two mitigations: keeping routing deliberately stable so instances do not shift under active flows, and terminating connections close to the client so that state, if lost, is cheap to rebuild. Worth noting one limitation that applies to both cases: anycast selects the topologically nearest instance by AS path and policy, which is not always the lowest-latency one — a poor peering arrangement can route a client a very long way to a nominally "near" server.

Recap

  1. Recursive resolvers walk the hierarchy and cache; authoritative servers answer for their own zones. A server doing both is usually a mistake.
  2. Only the last query in a cold resolution reaches the domain owner. The rest is cached for a long time.
  3. A CNAME cannot coexist with other records, so it is illegal at a zone apex.
  4. The SOA minimum controls negative caching — why a newly added record fails to appear.
  5. Lower the TTL a full old-TTL period before the change, or it applies to nobody who mattered.
  6. A low TTL is a request. Resolvers enforce minimums and applications cache for their own lifetime.
  7. DNS is a poor failover mechanism — eventually consistent with an unpredictable tail.
  8. Anycast suits DNS because queries are stateless, and it withdraws a failed site via BGP.
  9. Anycast finds the topologically nearest instance, which is not always the fastest one.
  10. Monitor from outside, and alert on serial mismatch, DNSSEC expiry and registration renewal.
