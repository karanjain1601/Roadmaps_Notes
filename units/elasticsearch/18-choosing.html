Choosing Elasticsearch

It is very good at two things and gets adopted for a third that it handles badly. Plus the thing every chapter has assumed: which of the two projects you are running, and whether the licence that caused the split still matters to you.

Where it clearly wins

Obvious fits

  • Full-text search with ranking, typo tolerance and highlighting. The original purpose, and unmatched
  • Log and event analytics at volume — arguably the larger use case now
  • Faceted navigation — one request returns results and every facet count (chapter 12)
  • Aggregations over hundreds of millions of documents, fast
  • Observability — logs, metrics and traces in one place
  • Security analytics, where EQL's ordered sequences have no real alternative (chapter 11)
  • Hybrid retrieval for RAG — BM25 and kNN fused, with filters (chapter 13)
  • Geospatial queries and distance sorting

Frequently a mistake

  • A primary datastore — no cross-document transactions, no constraints, no read-your-writes (chapter 1)
  • Relational data — no joins, and every workaround has sharp edges (chapter 5)
  • Exact reporting where the numbers must reconcile (chapter 12)
  • Small datasets — Postgres full-text search will do, with far less to run
  • Frequently-updated documents — every update reindexes the whole thing (chapter 6)
  • A queue or a message bus — it is neither
  • Numbered pagination into the thousands (chapter 9)
  • When nobody will operate it, and it is not managed
The question that decides it

If this cluster were destroyed, could you rebuild it? If the answer is "yes, we reindex from the database over a few hours", Elasticsearch is a good choice and most of this unit's warnings are things to know rather than problems to solve — the mapping is changeable, because you can always rebuild, and no failure is unrecoverable. If the answer is "no, this is where the data lives", then every constraint in this unit becomes load-bearing: you cannot change a field's type, cannot express a constraint, cannot do a multi-document transaction, and cannot upgrade past two majors without a reindex you have no source for. That is a much worse position than it looks from month one, and the escape route gets more expensive every week. Put the authoritative copy in something built to hold it.

THE DISTINCTION THAT MOST SUMMARIES FLATTEN: the SOURCE is triple-licensed,
and Elastic's DEFAULT BINARY DISTRIBUTION remains under the Elastic License
2.0. So "Elasticsearch is open source again" is true of the code and not of
the thing you download from elastic.co. If your requirement is an
OSI-licensed BINARY, that is still OpenSearch or a source build.

FOR MOST READERS THIS CHANGES NOTHING. Running Elasticsearch inside your own
application — as a search index, a log store, an analytics engine — was
permitted throughout and remains so.

Most of this unit applies to both, and less of it than it used to

Chapters 1 through 12 are essentially universal: the inverted index, shards and routing, analysis, mappings, the nested-array trap, the write path, the Query DSL, pagination, BM25 and aggregations are shared ancestry and behave the same way. Beyond that, check before relying on specifics. ES|QL, semantic_text, the retrievers API and much of the recent vector work are Elastic's; OpenSearch has its own PPL query language and its own vector implementation with different options. The security chapter differs most — OpenSearch gives away field- and document-level security and SSO that Elastic charges for, which for some organisations is the deciding factor by itself. Choose on licence policy, on what your cloud provider offers as a managed service, and on which specific recent features you need.

Signals you chose wrong

Worth checking against your own system. You are running update_by_query regularly to keep denormalised copies in step, which means the data is relational. Somebody has raised max_result_window. You cannot answer "what would we do if the cluster was lost". Your mapping has thousands of fields and nobody chose them. You are reconciling Elasticsearch counts against a database and losing. Every schema change is a multi-day reindex project. Nobody on the team can explain what a shard is, and it is not managed. Any one of these is survivable; three together means the data has outgrown the tool, and the honest move is to give it a proper home and let Elasticsearch go back to being an index over it.

Where the unit ends up

Almost everything in these eighteen chapters follows from the trade in chapter 1.

It precomputes an index over the contents of documents
Which is why content-based search costs what the results cost rather than what the corpus costs — and why analysis happens at index time (chapter 3), why the mapping is fixed once written (chapter 4), why an update reindexes the whole document (chapter 6), and why aggregations run over a columnar copy rather than over the documents themselves (chapter 12).
It distributes that index across independent shards
Which is why the shard count is in the routing formula and cannot change (chapter 2), why IDF is computed per shard and relevance is slightly local (chapter 10), why every shard must return from + size and deep paging is expensive (chapter 9), why a terms aggregation's top N can be wrong (chapter 12), and why there are no joins and no cross-document transactions at all (chapter 5).

The operational chapters are mostly consequences of those two. Segment immutability gives you near-real-time search and the merge process (chapters 6 and 14). The fact that deleting documents is expensive and dropping an index is free produces the entire lifecycle apparatus (chapter 15). And the security defaults are the residue of a tool built in 2010 for a trusted network, corrected in 2022 (chapter 16).

What is left, once all of that is accounted for, is a genuinely remarkable piece of engineering with an unusually clear set of things it will not do. It will not guarantee your write is visible to the next read. It will not join. It will not give you an exact count above ten thousand. It will not let you change your mind about a field's type. None of those are defects to work around — they are the shape of the trade, and every serious Elasticsearch problem in this unit came from someone expecting otherwise.

The short version

  1. Ask whether you could rebuild the cluster. "Yes, we reindex" means it fits; "no, this is where the data lives" means it does not.
  2. Two things it is excellent at: ranked full-text search, and analytics over large volumes of semi-structured data.
  3. Relational data is the commonest mis-fit, and every workaround has sharp edges.
  4. Elasticsearch source is triple-licensed including AGPLv3 since 2024; the default binary distribution is still ELv2.
  5. OpenSearch is Apache 2.0 throughout, governed by a Linux Foundation project, and gives away FLS, DLS and SSO.
  6. Chapters 1–12 apply to both. ES|QL, semantic_text, retrievers and the newer vector work do not.
  7. Postgres full-text search is enough for a lot of people, and it comes with joins and transactions.
  8. Everything here follows from precomputing an index and distributing it. The limits are the shape of the trade, not flaws in it.
