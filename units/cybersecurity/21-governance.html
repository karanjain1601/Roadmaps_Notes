Governance, risk and compliance

The unglamorous half of the field, and the half that decides whether any of the other chapters get funded. Governance is how security decisions get made and recorded; risk management is how they get prioritised; compliance is how somebody else checks. All three are useful and all three can become theatre.

~28 min read Frameworks · risk registers · policy · audit 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapter 1

21.1What governance is for

Three questions that have nothing to do with technology and determine everything about it: who decides, on what basis, and who is accountable when it goes wrong. Organisations without answers do not have less security — they have security that varies by whoever happened to be in the room.

The pieces, and what each is actually for
PiecePurposeSign it has become theatre

Security does not own security risk

The most consequential governance decision an organisation makes is whether risk sits with the security function or with the business owner of the system. If security owns it, then security is accountable for outcomes it cannot control, every decision becomes a negotiation with a team that can overrule it, and "the security team approved it" becomes a way to transfer blame. If the system owner owns it, security advises, quantifies and objects on the record, and the person who benefits from the risky decision is the person who carries it. That single arrangement changes behaviour more than any control in this unit, because it aligns who decides with who pays.

21.3The risk register

What a row needs to be worth writing down 
DESCRIPTION   A specific scenario, not a category.
              ✗ "Cyber attack"
              ✓ "Ransomware deploys through the unpatched

                 backups, halting order processing"
              If it cannot be pictured, it cannot be priced.

              the business owner, not the security team.

INHERENT      Likelihood × impact before controls.
RESIDUAL      …and after. Both, or you cannot show whether
              anything you did helped.

TREATMENT     Mitigate / transfer / avoid / accept — ch 1.
              With actions, owners and dates.

APPETITE      Is the residual inside what the organisation
              has said it will tolerate? This is the question
              a register exists to answer, and the one most
              registers never state a threshold for.

REVIEW DATE   Because everything above decays.

THE TESTS OF A REGISTER THAT WORKS
  · Has anything MOVED in the last quarter?
  · Is anything GREEN? If nothing is, the scale is wrong.
  · Would an owner recognise their own entry?
  · Did any decision this year cite it?
  A register nobody consults is a document, not a control.
Heat maps hide more than they show

The five-by-five grid of likelihood against impact is nearly universal and has well-known problems. It compresses several orders of magnitude into five bands, so a one-in-three-years event and a one-in-fifty-years event land in the same row. The colours are assigned arbitrarily and drive decisions the underlying numbers do not support. Different people mean different things by "medium", and nobody notices because the word is shared. And crucially it cannot aggregate: twenty ambers do not combine into anything, whereas twenty quantified risks add up to a number you can compare against a budget. Where decisions involve real money, express risk in currency with a range — the ALE arithmetic from Chapter 1 — and keep the grid for communication rather than for analysis.

21.4Policy that people follow

Why most policy fails, and what works instead 
IT FAILS BECAUSE
  · It forbids something people must do to work, so they
    route around it — and the workaround is unmanaged.
  · Nobody can find it, or it is forty pages of legal text.
  · There is no exception process, so the only options are
    comply or hide.
  · It is never enforced, which teaches that no policy is.
  · It was written for an audit rather than for a reader.

WHAT WORKS
  Short. One page per topic, in plain language, saying what
  to do rather than what not to do.

  Enforced by the system, not the document. A policy saying
  "use strong passwords" is a wish; a password filter that
  rejects breached ones is a control. Prefer the paved road:
  make the compliant path the easiest path.

  With a fast exception route. Days, not a change board. An
  exception that is recorded, owned and expiring is vastly
  better than a rule quietly ignored.

  Owned by someone who will revise it. A policy with no
  review date describes a company that no longer exists.

  Consistent with reality. Every rule nobody follows
  devalues every rule that matters.

21.5Compliance and security

Where compliance and risk diverge

Two sets that overlap and are not the same. The interesting parts are the bits outside the overlap.

What each set contains that the other does not
Required and does not reduce much riskReduces risk and is not required
Annual awareness training as a completion percentagePhishing-resistant MFA everywhere
Policy documents that exist and are signedRemoving local administrator rights
Quarterly scans, whether or not findings are fixedUnique local admin passwords, and disabling LLMNR
Asset inventory covering the audited scope onlyAdministrative tiering
Evidence collection for the auditor's sampleImmutable, tested backups
Password rotation every 90 days, in older standardsTesting that detections actually fire — ch 20

The honest position is that compliance is a floor and a forcing function, not a ceiling. It gets security budget approved, gives you leverage to require things, and imposes a discipline of documentation and review that most organisations would otherwise skip. It also consumes an enormous share of a small team's time in evidence collection, and it rewards the appearance of control. The productive stance is to treat the framework as a checklist you must satisfy and a risk register as the thing that decides where the remaining effort goes — and to map them explicitly, so that work done for one counts for the other.

Your organisation passed its audit with no findings and was then breached. Is the framework worthless?

No, but it was being used for the wrong purpose, and the outcome is predictable rather than surprising. An audit tests whether specified controls exist and operate, within a defined scope, at points in time — it does not test whether those controls address the threats you actually face, whether the scope covers where your risk lives, or whether the controls work against a competent adversary rather than against a sampling procedure. So a clean report and a breach are entirely compatible, and treating the first as evidence against the second is the core mistake. Three specific gaps explain most cases. Scope: audits frequently cover one product, one environment or one data type, and the intrusion arrives through the corporate estate, a supplier, or a system nobody put in scope. Point-in-time: evidence is collected for a sample of dates, and controls that operated during the audit window can degrade immediately afterwards — which is the drift problem from Chapter 11. Control depth: a requirement is satisfied by having a documented process, and having a process is not the same as the process being effective; "vulnerability scanning is performed quarterly" can be true while nothing is ever remediated. What the framework is genuinely good for is different from what people expect of it: it establishes a floor, forces documentation and review that would otherwise be skipped, provides leverage to require things from other teams, and gives executives a vocabulary. What it cannot do is prioritise your remaining effort — that is what a risk register and tested detection coverage are for, and an organisation that has the first and not the second is complying rather than defending.

Why should the business owner hold security risk rather than the security team?

Because accountability should sit with whoever benefits from the decision and can actually change it, and the security team is neither. Consider a product team that wants to ship without a control, on a deadline. If security owns the risk, the conversation becomes a negotiation the security team can lose, and if they lose, they remain accountable for a decision made against their advice — which is both unfair and ineffective, because the people who can change the design have no stake in the outcome. Worse, "security signed off" becomes a mechanism for transferring blame, and security functions learn to say no reflexively to protect themselves, which makes them an obstacle to route around. If the system owner holds the risk, the incentives realign: security's job becomes advising, quantifying and objecting on the record, and the owner weighs the risk against the benefit they are receiving, which is exactly the trade-off from Chapter 1 being made by the person best placed to make it. It also makes acceptance meaningful — a risk accepted by a named owner with a documented rationale and a review date is a management decision, whereas the same risk accepted by a security team is a security failure waiting to be described as one. Two practical requirements make this work rather than becoming a way to dump risk on people who do not understand it. The security function must present risk in terms the owner can evaluate, which usually means money and business consequence rather than CVSS scores. And there must be an escalation path when an owner accepts a risk that exceeds the organisation's stated appetite, because individual owners cannot accept risk on behalf of the whole company — which is what a risk appetite statement exists to define, and why so few registers are useful without one.

A requirement in your framework seems pointless for your environment. What do you do?

Ask what the control is intended to achieve, then either satisfy that intent differently or document a compensating control — and do it openly rather than either implementing something useless or quietly skipping it. Most frameworks are written to apply across wildly different organisations, so specific requirements sometimes make no sense in a particular architecture: a control specifying antivirus on servers is odd for immutable container hosts that are replaced rather than patched; a requirement for quarterly firewall rule reviews is strange where there are no firewalls in the traditional sense. In every case the requirement encodes an objective — prevent malicious code executing, ensure network access rules remain appropriate — and the objective usually is relevant even where the prescribed mechanism is not. So the productive response is to state the objective, describe how your architecture achieves it, and present that to the auditor as a compensating control with evidence. Auditors are generally receptive to this when it is reasoned and documented, and hostile to it when it looks like avoidance, so the quality of the write-up matters. Two things not to do. Do not implement the control literally when it achieves nothing — you will pay for it forever, it adds attack surface and operational burden, and it teaches everyone that the programme is performative. And do not skip it silently, because an undocumented gap discovered by an auditor costs far more credibility than a documented deviation raised by you. There is a third option worth remembering: sometimes the right answer is to shrink the scope so the requirement no longer applies, which is nearly always cheaper than complying with it.

Recap

  1. Governance answers who decides, on what basis, and who is accountable.
  2. The system owner holds the risk, not the security team — that single arrangement changes behaviour most.
  3. NIST CSF to structure, CIS Controls to know what to do next, ISO and SOC 2 when a customer requires the certificate.
  4. Map one control set to every framework so work done once counts many times.
  5. A risk register entry needs a picturable scenario, a named owner, and a review date.
  6. Heat maps cannot aggregate. Where money is involved, quantify with a range.
  7. Policy enforced by the system beats policy written in a document, and a fast exception route beats a rule people hide from.
  8. Compliance is a floor and a forcing function, and it rewards the appearance of control.
  9. Generate audit evidence as a by-product of controls operating, not in the fortnight before.
  10. Shrinking the scope is usually cheaper than complying with it.
