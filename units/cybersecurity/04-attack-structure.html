How an intrusion is structured

An intrusion is not an event, it is a sequence — and the sequence is the defender's advantage. The attacker must complete every step; you only have to break one, and you get several chances to notice. This chapter is the three models that make that concrete, and the one that tells you which detections are worth building.

~30 min read Kill chain · ATT&CK · diamond · pyramid of pain 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapters 1–3

4.1Why the structure is the point

The single most useful reframing in defensive security: a breach is not a moment when someone "gets in". It is a campaign with prerequisites, and each stage depends on the last succeeding. That asymmetry runs in the defender's favour, which is unusual enough to be worth stating plainly.

The asymmetry 
THE ATTACKER must succeed at every stage:
  find a way in → get code running → survive a reboot →
  get privileges → find what they want → move to it →
  collect it → get it out — all without being noticed.

THE DEFENDER needs to break the chain ONCE, and gets an
independent chance at every stage.

If you detect or block each stage with probability p, the
chance of catching a chain of n stages is 1 − (1−p)^n.
Even at p = 0.3, eight stages gives 94%.

WHICH MEANS the goal is not a perfect control anywhere.
It is ADEQUATE COVERAGE EVERYWHERE — and specifically,
coverage of stages the attacker cannot skip.
Not every stage is equally worth defending

Two things vary across the chain. Cost of detection to you — some stages generate abundant signal, others almost none. And cost of being detected to them — being caught at delivery costs the attacker a phishing email, while being caught at lateral movement costs them the weeks of work that got them there. The best detections sit where those two are favourable at once: cheap for you to see, expensive for them to lose. The panel in §4.6 makes that trade explicit.

4.2The cyber kill chain

Lockheed Martin's model, and the one most people meet first. Seven linear stages describing an intrusion from the attacker's side.

Seven stages, and what each looks like to a defender
#StageThe attacker isYou might see
1ReconnaissanceGathering names, addresses, technologies, suppliersAlmost nothing. Most of it happens on other people's systems
2WeaponisationBuilding the payload and the lureNothing at all — it happens entirely on their infrastructure
3DeliverySending it — email, a compromised site, a USB stick, an exposed serviceMail gateway logs, web proxy, edge telemetry. Your first real chance
4ExploitationGetting code to runProcess creation with an unexpected parent, EDR alerts, crash telemetry
5InstallationEstablishing persistenceNew autostart entries, services, scheduled tasks — ch 3
6Command and controlOpening a channel back outBeaconing patterns, new domains, unusual egress. A high-yield stage
7Actions on objectivesStealing, encrypting, destroying, or staying putBulk reads, mass file changes, large outbound transfers

Its weakness, worth knowing because you will hear it criticised: it is malware-and-perimeter shaped. It describes an external actor delivering a payload well, and describes poorly an attacker who logs in with valid credentials bought from a broker — which is now extremely common and skips stages 1 to 5 entirely. It also compresses everything interesting into stage 7, when in reality lateral movement, privilege escalation and discovery are most of the campaign and most of the detection opportunity.

4.3MITRE ATT&CK

The successor in practice: a catalogue of what attackers actually do, built from observed intrusions rather than from a theory of how they should proceed. Its structure is the important part.

Tactic, technique, procedure — and why the distinction matters 
TACTIC     the attacker's GOAL at that moment
           "Persistence" — they want to survive a reboot
           14 of them, and they are stable. Adversaries have
           been wanting these same things for decades.

TECHNIQUE  HOW they achieve it
           T1053 Scheduled Task/Job — one of many ways to persist
           Hundreds, with sub-techniques. Changes slowly.

PROCEDURE  the SPECIFIC implementation
           "creates a task named GoogleUpdateCheck running
            powershell -enc <base64> every 30 minutes"
           Infinite, and changes constantly.

THE 14 TACTICS, roughly in order of use:
  Reconnaissance          Resource development
  Initial access          Execution
  Persistence             Privilege escalation
  Defense evasion         Credential access
  Discovery               Lateral movement
  Collection              Command and control
  Exfiltration            Impact

WHY THIS SHAPE IS USEFUL
  Detect at the TECHNIQUE level, not the procedure level.
  A rule matching that exact task name catches one campaign.
  A rule matching "a scheduled task was created with an
  encoded PowerShell command line" catches a whole class,
  including tomorrow's variant.

Note that the tactics are not a sequence. An intrusion loops: discover, move, escalate, discover again. Defense evasion and command and control run throughout. Drawing ATT&CK as a linear chain is a common mistake that leads to expecting stages in an order they will not arrive in.

4.4The diamond model

The third model, and the one for analysis rather than detection. Every intrusion event has four features, and each edge between them is an inference you can make.

In practice this is what turns a single alert into an investigation. You have one malicious domain. Pivot to infrastructure: what else resolves to that IP, what else was registered by that registrant, on the same day, with the same certificate. Pivot to capability: what other samples share that code, that configuration, that mutex. Pivot to victim: who else in your estate contacted any of it. Each pivot expands the picture, and the expansion is how you find the parts of an intrusion the original alert did not cover.

4.5The pyramid of pain

David Bianco's model, and the single most useful idea in detection engineering. Not all indicators are equal: they differ enormously in how much it costs the attacker when you detect on them.

Indicator types by cost to the adversary

Pick what you are detecting on and see what it actually costs them to evade it.

The practical consequence: hash and IP blocklists are worth having and are not a detection strategy. They are free, they catch commodity noise, and a competent adversary defeats them without noticing they existed. The detections that actually cost an attacker something are the behavioural ones — and those are also the ones that survive the attacker changing their infrastructure, which they do constantly.

4.6Where detection pays

Chain coverage

Which stages you can see, what each catch is worth, and the probability the whole chain gets through.

The gap almost everyone has

Security spending concentrates at the perimeter and the endpoint — mail filtering, EDR, firewalls — because those are the products that exist and the stages people picture. The middle of the chain is where the gap is: credential access, discovery, lateral movement and command and control. An attacker who gets past the first two stages frequently operates for weeks unobserved, because nothing is watching an authenticated user reading files they have permission to read, from a machine they normally use, over a protocol that is always allowed. Those stages are also where detection is worth most to you, because catching an intruder there means catching them before objectives — and worth most against them, because they lose everything invested to get that far.

4.7Dwell time

The metric that matters more than "did we get breached", because the answer to that eventually becomes yes. Dwell time is how long an attacker is present before detection, and it decides whether an incident is an inconvenience or a catastrophe.

What dwell time buys the attacker
Detected atThey haveYour response is
Minutes — delivery or executionOne foothold on one machineReimage it. An event, not an incident
Hours — persistence or C2A foothold and a channelContain the host, hunt for others, rotate what was exposed
Days — lateral movementMultiple hosts, harvested credentials, a map of your networkA real incident. Assume credentials are compromised estate-wide
Weeks — collection or exfiltrationDomain privileges, data staged, backups locatedMajor. You are now deciding about disclosure, and you may not know the full scope
Months — often found by a third partyEverything, plus persistence you have not foundRebuild rather than remediate. Trust nothing in the environment

Two forces pull in opposite directions here. Detection has improved substantially over the past decade, so median dwell times have fallen from months to weeks or less in most published measurements. But attacker speed has improved faster in the ransomware case specifically: encryption now frequently begins within hours to a day or two of initial access, which means a detection capability that reliably catches things in three days is no longer sufficient for that threat. The metrics to actually track are mean time to detect and mean time to respond, and they should be measured from the earliest attacker activity you can establish afterwards, not from when the alert fired.

4.8Using the models

A coverage exercise you can run in an afternoon 
1  PICK A RELEVANT ADVERSARY
   Not "APT28" if you are a regional retailer. Pick the
   threats that match your sector and size — commodity
   ransomware, business email compromise, an insider.
   ATT&CK groups and campaigns give you their techniques.

2  LIST THE TECHNIQUES THEY USE
   Twenty to forty, typically. Not the whole matrix.

3  FOR EACH, ASK THREE QUESTIONS
   · Could we PREVENT it?      (a control)
   · Could we DETECT it?       (a rule, and the log to feed it)
   · Have we TESTED that?      (executed it and watched)
   Question three is the one that changes the answer. Assumed
   coverage and demonstrated coverage differ constantly.

4  RANK THE GAPS
   By stage — favour the middle of the chain — and by
   pyramid level. A behavioural gap outranks a hash gap.

5  FIX A FEW, AND RE-TEST
   Then repeat next quarter. Coverage maps that are produced
   once and framed are a compliance artefact; ones that get
   re-tested are a security programme.        → ch 16, ch 20
Why is detecting on file hashes described as almost worthless, when hash blocklists are used everywhere?

Because the cost of evasion is essentially zero, and the cost of maintaining the blocklist is not. Changing a file's hash requires changing one byte — appending a null, altering a string, recompiling — and any of those produces a completely different hash while the file behaves identically. Malware builders do this automatically, generating a unique sample per target, which is why the same family can have millions of distinct hashes. So a hash detection catches exactly one build of one tool used by whoever has not bothered to repack, which is real but is the bottom of the population. The reason they are used everywhere anyway is that they are cheap, they are unambiguous when they hit, and they clear a large volume of commodity noise that would otherwise consume analyst time — that is a legitimate function. The mistake is treating them as coverage. If your detection strategy is a threat-intelligence feed of hashes and IP addresses, you have a strategy against adversaries who are not trying, and none against anyone else. The corollary is where to spend instead: behaviour is at the top of the pyramid because changing it means changing how the adversary operates, retraining people, rewriting tooling and re-testing — weeks of work rather than a recompile — and a behavioural detection keeps working when they rotate infrastructure, which they do continuously.

An attacker buys valid VPN credentials and logs in. Which kill chain stages happen, and what does that imply?

Stages 1 through 5 mostly do not happen in your environment at all, which is exactly the criticism of the model. There is no weaponisation because there is no payload; no delivery because nothing is sent to you; no exploitation because nothing is exploited; and possibly no installation, because valid credentials are the persistence — they survive reboots and do not appear in any autostart location. The intrusion effectively begins at what the kill chain calls actions on objectives, and every control built around the earlier stages sees nothing. Mail filtering, EDR's exploit protection, attachment sandboxing and payload signatures are all bypassed, not defeated — they were never in the path. This is why the technique matters so much in practice: credentials are bought from access brokers or harvested by infostealers on unmanaged personal machines, and the login looks legitimate because it is. What does detect it is identity-layer telemetry: an impossible-travel or unusual-geography authentication, a first-time-seen device, an account that suddenly authenticates at an unusual hour, and then the discovery activity that follows — an account enumerating shares or querying the directory in ways it never has. That is the argument for identity monitoring being a distinct capability rather than a firewall log, and for phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication being the highest-value preventive control against the case entirely, since a stolen password alone is then insufficient.

If each of eight stages is detected with probability 0.3, why is the real-world catch rate lower than the 94% the arithmetic gives?

Because the stages are not independent, and the formula assumes they are. Several kinds of correlation break it. A single evasion covers many stages: an attacker who disables or blinds the EDR agent removes the detection at execution, persistence, privilege escalation and lateral movement simultaneously, so those four probabilities collapse together rather than multiplying. Shared telemetry: if six of your eight detections read from the same log source, a gap in that source — an agent not deployed on a subnet, a log pipeline that dropped events, a server excluded for performance — removes all six at once. Correlated blind spots: if every detection assumes activity is visible on the network, encrypted C2 through a legitimate cloud service defeats them all. And the alert is not the catch: a detection that fires into a queue nobody triages within the attacker's timeline has a real-world probability far below its nominal one, which is why alert volume and analyst capacity are security controls even though they appear on no product datasheet. The practical reading of the formula is therefore not "94%, we are fine" but rather its qualitative point: coverage at several independent stages beats excellent coverage at one. And the test that matters is the same one as in §4.8 — execute the technique and see whether the detection actually fires and actually gets actioned, because assumed probabilities are consistently higher than measured ones.

Recap

  1. The attacker must complete every stage; you need to break one — and you get an independent chance at each.
  2. The kill chain is perimeter- and malware-shaped, and describes a credential-based intrusion badly.
  3. ATT&CK separates tactic, technique and procedure. Detect at the technique level — procedures change constantly.
  4. The tactics are not a sequence. Intrusions loop, and evasion and C2 run throughout.
  5. The diamond model is for pivoting: each edge turns one indicator into an investigation.
  6. The pyramid of pain ranks indicators by evasion cost. Hashes and IPs are free to change; behaviour is not.
  7. The gap is the middle of the chain — credential access, discovery, lateral movement, C2 — because that is where nobody is looking.
  8. Dwell time decides severity. Minutes is an event; months means rebuild rather than remediate.
  9. Ransomware now moves in hours, so a three-day detection capability is not sufficient for it.
  10. Coverage must be tested, not assumed. Executing the technique changes the answer more often than not.
