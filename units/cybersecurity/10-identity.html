Identity and access

Identity is the real perimeter now, and it is where intrusions are won. An attacker with valid credentials does not need an exploit, does not trip malware detection, and looks exactly like an employee — so the controls that matter are the ones governing how identities are proved, what they are permitted, and how quickly one can become another.

Origin binding is the whole difference

Every method above the line produces a code — a value the user reads and types, which means it can be typed into the wrong place. Every method below it performs a cryptographic operation scoped to a domain: the authenticator checks which origin is asking and signs only for that one. A user who is completely deceived, on a pixel-perfect replica, still cannot produce a credential the attacker can use, because the attacker's domain is not your domain and the authenticator refuses. That is why the phishing-resistant category is not an incremental improvement but a different guarantee, and why it is the highest-value control against the most common intrusion route (Chapter 5).

10.2Directories and Kerberos

Inside an organisation, identity usually means Active Directory: a database of users, computers and groups, queried over LDAP, with authentication by Kerberos. Understanding the Kerberos exchange matters because most internal attack techniques are steps in it being abused.

The Kerberos exchange, and where it is attacked

Each message in the flow, with the technique that targets it. Toggle the controls to see which paths close.

Why kerberoasting works and cannot simply be blocked

Any authenticated user may request a service ticket for any service principal name — that is not a bug, it is how the protocol distributes tickets. The ticket is encrypted with the service account's password-derived key, so anyone who receives one can attempt to crack that password offline, at whatever rate their hardware allows, with no failed logons and nothing to lock out. The request itself is indistinguishable from legitimate use. So the defence is not to prevent the request but to make cracking pointless: use group managed service accounts, whose passwords are 120 random characters rotated automatically, at which point an offline attack has nothing to find. Failing that, enforce genuinely long random passwords on any account with an SPN, remove SPNs that are no longer needed, and never give a service account privileged group membership — the reason this technique is so valuable is that service accounts are so often domain admins.

The other protocols in this space: LDAP for directory queries and simple bind authentication, which sends credentials in the clear on port 389 unless TLS is used. RADIUS for network access — the back end behind 802.1X port authentication and WPA2-Enterprise, and worth noting that its original packet protection is weak, so it should run over a protected transport. And SCIM for provisioning, which is the unglamorous piece that actually matters: without automated deprovisioning, leavers keep access indefinitely, and dormant accounts belonging to people who left are a recurring finding.

10.4Least privilege in practice

Everyone agrees with the principle and almost nobody implements it, because permissions accumulate and nothing removes them. Three mechanisms make it real:

10.5The escalation path

From a phishing click to domain admin

The standard path, and which control breaks each hop. Every hop must succeed; you need to close one.

Attack paths are a graph, and graphs can be analysed

The escalation above is not a list of separate weaknesses, it is a path through a graph whose nodes are principals and machines and whose edges are "can control". Tools such as BloodHound compute those edges from directory data and find the shortest route from any starting point to domain admin — and the striking result, in most environments that run it for the first time, is that the path is three or four hops from an ordinary user, through edges nobody designed: a group nested inside a group, a service account with write permission on an object, a workstation where an administrator once logged on. Running this analysis on your own directory is one of the highest-yield exercises available, because it turns "we should follow least privilege" into a specific list of edges to delete, ranked by how many paths each one removes.

Why does a domain administrator logging on to a workstation create a serious risk?

Because authenticating to a machine leaves credential material on it, and that machine is far less trustworthy than the account is valuable. Depending on the logon type and configuration, what remains in memory can include an NTLM hash, a Kerberos ticket-granting ticket, or a reusable credential — and anyone who later obtains administrative access on that workstation can extract it and become the domain admin. Note the direction of the risk: the workstation does not need to be compromised at the time. The credential sits there, and a compromise weeks later still yields it, which is why "I only logged on once to fix something" is not mitigation. The workstation is also the machine that opens email attachments, browses the web and runs whatever software the user needs, so it is the most likely thing in the estate to be compromised — you have put the most valuable credential on the least defensible host. This is precisely what administrative tiering prevents, with the rule that a credential from a higher tier is never exposed on a lower-tier machine: domain admins administer domain controllers from privileged access workstations, server admins handle servers, and workstation support uses accounts that are powerful on workstations and powerless everywhere else. Supporting controls make the rule enforceable — the Protected Users group and Credential Guard reduce what is left in memory, logon-restriction policies deny tier-0 accounts the right to log on to lower tiers at all, and just-in-time elevation means there is usually no standing domain admin credential to expose. If you implement one thing from this chapter in a Windows environment, it is this.

What is a golden ticket, and why does rotating krbtgt twice matter?

A golden ticket is a forged ticket-granting ticket, made possible by having the password hash of the krbtgt account. That account's key is what the domain controller uses to encrypt and sign every TGT it issues, so anyone holding it can mint a TGT for any user — including one who does not exist — with any group membership, any validity period, and no interaction with the domain controller during creation. The domain controller then accepts it, because it verifies the ticket by decrypting it with a key that matches. This is why it is regarded as full and persistent domain compromise rather than one more escalation: the attacker no longer needs any real account, password changes do not affect them, disabling accounts does not affect them, and the forged ticket can be valid for years. Recovery requires changing the krbtgt password, and it must be done twice because Active Directory retains the previous key so that tickets issued just before a change continue to work. A single rotation leaves the old key still valid, so an attacker holding it retains access; the second rotation, after replication of the first has completed across all domain controllers, finally invalidates it. Rushing the two changes together breaks legitimate authentication estate-wide, so the sequence matters. Two related points worth holding. The same logic applied to a service account's key produces a silver ticket, which forges a service ticket for one service and never contacts the domain controller at all, making it much quieter and correspondingly harder to detect. And because obtaining the krbtgt hash requires domain-controller-level access already, a golden ticket is a persistence mechanism rather than an escalation — which means finding one implies the real compromise happened earlier and is what the investigation must actually establish.

Your organisation has MFA on the identity provider. Is internal access protected?

Only for the paths that go through it, and inside a traditional network a great many do not. MFA at the identity provider protects federated sign-in to web applications — the SaaS estate, the VPN portal, the cloud console. It does nothing for authentication that happens directly against Active Directory using Kerberos or NTLM, which is how a workstation reaches a file share, how a remote desktop session opens, how WinRM executes a command, and how most lateral movement operates. An attacker who has a password or a hash, or who has stolen a Kerberos ticket from memory, moves between internal systems without ever presenting to the identity provider, so the MFA prompt is simply not in the path. Three gaps compound it. Legacy protocols that predate modern authentication often cannot support a second factor and are left enabled for one application nobody wants to break. Service accounts and machine accounts authenticate with secrets and no interactive factor by definition, and they are frequently over-privileged. And session tokens issued after a successful MFA remain valid for their lifetime, so an infostealer that takes a cookie has authenticated access without touching the login at all. What closes the gap is a combination: phishing-resistant factors so the initial credential is hard to steal, conditional access that evaluates device state and re-checks continuously rather than trusting a token until expiry, disabling legacy authentication protocols outright, tiering so that a compromised workstation credential cannot reach tier-0 systems, and treating internal authentication events as telemetry worth alerting on. The summary is that MFA is necessary and is a control on one doorway, and internal networks have many doors.

Recap

  1. A password plus a security question is one factor twice. Location and behaviour are signals, not factors.
  2. Origin binding is the difference between codes that can be relayed and credentials that cannot.
  3. Kerberoasting cannot be blocked, only made pointless — managed service accounts with random rotated passwords.
  4. AS-REP roasting needs only an account with pre-authentication disabled. Audit for them.
  5. A golden ticket is persistence, not escalation, and recovery needs krbtgt rotated twice.
  6. The identity provider is a single point of total compromise. Administering it is the most privileged role you have.
  7. Tiering is the highest-value structural control: a higher-tier credential is never exposed on a lower-tier machine.
  8. Just-in-time access turns a decade-old list into a question about right now.
  9. MFA at the identity provider does not cover Kerberos, NTLM or stolen session tokens.
  10. Attack paths are a graph. Compute it, and delete the edges that remove the most paths.
