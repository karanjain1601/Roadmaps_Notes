Red, blue and purple

The colours describe functions rather than teams, and the most valuable of the three is the one that is not a team at all. Purple is what happens when the people simulating attacks and the people detecting them work in the same room — and it converts assumed coverage into demonstrated coverage, which is usually a shorter list than anyone expects.

Red teaming is not penetration testing with a better name

A penetration test asks what can be exploited in a defined scope, and success is a thorough list. A red team engagement asks whether the organisation would notice and stop a determined adversary pursuing a specific objective — reaching the payment system, exfiltrating the customer database, deploying to production. It is broad rather than deep, uses whatever route works including physical and social, is usually unannounced to the defenders, and produces very few findings: often just a narrative and an answer to "were we detected, and when?". That makes it a test of people and process at least as much as technology, and it is only worth the cost once there is a detection capability to test.

20.2Adversary emulation

The discipline that makes red work reproducible: rather than an operator improvising, you emulate the documented behaviour of a specific threat actor relevant to your sector, using their techniques in their sequence.

Building an emulation plan 
1  CHOOSE A RELEVANT ADVERSARY
   From your sector's threat picture, not from whoever is
   in the news. A regional manufacturer should emulate the
   ransomware affiliates that target manufacturers, not a
   state programme they will never meet.

2  EXTRACT THEIR TECHNIQUES
   ATT&CK group pages, incident reports, vendor research.
   Typically twenty to forty techniques in a rough order.

3  BUILD THE SEQUENCE
   Initial access → execution → persistence → credential
   access → discovery → lateral movement → collection →
   exfiltration or impact. Their sequence, not a generic one.

4  EXECUTE AT THE RIGHT LEVEL
   Atomic tests    one technique, one command, minutes
                   → validate a single detection
   Micro-emulation a short chain of three or four
                   → validate that a sequence correlates
   Full emulation  the whole plan, in order, over days
                   → validate the response process too

5  MEASURE PER TECHNIQUE, not overall
   For each: was it PREVENTED, DETECTED (an alert fired and
   was actioned), LOGGED (the data exists but nothing fired),
   or INVISIBLE (no telemetry at all)?
   Those four outcomes need four different remedies.

FREE STARTING POINTS
   Atomic Red Team gives per-technique tests you can run
   today. CALDERA automates chains. MITRE publishes full
   emulation plans for several named adversaries.

20.3Purple teaming

Assumed coverage against demonstrated coverage

Run each technique and record what actually happened. The four outcomes need four different fixes.

Four outcomes, four different remedies
OutcomeMeansFix
PreventedThe technique did not workNothing — but verify it is a control rather than luck, and that it holds across the estate
DetectedAn alert fired and someone acted on itRecord it as demonstrated coverage, with the date
LoggedThe evidence exists; nothing firedWrite the rule. This is the cheapest gap to close and usually the largest category
InvisibleNo telemetry at allA data problem, not a detection one. Enable the source first — ch 15
"Logged but not alerted" is where the value is

The most common result of a first purple team exercise is a large "logged" column: the telemetry was already being collected and paid for, and nothing was written to act on it. Those are the cheapest possible improvements — no new product, no new data source, no licence increase, just a rule written against data you already have. It is also the finding that most reliably justifies the exercise, because it converts existing spend into coverage. The "invisible" column is more expensive because it requires enabling or buying a source first, and "detected" needs a date attached, since a detection that worked last year against a source that has since gone quiet is not coverage.

20.4Running an exercise that works

The format, and the parts people skip 
BEFORE
  · Pick 10–20 techniques, not the whole matrix
  · Agree the outcome definitions above, in writing
  · Everyone in one room or one call, red and blue together
  · Authorisation and a rollback plan for anything intrusive

DURING, per technique
  · Red states what they are about to do, then does it
  · Blue watches their tooling in real time
  · Record the outcome, the timestamp, and the exact
    telemetry that did or did not appear
  · If nothing fired, blue writes the rule THERE AND THEN
    and red re-runs it to confirm — this immediate loop
    is the single most valuable part of the format

AFTER
  · A coverage map with dates, not a percentage
  · New and modified detections, in version control
  · A list of telemetry gaps with owners
  · Schedule the re-test; coverage decays

WHAT MAKES IT FAIL
  · Treating it as an audit rather than a joint exercise
  · Red running everything first and reporting later —
    that is a red team engagement, not a purple one
  · No follow-through: gaps identified and never closed
  · Reporting a coverage percentage to management, which
    invites optimising the number rather than the coverage

Practise only on what you are allowed to touch

Unauthorised access to a computer system is a criminal offence in most jurisdictions, and intent is not a defence — "I was only looking" and "I was going to report it" are not exemptions under the UK Computer Misuse Act, the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, or their equivalents. Scanning a company because you were curious, testing a login page you noticed, or probing a service you happen to use are all offences, regardless of whether you caused harm. The legitimate options are abundant: your own lab, deliberately vulnerable platforms built for the purpose, and bug bounty programmes where the scope is the authorisation — and even there, read the scope carefully, because testing an out-of-scope asset on an in-scope company is not covered. Everything in Part 2 of this unit is written to be used against systems you own or have written permission to test.

Why is purple teaming usually better value than red teaming?

Because it produces specific, fixable gaps rather than a verdict, and it does so far more cheaply and repeatably. A red team engagement answers one broad question — would a determined adversary achieve this objective without being stopped — and for most organisations the answer is yes, which costs a great deal to establish and does not tell you which of a hundred possible improvements to make. It also produces a single narrative rather than a measurement, so you cannot easily compare this year to last year or verify that a fix worked. A purple team exercise inverts all of that: you take twenty relevant techniques, execute them one at a time with the defenders watching, and record for each whether it was prevented, detected, merely logged, or invisible. The output is a coverage map with dates and a list of rules to write, most of which get written during the exercise itself and re-tested immediately — that instant loop is the part that makes the format valuable. It is also cheap enough to repeat quarterly, which matters because coverage decays as environments change and log sources quietly stop reporting. Two qualifications worth keeping. Red teaming tests things purple cannot: the response process under genuine uncertainty, whether people escalate, whether the on-call path works at 3am, and whether the organisation reacts sensibly to something unexpected — those are real and they need an unannounced exercise. And the sequence matters: purple first to build and verify coverage, red afterwards to test whether the whole apparatus holds under pressure. Red first, against nothing, buys an expensive confirmation of the obvious.

A purple team exercise shows 80% of techniques detected. Why be sceptical of that number?

Several reasons, and the first is that a percentage invites optimisation of the percentage. The denominator is chosen — twenty techniques from a matrix of hundreds — so the figure is a property of the selection at least as much as of the defences, and a selection weighted towards things you already detect produces a flattering number that means nothing. Ask which techniques were chosen, by whom, and whether they reflect what a relevant adversary actually uses. Second, "detected" needs a strict definition: an alert appearing in a console is not detection unless someone saw it, recognised it and acted, and a great many exercises count a fired rule while the alert would in reality have queued unread overnight (chapter 12). Third, techniques were executed in isolation with the defenders watching and knowing what was coming, which is close to a best case — the same rule may not fire when the technique is buried in normal activity, or when the attacker has varied the procedure slightly rather than running the atomic test verbatim. Fourth, coverage decays: a detection demonstrated in March may be broken by June because a log source stopped reporting, an exclusion was added, or the environment changed, which is why every entry in a coverage map needs a date and a re-test schedule. And fifth, prevention and detection are being averaged together when they are quite different outcomes. The more useful reporting is not a percentage at all but the four-way breakdown per technique with dates attached, and specifically the size of the "logged but not alerted" column — because that is the actionable part, and it tends to shrink for good reasons or for bad ones depending on whether rules were written or techniques were quietly dropped from the list.

You want to move into detection work. Are CTFs the right way to prepare?

Not primarily, and this is worth saying because CTFs are the default advice regardless of the destination. Competitions are built around offensive puzzles with a hidden answer — reverse this binary, exploit this service, find this flag — and they genuinely develop tool fluency, persistence and specific exploitation skills. What they do not develop is anything resembling defensive work: there is no telemetry to interpret, no base rate problem, no alert queue, no ambiguity about whether something is malicious, and no writing. A detection analyst spends their time reading logs from systems behaving mostly normally and deciding which of a thousand odd-looking things is worth pursuing, which is a different skill from solving a puzzle that is known to have a solution. The route that transfers most directly is a detection lab: build a small Windows domain with a couple of workstations, ship the logs into a free SIEM, install Sysmon with a curated configuration, then run atomic tests against it and try to detect them. That single project teaches log sources and their defaults, what normal looks like, why the command-line field matters, how to write and tune a rule, and why coverage decays — all of which are the actual job. Supplement it with reading real incident write-ups, which show how intrusions genuinely unfold as opposed to how they are taught, and with blue-team-oriented challenges where you are given evidence and asked what happened. CTFs remain worth some time even so: understanding how an attack works makes you better at recognising it, and the offensive perspective is what stops a detection engineer writing rules for things that never happen.

Recap

  1. The colours are functions, not teams, and purple is a format rather than a headcount.
  2. Red teaming asks whether you would notice, and is only worth the cost once there is something to notice with.
  3. Emulate a relevant adversary, from your sector's threat picture rather than the news.
  4. Measure per technique with four outcomes: prevented, detected, logged, invisible.
  5. "Logged but not alerted" is the cheapest gap — the data is already paid for.
  6. Write the rule during the exercise and re-run it. That immediate loop is the format's value.
  7. Report a dated coverage map, not a percentage, because a percentage gets optimised.
  8. Coverage decays. Re-test on a schedule.
  9. Practice routes teach different things. For detection work, build a lab and attack it.
  10. Unauthorised testing is a criminal offence, and intent is not a defence.
