The profession

Security is not one job, and the roles that share the name have less in common than the label suggests. This chapter is what each actually involves day to day, the routes in, an honest read on certifications, and how to keep doing it for longer than three years.

22.1The specialisms

What each role is actually like

Pick a role and see the day, the skills that matter, and the routes in.

Security is a second discipline

Almost every role here is more effective when built on top of something else — systems administration, networking, software development, cloud engineering, data analysis. That is not gatekeeping, it is a description of the work: you cannot secure what you do not understand, and the fastest route into most of these roles is to be good at the underlying thing first. It also explains why the "entry-level security job" is scarce and often mislabelled. A sysadmin who understands Windows internals becomes a strong detection engineer quickly; someone with a security certificate and no operational background usually does not, and struggles in interviews for reasons nobody explains to them.

What actually gets people hired, in rough order 
1  DEMONSTRABLE WORK
   A home lab you can describe. A detection you wrote and
   tuned. A tool you built. A write-up of something you
   investigated. Anything you can be asked questions about
   and answer in depth.

2  ADJACENT EXPERIENCE
   Three years of Linux administration or writing software
   is worth more than three certifications, because the
   job is mostly applying that knowledge adversarially.

3  COMMUNICATION
   Underweighted by candidates and decisive in practice.
   Most of the work is explaining a risk to someone who
   does not want to hear it, or writing a finding that
   causes action. If you can write clearly you are ahead
   of most of the field.

4  CERTIFICATIONS
   Real but limited value — see below. They pass filters
   and rarely win the interview.

5  A DEGREE
   Helps early, matters progressively less, and is not a
   requirement in most of the sector.

THE UNCOMFORTABLE TRUTH ABOUT "ENTRY LEVEL"
  Much of the sector's supposed skills shortage is a
  shortage of MID-LEVEL people, and many "junior" postings
  ask for several years of experience. That is a real
  structural problem and not a reflection on you. The
  reliable workaround is to enter through an adjacent
  technical role and move sideways, which is how a large
  share of practitioners actually arrived.

Is this certification worth it for you?

Value depends heavily on career stage, target role and who is paying.

Two failure modes worth avoiding

Collecting. A candidate with six certifications and no practical work is a recognisable and unhelpful profile — it reads as effort spent on the measurable proxy rather than the thing. Two relevant certifications plus a project you can discuss in depth beats six certificates every time.

Paying for the expensive ones yourself, early. Several well-regarded certifications cost more than a month's salary. Before spending that, exhaust the free and cheap routes: platform documentation, open training material, home labs, and community resources. If a certification is genuinely required for a role, an employer will frequently fund it — and if you are paying, the practical hands-on ones give the best return because they teach as well as certify.

22.4Staying current without drowning

A sustainable information diet 
THE PROBLEM
  The volume is unlimited and most of it is noise, vendor
  marketing, or the same story repeated. Attempting to
  follow everything produces anxiety and no depth.

WHAT ACTUALLY REPAYS ATTENTION
  · Incident write-ups and post-mortems — how intrusions
    genuinely unfold, which differs from how they are
    taught. The single highest-value reading there is.
  · Primary research from the small number of teams doing
    it, rather than the coverage of that research.
  · Your own environment's telemetry. An hour looking at
    what your systems actually do teaches more than a week
    of reading about what other people's systems do.
  · One or two deep sources in your specialism.

WHAT DOES NOT
  · Breach news as it happens. The early reporting is
    usually wrong and the analysis arrives months later.
  · Vulnerability announcements as a feed. Your
    prioritisation should be driven by your own asset
    inventory, not by what is trending — chapter 13.
  · Anything framed as a countdown or a threat to your
    relevance.

A SUSTAINABLE PATTERN
  A fixed, small time budget — an hour or two a week, held
  to. Depth in one area rather than breadth in ten. Write
  something occasionally, because writing exposes what you
  do not actually understand. And accept that you will not
  keep up, because nobody does, including the people who
  appear to.

22.6What good looks like

The habits of practitioners worth learning from 
THEY ASK WHAT THE THREAT IS before recommending anything,
and can say which one a control addresses.

THEY QUANTIFY when it matters, and admit when they cannot.
"I estimate low likelihood and severe impact, and here is
why" beats a colour on a chart.

THEY ARE HONEST ABOUT UNCERTAINTY. "We do not know yet, and
here is what would tell us" is a professional answer, and
early certainty is the thing that damages credibility.

THEY OPTIMISE FOR THE ORGANISATION, not for the security
function. A control that makes everyone's work harder for a
marginal gain is a bad trade even though it is more secure.

THEY WRITE WELL. In this field the work is only as valuable
as the decision it causes, and decisions are caused by
writing.

THEY UNDERSTAND THE SYSTEM, not just the security of it.
The best findings come from knowing how something works
well enough to see what its designers assumed.

THEY ARE KIND ABOUT MISTAKES. The person who clicked the
link, the engineer who left the bucket open, the colleague
who missed the alert — every one of them is more useful to
you as an ally than as an example, and blame is how
organisations stop hearing about problems.

AND THEY KEEP LEARNING WITHOUT PANICKING, because the
fundamentals in this unit change slowly, and almost
everything that feels urgent is a new arrangement of them.
You have a certification and no experience, and cannot get an interview. What now?

Change the strategy rather than adding another certification, because the constraint is not credentials. Employers hiring for security roles are trying to establish whether you can apply knowledge to a messy real system, and a multiple-choice qualification does not answer that question — which is why the candidate with six certificates and no practical work is a recognisable profile that struggles. Three moves change the odds. Build something and write about it: a home lab with a small Windows domain, logs shipped to a free SIEM, atomic tests run against it and detections written for them, documented publicly. That single project gives you concrete answers to interview questions, demonstrates initiative, and is far more discussable than any exam. Enter sideways: the highest-probability route into security is from an adjacent technical role, so a service desk, systems administration, cloud operations or development position is a legitimate step rather than a detour — and once inside an organisation, volunteering for the security-adjacent work before asking for the title is how most internal transfers actually happen. Get a public record: bug bounty findings, open-source contributions, incident write-ups, or answering questions in a community leave evidence someone can check. Two things worth knowing so you calibrate correctly. Much of the sector's talked-about shortage is a shortage of mid-level people, and a great many "junior" postings ask for years of experience, so the difficulty you are experiencing is structural rather than a verdict on you. And communication is weighted far more heavily than candidates expect — if you can write a clear, well-reasoned page about something technical, you are ahead of a large part of the field.

Which specialism should you pick?

The one whose daily activity you would choose to do, rather than the one with the best-sounding title — because the roles differ far more in texture than the shared label suggests, and the mismatch is what makes people leave. Three questions discriminate well. Do you prefer finding problems or building solutions? Penetration testing and threat intelligence are fundamentally about finding and explaining; security engineering, platform work and appsec are about building things that stop the problem recurring. Both are valuable and they suit different temperaments, and people who enjoy breaking things sometimes find that a career of writing reports about broken things is less satisfying than they expected. Do you want depth or breadth? Detection engineering and appsec reward going deep on a narrow area for years; architecture, GRC and consulting reward knowing a little about everything and connecting it. How do you feel about interruption? Security operations and incident response are reactive by nature, with real on-call and genuinely unpredictable days; engineering and governance roles are mostly planned work. That last one is underrated as a predictor of whether someone stays. Two practical notes. Your first specialism is not permanent — movement between them is normal and the fundamentals transfer, so treat the choice as a starting direction rather than a commitment. And the most reliable way to find out is to try the activity rather than read about it: build a detection lab for a weekend and see whether reading logs is absorbing or tedious, because that answer is more informative than any career guide, including this one.

How do you avoid burning out in this field?

By treating several of the causes as engineering problems rather than personal failings, because most of them are. Alert fatigue is arithmetic, not weakness: at security base rates most of what an analyst examines is benign, and a queue that is 95% false positives will exhaust anyone. That makes rule precision a wellbeing metric as much as a technical one, and deleting a noisy detection is an act of care for the team rather than an admission of failure. Being the department of no is usually a governance defect: if the security function owns risk it cannot control, refusal becomes the only available lever, and the fix is moving risk ownership to the business owner (Chapter 21) and offering paved roads rather than prohibitions. Invisible success needs deliberate reporting — prevented and detected things, in specifics, before anyone asks — because otherwise a quiet year looks like an unnecessary team. Three more that are personal rather than structural. Accept that you cannot keep up: the field is unbounded, everyone is ignorant of most of it including the people who appear otherwise, and specialising deliberately is healthier than sampling anxiously. Keep a record of what improved, because memory retains failures and discards progress, and cynicism grows in that gap. And take the moral weight seriously — investigations touch genuinely distressing material, and treating that as an occupational exposure rather than something to absorb quietly is the professional response. The final point is the one people resist: this work will still be here tomorrow, and an unsustainable pace produces worse security than a sustainable one, because tired people miss things and burnt-out people leave.

Recap

  1. Security is a second discipline. You cannot secure what you do not understand.
  2. The roles differ far more than the shared label suggests. Pick by daily activity, not title.
  3. Internal transfer from an adjacent technical role is the highest-probability route in.
  4. Demonstrable work beats certifications, and communication is weighted more than candidates expect.
  5. Hands-on certifications are the respected category; collecting them is a recognisable anti-pattern.
  6. Do not fund the expensive ones yourself early. Exhaust the free routes, and let an employer pay.
  7. Read incident write-ups and your own telemetry; ignore breach news as it happens.
  8. You will not keep up. Nobody does. Specialise and hold a small, fixed time budget.
  9. Alert fatigue and being the department of no are structural, and both have engineering fixes.
  10. Be kind about mistakes — blame is how organisations stop hearing about problems.
End of the unit

Twenty-two chapters, from the CIA triad to the career. If you read it in order, the through-line was this: security is a series of trade-offs made under uncertainty, the fundamentals change slowly, and most real failures are ordinary — a permission set too broadly, a log nobody read, a control that was never tested. The interesting work is in the arithmetic and the judgement, not in the exotic attacks.
