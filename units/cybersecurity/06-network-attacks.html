Network attacks

Most network attacks exploit the same original sin: the protocols the internet runs on were designed for a cooperative network and have no way to verify who is speaking. ARP, DHCP, DNS and BGP all accept assertions from strangers. Everything in this chapter follows from that, and so does every defence.

The general defence

Because the protocols cannot authenticate, the defences fall into three families. Authenticate at a higher layer — TLS makes it irrelevant who relays the packets, which is why the answer to most machine-in-the-middle attacks is "use TLS and validate the certificate" rather than anything at the network layer. Constrain the segment — an attacker who cannot get onto the broadcast domain cannot lie to it, which is what 802.1X and segmentation buy. Add verification the protocol lacks — DHCP snooping, dynamic ARP inspection, RA Guard, RPKI. In practice the first is the one that scales.

6.2Machine-in-the-middle

The attacker positions themselves between two parties who believe they are talking directly. Every technique in §6.1 is a way to achieve that position; what matters afterwards is what the traffic reveals.

Who can be intercepted

An attacker has a foothold on one host. What can they position themselves in the middle of?

Name-resolution poisoning is the quiet one

When a Windows host fails to resolve a name through DNS — a typo, a decommissioned server still in a mapped drive, a stale printer — it falls back to broadcasting the question to the local segment via LLMNR or NBT-NS. Any host can answer "that's me". The victim then authenticates to the attacker, handing over a challenge-response that can be relayed to a real service or cracked offline. No exploit, no malware, no user interaction — just an enabled-by-default protocol and a stale reference. It is one of the highest-yield techniques on an internal network and the fix is genuinely free: disable LLMNR and NBT-NS by policy. Almost nothing needs them, and SMB signing blocks the relay path as well.

6.3Denial of service

Three distinct mechanisms, frequently conflated, requiring different defences.

What is being exhausted

VolumetricBandwidthUDP floods, reflection amplificationUpstream scrubbing. You cannot filter what has already saturated your link

ApplicationCPU, database, workersAn expensive search repeated, a heavy report, a password-hashing endpointRate limiting, caching, cost analysis, shedding — Backend ch 4

The application layer is the one people underestimate: a single request costing you a full-table scan can be more damaging than a gigabit of packets, because it needs no botnet and looks like legitimate traffic. The general principle is asymmetry of cost — anywhere a small request causes large work, you have a denial-of-service surface, and that includes your own password hashing (Backend ch 14), your search endpoint and your export function.

Reflection amplification

The attacker sends a small spoofed query to a public server; the server sends a much larger reply to the victim. Figures are published amplification factors.

Two things make reflection possible, and both are somebody else's misconfiguration. Source address spoofing — the attacker forges the victim's address as the source, which works because most networks do not filter outbound traffic for valid source addresses. The fix is BCP 38 ingress filtering at every network edge, which has been the recommendation since 2000 and is still not universal. And open services on UDP — a connectionless protocol that answers strangers with more data than it received. If you run any UDP service, the question to ask is whether it responds to unauthenticated queries with amplification, and whether it needs to be reachable from the internet at all.

6.5Wireless

Wireless removes the physical constraint that makes layer 2 tolerable: an attacker no longer needs to be inside the building, only within radio range, and radio range is larger than most people assume.

Evil twin
A rogue access point broadcasting your SSID, often with a stronger signal, so clients associate with it. Combined with a captive portal it harvests credentials directly. The client-side defence is certificate validation on the network profile — with WPA2-Enterprise, a client configured to verify the RADIUS server's certificate will not authenticate to an impostor, and a client configured to "accept any certificate" will.
Deauthentication
Management frames in older standards are unauthenticated, so anyone can forcibly disconnect any client. Used as denial of service, and to force a reconnection so the four-way handshake can be captured for offline cracking. Management frame protection, mandatory in WPA3, closes it.
Handshake capture and offline cracking
With WPA2-PSK, capturing the handshake lets an attacker test passphrases offline at whatever rate their hardware allows, with no interaction and no lockout. Everyone shares one passphrase, so it cannot be rotated per person and one departing employee retains it. WPA3's SAE removes the offline attack; WPA2-Enterprise removes the shared secret.
Rogue access points
Often not malicious — someone plugs in a consumer access point for convenience, bridging your wired network to an unsecured wireless one. Wireless intrusion detection and 802.1X on wired ports both address it.
Karma and preferred-network attacks
Devices broadcast the names of networks they have previously joined. An attacker responds to whatever is asked for — including your corporate SSID, or a hotel network from last year — and the device connects automatically. Disabling auto-join for open networks, and removing saved networks, both help.
Why does TLS make most machine-in-the-middle attacks irrelevant, and when does it not?

Because TLS moves the trust decision from "who is relaying my packets" to "who can prove they hold the private key for this name", and the attacker in the middle cannot prove that. They can see and modify the ciphertext, but modification is detected by the record MAC and the content is unreadable, so the position is worth far less than it looks. That is why the answer to ARP poisoning is usually TLS rather than dynamic ARP inspection — one control fixes the whole class at every layer beneath it. Four cases where it does not hold. The client does not validate, which is overwhelmingly the most common real failure: an application that disabled certificate verification to work with a self-signed development certificate and shipped that way accepts any certificate the attacker presents, and there is no server-side fix. The first request is plaintext: a user typing a bare hostname sends an HTTP request that the attacker can intercept before any redirect, which HSTS mitigates and HSTS preloading removes entirely. Metadata still leaks: the attacker sees which hosts you contact, when, how often and how much data moves, and the SNI field exposes the hostname in the clear unless Encrypted Client Hello is in use — for some threat models that is the sensitive part. And a trusted certificate authority can be compelled or compromised, which is what certificate transparency logs exist to detect and what pinning defends against for high-value cases. So TLS with validation is the right answer and "we use HTTPS" is not the same statement.

Why can a 100 Mb/s connection be used to generate a much larger attack, and whose fault is it?

Through reflection with amplification, and the fault lies with two other parties. The attacker sends small UDP queries to publicly reachable servers, forging the victim's IP as the source address. Each server replies to the victim with a response much larger than the query — the amplification factor. For DNS ANY queries that is roughly 28 to 54 times; for NTP's monlist command it was around 550; for exposed memcached instances it reached the tens of thousands, which is how a modest connection produced some of the largest attacks ever recorded. So 100 Mb/s of queries becomes several gigabits or more arriving at the victim, from thousands of legitimate servers rather than from the attacker. Two enabling failures, neither of them the victim's. Source address spoofing works because most networks do not verify that outbound packets carry a source address belonging to that network — BCP 38 ingress filtering has been the recommendation since 2000 and remains incompletely deployed, and any provider that implements it removes their customers from the pool of possible attackers. Amplifying services are publicly reachable — a memcached instance on the internet with no authentication, an NTP server with monlist enabled, a DNS resolver that answers anyone. What the victim can actually do is limited and worth being clear about: you cannot filter traffic that has already saturated your link, so the only effective defence is upstream — a scrubbing provider or an anycast network that absorbs it before it reaches your capacity. Always-on beats on-demand, because the ten minutes it takes to detect and reroute is a complete outage.

LLMNR is enabled by default and nobody uses it deliberately. Why is that dangerous?

Because it turns every failed name lookup into an authentication attempt against whoever answers first. When a Windows host cannot resolve a name via DNS, it broadcasts the question to the local segment through LLMNR and NBT-NS, and any host on that segment can reply "that is me". The victim then connects and authenticates, sending a challenge-response the attacker can either crack offline or relay directly to a real service while it is still valid. The reason this fires constantly without anyone attacking is that failed lookups are routine — a typo in a share path, a mapped drive pointing at a decommissioned server, a printer that was replaced, a hardcoded hostname in an old script. Each one is an authentication handed to whoever is listening. Three properties make it particularly valuable to an attacker: it requires no exploit and no user interaction, it works from any host on the segment including an unmanaged one, and the credentials belong to whichever user happens to trigger it, which on a shared segment eventually includes an administrator. The fix is unusually clean: disable LLMNR and NBT-NS by group policy, which almost nothing legitimately needs, and enable SMB signing so that even a captured response cannot be relayed. Both are free, both are configuration changes, and together they remove one of the highest-yield internal techniques there is. It is worth checking rather than assuming — the defaults have historically been to leave them on.

Recap

  1. ARP, DHCP, DNS, BGP and NTP accept assertions from strangers. Nearly every network attack is that fact being used.
  2. Authenticating at a higher layer is the defence that scales. TLS with validation makes the relay position nearly worthless.
  3. Certificate validation failures are client bugs, and they are the most common real TLS failure.
  4. HSTS removes the plaintext first request; preloading removes it entirely.
  5. LLMNR and NBT-NS turn typos into captured credentials. Disable them and enable SMB signing.
  6. Denial of service comes in three kinds — bandwidth, state and application — and only the last is defensible on your own equipment.
  7. Anywhere a small request causes large work is a DoS surface, including your own password hashing.
  8. Amplification depends on spoofing plus open UDP services, both of which are other people's misconfigurations.
  9. 802.1X addresses the whole layer-2 category by keeping unauthorised devices off the segment.
  10. WPA2-PSK's handshake can be cracked offline, so the shared passphrase is the entire defence. Enterprise or WPA3 removes that.
