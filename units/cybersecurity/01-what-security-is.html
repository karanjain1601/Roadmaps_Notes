What security work is for

Security is not about making systems safe, because that is not achievable. It is about making the expected loss acceptable at a cost the organisation is willing to pay — which turns it into an engineering discipline with budgets and trade-offs rather than a moral one. This chapter is the vocabulary that makes those trade-offs discussable.

1.1The job

A system with no users, no network and no power is secure. Every step away from that adds capability and adds exposure, and the entire discipline lives in the trade between them. So the useful framing is not "is this secure?" — which has no answer — but:

The four questions that structure every security decision 
1  WHAT are we protecting, and what is it worth if lost?
2  WHO would want it, and what can they do?
3  HOW could they get it, given how the system is built?
4  WHAT does it cost to make that harder, and is that
   cheaper than the expected loss?

Question 4 is the one that separates security engineering
from security theatre. A control that costs more than the
loss it prevents is a net negative, and so is one that
makes the system unusable — because people route around
controls that get in their way, and the workaround is
almost always worse than the control was.
Security is a property of a system in a context

The same configuration can be appropriate for a personal blog and negligent for a hospital. There is no absolute scale. What makes a decision defensible is that you can state the threat it addresses, the asset it protects, and the cost of both the control and the loss. A recommendation without those three is a preference.

1.2The CIA triad, used precisely

Three properties. Most people can recite them and fewer use them as the analytical tool they are — the point is that a given control usually serves one and can actively damage another.

What each means, and what it costs the others
MeansViolated byTension
ConfidentialityOnly authorised parties can read itData breach, eavesdropping, over-broad access, a misconfigured bucketStrong encryption with lost keys destroys availability. Strict access control blocks legitimate work
IntegrityData is accurate and has not been altered without authorisationTampering, injection, a corrupted backup, a malicious insider editImmutability and approval workflows slow legitimate change
AvailabilityAuthorised parties can use it when they need toDenial of service, ransomware, a failed deployment, an over-aggressive block ruleEvery control that can say "no" can say it wrongly and cause an outage

The tension column is the important one, because availability is the property security teams damage most often. A blocking WAF rule, an expired certificate, an over-eager EDR agent quarantining a legitimate binary, a password policy that triggers mass lockouts — each is a security control causing exactly the outage an attacker would have wanted. Ranking the three for a given system, explicitly, is the first thing to do: for a trading platform integrity may outrank confidentiality; for a public status page availability dominates both.

Two properties are often added and are worth having. Authenticity — the thing is from who it claims to be, which integrity alone does not give you. And non-repudiation — the actor cannot credibly deny having done it, which requires signatures and logs the actor cannot alter.

1.3Risk

Risk is the unit of account. The standard formulation is deliberately simple:

Risk = likelihood × impact — and the terms underneath it 
THREAT          someone or something that could cause harm
                  (a ransomware crew, a careless admin, a flood)
VULNERABILITY   a weakness that could be exploited
                  (an unpatched CVE, a shared password, no backups)
ASSET           the thing with value
EXPOSURE        how much of the asset is reachable
CONTROL         something that reduces likelihood or impact

RISK exists only where a THREAT can reach a VULNERABILITY
that affects an ASSET. Remove any one and the risk is gone —
which is why "we have vulnerabilities" is not by itself a
finding, and why an unreachable critical CVE can rank below
a reachable medium one.

QUANTIFIED, for decisions with real money attached:
  SLE  single loss expectancy   = asset value × exposure factor
  ARO  annual rate of occurrence
  ALE  annualised loss expectancy = SLE × ARO

  A control is worth buying when its annual cost is less
  than the ALE reduction it produces. That comparison is
  the whole argument, and it is the one that gets budget.

Is this control worth buying?

The same arithmetic a risk register runs, on one scenario at a time.

The four responses to a risk

And all four are legitimate
ResponseMeansExample
MitigateReduce likelihood or impact with a controlPatch it, add MFA, segment the network, take backups
TransferMove the financial consequence elsewhereCyber insurance; a contractual liability cap with a supplier. Note this transfers the cost, never the reputational damage or the regulatory duty
AvoidStop doing the risky thingDo not collect the data; retire the legacy system; decline the feature
AcceptDecide the risk is tolerable and record that decisionA low-impact issue on an isolated system with a documented owner and a review date

Acceptance is a real answer and is constantly mistaken for negligence. The distinction is whether it was decided: a risk accepted by a named person, written down with the reasoning and a date to revisit, is management. The same risk unrecorded because nobody looked is not acceptance — it is ignorance wearing its clothes.

1.4Threat modelling

The structured version of question 3. You do it early, on a design, before anything is built — because the findings are architectural and architecture is what you cannot change later.

The four-question method, and STRIDE for the third 
1  WHAT ARE WE BUILDING?
   Draw it. Components, data stores, processes, external
   entities, and every place data crosses a TRUST BOUNDARY —
   a line where the level of trust changes. Most findings
   are on those lines.

2  WHAT CAN GO WRONG?
   Walk each element against STRIDE:

     Spoofing                pretending to be someone else
       ↔ counters Authentication
     Tampering               altering data or code
       ↔ counters Integrity
     Repudiation             denying an action was taken
       ↔ counters Non-repudiation
     Information disclosure  exposing data to the wrong party
       ↔ counters Confidentiality
     Denial of service       degrading availability
       ↔ counters Availability
     Elevation of privilege  gaining rights you should not have
       ↔ counters Authorisation

3  WHAT ARE WE GOING TO DO ABOUT IT?
   Each finding gets one of the four responses above, with
   an owner. A threat model that produces no decisions was
   a drawing exercise.

4  DID WE DO A GOOD JOB?
   Revisit when the design changes. A model of last year's
   architecture describes a system that no longer exists.

Two practical notes that decide whether this is useful or ritual. It must happen with the people who built it, because they know where the shortcuts are and an external reviewer does not. And the output must be a small number of prioritised, owned actions — a threat model producing sixty unranked findings gets filed and never read.

STRIDE over a trust boundary

Pick a component and see what applies. Note how the applicable threats change with what crosses the line.

1.5Defence in depth

No single control holds. Every one of them has a bypass, a misconfiguration and a day when it is turned off for troubleshooting. Defence in depth is the assumption that each layer will fail, arranged so that failures must coincide.

The arithmetic is what makes it worth doing. If one control stops 90% of attempts, ten percent get through. Four independent controls at 90% each leave 0.01% — but only if they are independent, and that qualifier does most of the work. Four firewalls from the same vendor sharing one management console are one control with four instances: the same vulnerability, the same misconfiguration, the same compromised administrator. Genuine depth requires layers that fail for different reasons.

The two failure modes of layering

Correlated failure: layers that share a dependency all fail together. Everything behind one VPN, everything trusting one identity provider, everything patched by one tool. Ask of every layer: what would have to be true for this and the next one to fail at the same time?

Diffused responsibility: with five layers, each team assumes another one is handling it. The application team relies on the WAF; the WAF team assumes input validation. Both are true and neither is done. Name the owner of each control and test it in isolation.

1.6Principles that generate the rest

Saltzer and Schroeder, mostly, and still the best list
PrincipleMeansIn practice
Least privilegeEvery subject gets the minimum rights needed, for the minimum timeThe highest-value principle in the list. Most breaches escalate through excess standing permissions

1.7Zero trust

The most abused term in the field, and it does mean something. The traditional model was perimeter-based: a hard boundary at the network edge, and broad trust inside it. That fails for three reasons that are all now normal — the perimeter is porous (cloud, SaaS, remote work, contractors), an attacker who gets inside moves freely, and an insider is inside by definition.

What actually changes
Perimeter modelZero trust
Trust derives fromNetwork location — "it came from inside"Verified identity of user and device, plus context
VerificationOnce, at the edgePer request, continuously
Default postureAllow inside, deny outsideDeny by default everywhere
Access grantedBroad, standingLeast privilege, time-bounded, per resource
Lateral movementEasy once insideEach hop is a new authorisation decision
EncryptionAt the edge; plaintext internallyEnd to end, including internal traffic

The one-line version: never trust, always verify — and "it is on our network" is not verification. What it is not is a product you can buy. It is an architectural stance that requires strong identity for people and machines, device posture checks, micro-segmentation, per-request authorisation and comprehensive logging — several years of work for most organisations, which is why the term appears on so many marketing pages and in so few implementations.

A note on the attack chapters

Part 2 explains attacks at the level needed to defend against and detect them: the mechanism, what it looks like in logs, and which control breaks it. That is the level a defender needs and it is deliberately not a set of operational instructions. If you want hands-on practice — and you should, because reading about it is a poor substitute — use deliberately vulnerable targets built for the purpose, covered in Chapter 20. Testing systems you do not own or have written authorisation to test is a criminal offence in most jurisdictions, regardless of intent.

A team proposes blocking all outbound traffic except an allow-list. How would you evaluate it?

Against the four questions, not against whether it sounds strict. What does it protect? It is a control against exfiltration and command-and-control — a compromised host cannot reach an arbitrary attacker-controlled server. That is a genuinely strong control, because it attacks a step every intrusion needs rather than a specific technique. What does it cost? Considerable, and mostly in availability: every legitimate outbound dependency must be enumerated and maintained, and the list will be wrong on day one and will drift constantly. Package installs, certificate revocation checks, software updates, telemetry, third-party APIs, and someone's build pipeline all break, and each break is an outage attributed to security. Is it proportionate? Depends entirely on the environment: for a segment of servers with a stable, small set of dependencies it is excellent and low-friction. For developer workstations it will be bypassed within a week, which is worse than not having it because it produces confidence without protection — psychological acceptability again. The productive answer is usually to scope it: strict allow-listing where the dependency set is stable and the asset is valuable, monitoring-only elsewhere, with a documented process for adding entries that takes minutes rather than a change board.

Why is an unreachable critical vulnerability sometimes lower priority than a reachable medium one?

Because risk requires a threat to be able to reach a vulnerability affecting an asset, and severity scores measure only the third part. A CVSS 9.8 on a library that is present in your image but never loaded, in a container with no network listener, on an isolated subnet, has no path — the likelihood term is close to zero, so the risk is close to zero regardless of the score. A CVSS 6.5 on an internet-facing service with a public exploit, being actively used by ransomware crews, has a likelihood near one. Multiply and the ordering reverses. This is why mature vulnerability management does not sort by CVSS: it combines severity with reachability (is the vulnerable code path actually executed?), exposure (who can reach the service?), exploit availability (is there working public code, and is it in the CISA Known Exploited list?) and asset value. Chapter 13 works through the scoring. The caveat worth keeping: "unreachable" is a claim about the current architecture, and architectures change without anyone re-checking the assumption — so unreachable means deprioritised, not closed.

Four security controls each stop 90% of attempts. Why might the combination not stop 99.99%?

Because that number assumes independence, and layered controls are frequently correlated in ways nobody has examined. Several kinds of correlation appear in practice. Shared technology: four network controls from one vendor share a codebase, so one vulnerability defeats all four. Shared configuration: everything managed from one console means one compromised administrator account, or one bad push, disables the lot. Shared dependency: if every control relies on the same identity provider, certificate authority or time source, that component's failure is a common-mode failure. Shared blind spot: four controls that all inspect network traffic are all defeated by an encrypted channel or a technique that never crosses the network — an insider with legitimate access defeats every one. And shared assumption: if all four assume the attacker comes from outside, none of them examine an authenticated internal actor. The practical test is to ask, for each pair of layers, what single event would defeat both — and if the answer exists and is plausible, they are one layer. Genuine depth requires controls that fail for different reasons: a network control plus a host control plus an application control plus an offline backup, owned by different teams, is real depth in a way that four firewalls is not.

Recap

  1. Security makes expected loss acceptable at an acceptable cost. A control costing more than the loss it prevents is a net negative.
  2. The CIA triad is an analytical tool, and availability is the property security teams damage most often.
  3. Risk needs a threat that can reach a vulnerability affecting an asset. Remove any one term and the risk is gone.
  4. ALE = SLE × ARO, and the control is worth buying when its annual cost is below the ALE reduction.
  5. Mitigate, transfer, avoid, accept — all four are legitimate, and acceptance means decided and recorded, not overlooked.
  6. Threat model early, on the design, with the people who built it, and produce a small number of owned actions.
  7. STRIDE maps one-to-one onto the properties each threat violates.
  8. Defence in depth only multiplies if the layers are independent. Ask what single event would defeat two of them.
  9. Least privilege and psychological acceptability are the two principles that pay most and get ignored most.
  10. Zero trust means location is not verification. It is an architecture, not a product.
