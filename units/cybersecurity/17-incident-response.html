Incident response

Everything before this chapter reduces the chance of an incident and none of it reaches zero. Response is what determines whether an intrusion becomes a bad week or a company-ending event, and most of the difference is decided by preparation done months earlier and by a handful of judgement calls made under pressure.

~30 min read Lifecycle · containment · comms · retrospectives 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapters 4, 15

17.1The lifecycle

Six phases. The first and last are the ones that get skipped
PhaseGoalWhere it goes wrong
1 PreparationPlan, roles, tooling, access, contacts, retainers, practiceThe phase that decides the outcome, and the only one you can do calmly. Skipped because nothing is happening
2 IdentificationEstablish that this is an incident, and its scope and severityDeclaring too late; or scoping from the first alert rather than hunting for the rest
3 ContainmentStop it spreading, and stop the damage growingActing too fast and destroying evidence, or too slow and watching it spread. §17.2
4 EradicationRemove the attacker's access — all of itCleaning one host while persistence remains on three others
5 RecoveryRestore service, with monitoring for returnRestoring from a backup taken after the compromise began
6 Lessons learnedChange something so this class of thing is harderHeld late, blame-shaped, or producing actions nobody owns
Preparation is most of the outcome

During an incident you will need things that take days to obtain and minutes to use: out-of-band communications that do not run on the compromised network, contact details for legal counsel and your insurer, a pre-agreed forensics retainer, administrative access that still works when the domain is untrusted, a decision-making structure with named authority to disconnect production, and current network and data-flow documentation. Every one of those is trivial to arrange in advance and close to impossible to arrange at 2am on a Sunday. The single most valuable preparation activity is a tabletop exercise — walking a realistic scenario with the actual decision-makers — because it reliably surfaces the things nobody had thought about, and it does so while there is time to fix them.

17.2The containment decision

The hardest judgement in the discipline, made early and with poor information. Isolating immediately stops the damage and tells the attacker they are detected; observing preserves the ability to find their full footprint and lets the damage continue while you look.

Isolate now, or watch?

The trade depends on what they are doing, how much you can see, and what they can still reach.

Containment options, from least to most disruptive 
NETWORK ISOLATION of a host
  EDR isolation keeps your management channel and cuts
  everything else. Preserves memory and the running state.
  The default first move for a single host.

BLOCK the C2 destination
  Cheap and quiet-ish. They may have a second channel, and
  a blocked beacon can trigger fallback behaviour.

DISABLE the compromised accounts
  Necessary, and it tells them immediately. Do it for ALL
  affected accounts at once, not one at a time.

REVOKE sessions and tokens
  Frequently forgotten, and disabling an account does not
  always invalidate an existing session or refresh token.

RESET krbtgt (twice), and privileged credentials
  Required if domain compromise is suspected — ch 10.
  Highly disruptive and non-optional in that case.

SEGMENT or disconnect a network
  Blunt, effective, and an outage.

FULL DISCONNECTION from the internet
  The nuclear option. Appropriate against active ransomware
  deployment, and almost never otherwise.

THE ORDERING PRINCIPLE
  Contain in a way that is REVERSIBLE and PRESERVES EVIDENCE
  where you can, and accept destroying both where the damage
  is accruing faster than the investigation can proceed.

AND: DO IT ALL AT ONCE
  Piecemeal containment is the classic failure. Remove one
  foothold and a competent attacker notices, activates
  persistence you have not found, and becomes quieter. Map
  first, then evict everything simultaneously.

17.3Eradication and recovery

The steps people miss
StepWhy it is missed

Three communication mistakes that cause lasting damage

Using the compromised channel. If an attacker is in the email system, your incident planning is being read, and your containment plan arrives before you execute it. Agree an out-of-band channel in advance and know how to reach everyone on it.

Stating scope too early. "Only twelve records were affected" said on day two and corrected to twelve thousand on day nine does more reputational harm than the breach. Say what you know, say what you are still establishing, and give a time for the next update.

Treating notification as optional until you are certain. Regulatory clocks generally start when you become aware of a breach, not when you finish investigating it, and the initial notification is allowed to be incomplete. Late notification is a separate penalty from the breach itself.

17.6The retrospective

Making the last phase the one that pays 
HOLD IT WITHIN TWO WEEKS, while memory is accurate and
before everyone has moved on.

BLAMELESS, and mean it. The person who clicked the link is
not the cause; the system that made one click sufficient is.
A retrospective that produces blame produces silence next
time, and silence is what turns an event into a breach.

BUILD THE TIMELINE FIRST, from evidence rather than memory.
Most insights come from the gaps: the alert that fired and
was closed, the four days between the first indicator and
anyone connecting it, the log that would have answered the
question and was not collected.

ASK, IN THIS ORDER
  1  How did they get in, and what would have stopped it?
  2  Why did detection take as long as it did?
  3  What slowed the response, and what would have helped?
  4  What did we not know that we needed to know?
  5  What surprised us?          ← this one is the richest

OUTPUT: A SMALL NUMBER OF OWNED ACTIONS WITH DATES
Three funded, owned, dated actions beat thirty
recommendations. Track them like any other work, and report
completion — an action list nobody revisits is the most
common reason the same incident happens twice.

AND SHARE IT. Internally always; externally where you can.
The industry gets better at this only when organisations
describe what actually happened to them.
You find one compromised host. Why is immediately reimaging it often the wrong move?

Because it destroys the evidence you need to find the rest, and one host is almost never the whole intrusion. Reimaging removes the memory contents, the process state, the network connections, the persistence mechanisms and the timeline artefacts that tell you how they got in, what credentials they took, which other systems they reached, and how long they have been there — and without those you cannot scope the incident. The likely result is that you clean one machine, feel finished, and leave the attacker in place on three others, which is the single most common way an incident recurs a fortnight later with the same actor. The better sequence is to isolate rather than destroy: EDR network isolation keeps your management channel while cutting everything else, so the host is neutralised and its state is preserved for analysis. Then capture volatile evidence in order of volatility (Chapter 18) — memory first, then the disk image — before doing anything irreversible. Then use what you learn to hunt for the rest of the footprint, and only when you believe you have mapped it do you evict everything at once. The important exception is when damage is accruing faster than the investigation can proceed: active ransomware deployment, live exfiltration of sensitive data, or an attacker who has clearly noticed you. In those cases stopping the harm outranks preserving evidence, and it is a legitimate call — but it should be a deliberate decision with someone's name on it, not the default reflex of a responder who reached for the fastest thing.

Why is piecemeal containment worse than waiting and containing everything at once?

Because it teaches the attacker that they are detected while leaving them the means to react, and a competent one reacts badly for you. Remove one foothold and they see it: the beacon stops checking in, the account stops working, the host goes quiet. Their response is to activate persistence you have not found, switch to a second command-and-control channel prepared for exactly this, move to credentials you have not identified, and become substantially quieter — so your remaining visibility drops at precisely the moment you need it most. In the ransomware case the reaction can be worse than quiet: an operator who realises they are being evicted may deploy immediately rather than lose the access they have spent weeks building, converting a containable intrusion into an encryption event. The alternative is to map first and evict simultaneously: identify every compromised host, account, credential and persistence mechanism while they still believe they are unobserved, then in one coordinated action disable all affected accounts, revoke all sessions and tokens, isolate all hosts, block all known channels and rotate the credentials they could have taken. Two practical qualifications. Mapping takes time you may not have, so this is a judgement about the rate of damage rather than a rule — and where you cannot afford the time, contain fast and accept a messier investigation. And "simultaneously" needs preparation: knowing in advance how to disable a hundred accounts in one action, how to isolate a set of hosts, and who has the authority to approve it, which is exactly what a tabletop exercise surfaces.

Your organisation restores from backup after ransomware and is encrypted again a week later. What happened?

Almost certainly one of three things, and possibly all of them. The backup contained the intrusion. Encryption is the last step of a campaign that typically ran for days or weeks beforehand, so a backup taken shortly before the encryption already contains the attacker's persistence — restoring it restores them too. The remedy is to restore from before the earliest evidence of compromise, which is why establishing the initial access date matters so much and why retention shorter than dwell time is so damaging (Chapter 15). The entry route was never closed. If they came in through an exposed remote desktop service, a VPN appliance missing a patch, or a set of valid credentials bought from a broker, then restoring the systems changes nothing about the door — and the same actor, who now knows the environment well, simply walks back in. Credentials were not rotated. They harvested domain credentials during the first intrusion, so every password, service account, API key and certificate they could have read must be treated as compromised, including the krbtgt key rotated twice if domain compromise is suspected (Chapter 10). Restoring systems while leaving the credentials valid hands them the keys back. There is also a fourth possibility worth checking: persistence somewhere the restore did not touch — firmware, a network device, a virtualisation host, a cloud identity, or a machine that was offline during the response and rejoined afterwards. The general lesson is that recovery is not eradication, and the order matters: establish the timeline, close the route, rotate everything, eradicate everywhere, and only then restore, with heightened monitoring specifically for their return.

Recap

  1. Preparation decides the outcome and is the only phase you can do calmly. Tabletop exercises surface what nobody thought of.
  2. Isolate rather than reimage — EDR isolation neutralises a host and preserves its state.
  3. Map first, then evict everything at once. Piecemeal containment makes a competent attacker quieter and faster.
  4. Rebuild rather than clean. You cannot prove a compromised host is clean.
  5. Restore from before the earliest evidence, not from the most recent backup.
  6. Rotate everything they could have read, and the list is always longer than the first draft.
  7. Eradication without closing the entry route is a delay.
  8. Coordinate out of band. If they are in your email, they are reading your plan.
  9. Regulatory clocks start at awareness, not certainty, and stating scope too early causes lasting harm.
  10. Retrospectives must be blameless and produce a few owned, dated actions.
