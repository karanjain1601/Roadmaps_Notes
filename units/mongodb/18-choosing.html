Choosing MongoDB

The chapter this unit was written to be able to end on. MongoDB is a good fit for some problems and a poor one for others, the difference is knowable in advance, and getting it wrong is expensive in a way that is hard to reverse.

fit the honest comparison signals closing

The actual question

Not "is MongoDB good" — it is a mature, well-engineered database with automatic failover, real transactions and a sound replication model. The question is narrower and answerable: does your data have a natural aggregate boundary that matches how you access it? If most operations load, modify and store one self-contained thing, the document model is a genuinely better fit than tables and joins. If your data is a graph of entities queried from many directions, it is a worse one, and no amount of skill with the aggregation pipeline changes that.

Where it genuinely wins

A natural aggregate
An order with its lines, a document with its revisions, a device reading with its metadata. One read, one atomic write, no join, and the invariant is enforced by the storage model rather than by a transaction.
Variable shape
Product catalogues across categories, event payloads by type, integrations with different providers. A relational schema handles this with sparse columns, an EAV table or a JSON column — all of which are worse than a document.
Rapid early iteration
Changing the shape is a code change. During the phase where the model changes weekly, that is a real velocity gain — and chapter 17 is about paying it back deliberately.
Horizontal write scaling
Sharding is built in and coherent. Doing the equivalent on a relational database means application-level partitioning you build and operate yourself.
Operational simplicity for HA
Elections, driver-side topology awareness and automatic failover are in the box. Compare with Patroni, etcd, HAProxy and a chapter of the PostgreSQL unit.
Change streams
A supported, resumable feed of every change, without deploying a CDC pipeline.

Where it genuinely loses

Highly relational data
Entities queried from many directions with no dominant aggregate. You will normalise, and then you are paying for joins that are weaker than SQL's with no foreign keys and no join reordering.
Ad-hoc analytical queries
The pipeline is capable and it is not SQL, the optimiser is far less sophisticated, and the tooling ecosystem is smaller. If unpredictable reporting is a core requirement, that is a strong signal.
Referential integrity that matters
There are no foreign keys. Orphans are prevented by application discipline, which is to say sometimes.
Complex multi-entity transactions
They exist and they cost more, especially across shards. If most operations need one, the model is fighting you.
Strong constraints
Unique indexes and JSON schema validation, and no CHECK constraints, no exclusion constraints, no deferrable constraints.
Existing SQL expertise and tooling
Not a technical argument and frequently the decisive one. Your team, your BI stack, your ORMs and your hiring pool all already speak SQL.
The strongest signal is your own schema

If the document schema you designed looks like a relational schema — a collection per entity, ids between them, $lookup wherever a join used to be — the fit is wrong, and it will not improve. You have taken on the costs of the document model (no foreign keys, weaker joins, expensive transactions, manual referential integrity) and none of its benefits (single-document atomicity, one-read access, no joins on the hot path). That is strictly worse than either alternative. The response is either to redo the modelling properly (chapters 2 and 3), or to accept that this data wants a relational database.

"Just use Postgres" is good advice and not a universal law

The advice is widely repeated and mostly sound: PostgreSQL is exceptional, jsonb covers a lot of what people reach for MongoDB to do, and one database you understand deeply beats two you understand partly. It is also frequently repeated by people describing MongoDB as it was a decade ago — before transactions, before w: "majority" defaults, before schema validation, before a sane bind address. The good version of the argument is about operational surface and team expertise, not about MongoDB being broken. If you would reach the same conclusion knowing everything in this unit, it is a sound conclusion. If it rests on "no transactions" or "loses data", it is out of date by about ten years.

Signals you chose wrong

  • Most queries use $lookup, or the application joins in code.
  • Most writes need a transaction.
  • You are writing referential integrity by hand, and finding orphans anyway.
  • Aggregation pipelines are long enough to need comments to be readable.
  • The same data is duplicated in five places with no clear owner for any copy.
  • Analysts cannot answer their own questions and every report is an engineering ticket.
  • Every collection has four document shapes and nobody knows which are live.

One or two of these are normal and fixable — usually by redoing the modelling in chapters 2 and 3. Most of them together is a fit problem rather than a skill problem, and continuing to work at it will not resolve it.

If you need to move

it is possible, and it is a project
Migrating away is not exotic and it is not cheap. The realistic sequence:

  1. Design the relational schema properly. Do not transliterate documents
     into tables — the embedding decisions were made for MongoDB's access
     model, and they should be revisited for a relational one
  2. Dual-write both databases, with the old one authoritative
  3. Backfill history, in batches
  4. Reconcile continuously until they agree, and keep the check running
  5. Switch reads over, one endpoint at a time
  6. Switch the authoritative writer
  7. Keep dual-writing until you are confident, then stop

The alternative shape, and often the better one: move the parts that fit
badly and keep the parts that fit well. A product catalogue with variable
attributes can stay in MongoDB while orders and ledger entries move to
PostgreSQL. Two databases is a real cost — chapter 10 of the PostgreSQL
unit's argument about operational surface applies — and it is sometimes
smaller than forcing one model onto data that resists it.

What is NOT a reasonable plan: keeping the schema as-is and hoping the next
version of MongoDB fixes it. The mismatch is architectural, not a missing
feature.

Where this unit ends

Eighteen chapters from two properties. The unit of atomicity is the document, and the schema lives in your application rather than the database. Almost everything else follows from those.

Document atomicity is why chapter 2's embedding decisions have correctness consequences and not just performance ones — an order and its line items in one document are consistent by construction, where the relational version needs a transaction. It is why chapter 12 spends more effort on avoiding transactions than on using them. It is why chapter 6 insists on operators rather than read-modify-write, and why a work queue needs no special machinery. And it is why the 16 MB limit is a design constraint rather than a technicality: the unit of atomicity is also the unit of transfer, so unbounded documents would mean unbounded write amplification.

The schema living in your application is why chapter 1 argues for validation on day one, why chapter 3's patterns all need a named owner and a reconciliation job, why chapter 17 exists at all, and why the flexibility that makes early development fast is a loan rather than a gift. The database stopped enforcing your assumptions; something else has to, and the only question is whether you chose what.

Three things are worth doing this week whatever else you take from this. Run the field and type histogram from chapter 17 on your largest collection, because whatever it shows will be informative. Check getDefaultRWConcern, because a cluster default of w: 1 set years ago is invisible from application code. And run a restore, end to end, and time it. Each takes under an hour, and each addresses something that is silent until it is not.

The short version

  1. The question is whether your data has a natural aggregate matching how you access it.
  2. If your document schema looks relational, the fit is wrong — you have the costs of both models and the benefits of neither.
  3. MongoDB wins on aggregate access, variable shape, built-in HA, sharding and change streams.
  4. It loses on ad-hoc relational queries, constraints, transaction cost, optimiser sophistication and ecosystem.
  5. "Just use Postgres" is good advice — when it rests on operational surface and team expertise, not on decade-old criticisms.
  6. Most writes needing a transaction is the clearest single signal of a bad fit.
  7. Moving part of the data is often better than moving all of it, or none.
  8. Everything in this unit follows from two things: document atomicity, and where the schema lives.
