MongoDB

MongoDB is usually explained by what it lacks — no schema, no joins, no transactions — and every one of those has been wrong for years. What it actually offers is a different unit of atomicity and a different place to put your schema, and nearly every design decision in this unit follows from those two.

How to use this

This unit assumes no MongoDB experience. It does assume you have seen a relational database — the SQL unit is the reference point it argues against, and having read it makes the comparisons land. Where MongoDB does something differently, this unit says what the relational answer was and why the document answer is or is not better.

Chapters 1–3 are the model, and they are the ones that matter most: a MongoDB deployment that goes wrong usually went wrong at the schema, months before anyone noticed. Chapters 4–6 are querying and writing. Chapters 7–9 are indexes and performance. Chapters 10–12 cover replication, the consistency dials and transactions. Chapters 13–17 are scale and operations, and chapter 18 is the honest account of when to use something else.

Demos run real logic — you can watch the aggregation pipeline transform documents stage by stage, see index selection change with the ESR rule, and price a shard key against the queries it has to serve. Examples target MongoDB 7 and note where behaviour changed, because a lot of the advice online describes MongoDB 3.

Two commitments

No advocacy

MongoDB is a good fit for some problems and a poor one for others, and the difference is knowable in advance. This unit says which is which — including chapter 18, which exists to argue the other side properly rather than as a disclaimer.

Current, not folklore

Much of what people repeat about MongoDB describes versions from a decade ago: no transactions, unsafe defaults, silent data loss. Those criticisms were once fair and mostly are not now. Where they still hold, this unit says so plainly.
