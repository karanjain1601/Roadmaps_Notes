Load balancing and quality of service

Two ways of dealing with more demand than one thing can serve: spread it across several, or decide whose traffic suffers. The first is load balancing and the second is quality of service, and both are exercises in choosing losers deliberately rather than by accident.

~28 min read Algorithms · health checks · queues · shaping 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapters 1, 9

15.1Distributing load

Algorithms under realistic conditions

Servers are rarely identical and requests are rarely the same size. That is where the algorithms diverge.

What each algorithm assumes, and where the assumption fails
AlgorithmAssumesFails when
Round robinServers are identical and requests cost the sameEither assumption is false, which is nearly always
Weighted round robinYou know the relative capacities in advanceCapacity changes, or the weights are never revisited
Least connectionsConnection count reflects loadConnections are long-lived and idle, so a server with many websockets looks busy while doing nothing
Least response timeMeasured latency reflects capacityCan oscillate — everyone piles onto the fastest server until it is not
Source hashYou need the same client on the same serverDistribution is uneven, and adding a server reshuffles everyone unless it is consistent hashing
Power of two choicesSample two at random, pick the less loadedRarely. Nearly as good as least-connections with none of the global state, which is why it is common at scale
The health check is the load balancer

Everything else is arithmetic; the health check is the part that decides whether a real failure is survived. Three properties matter and are routinely got wrong. It must test the thing that matters — a TCP connect check confirms a process is listening, which a server with a dead database will happily do while returning errors to everyone; check an endpoint that exercises the dependencies. It must be fast enough to matter and slow enough to be stable: a check every thirty seconds means half a minute of failed requests, while an aggressive one with a single failure threshold ejects servers during a garbage collection pause. And it needs protection against ejecting everything — if a shared dependency fails, every server fails its check simultaneously and a naive load balancer removes them all, converting a degraded service into an outage. That last one has a name and a setting on most platforms, and it is worth finding before you need it.

15.2Where the balancer sits

Layer 4 against layer 7, and the operational difference 
LAYER 4     forwards by address and port
  Fast, cheap, protocol-agnostic. Cannot see inside the
  connection, so it cannot route by URL, cannot retry a
  failed request on another server, and cannot terminate
  TLS. A connection is pinned to a server for its lifetime.

  Direct server return is possible: the reply goes straight
  to the client rather than back through the balancer, which
  removes it from the return path entirely. Useful at very
  high throughput and awkward to troubleshoot.

LAYER 7     terminates the connection and makes a new one
  Sees the request, so it can route by path or header,
  retry elsewhere on failure, rewrite, compress, cache and
  enforce policy. It also becomes a proxy, which means it
  holds state, needs capacity proportional to traffic, and
  is a place where things can be misconfigured.

  THE CONSEQUENCE PEOPLE MEET FIRST: the server now sees the
  balancer's address as the client. Every log, every rate
  limit and every geo decision breaks unless the original
  address is carried in a header and the server is
  configured to trust it — and to trust it ONLY from the
  balancer, or clients can forge it.

DIRECT-PATH AND DNS APPROACHES
  DNS "load balancing" hands out different addresses and
  inherits every caching problem in chapter 10 — it is a
  coarse distribution tool, not a failover mechanism.
  ANYCAST distributes by routing and is genuinely good for
  stateless traffic.                                → ch 10

WHERE TO PUT THE FAILURE DOMAIN
  A single load balancer in front of ten servers has moved
  the single point of failure rather than removed it. A pair
  with a shared virtual address, or an anycast set, is the
  minimum.                                          → ch 18

15.3Quality of service

Quality of service does not create bandwidth. It decides, in advance and deliberately, whose traffic is delayed or dropped when there is not enough — which is worth doing precisely because the alternative is deciding by accident.

A congested link, with and without a policy

Four traffic classes competing for a link that cannot carry them all.

The mechanisms, and which to reach for 
CLASSIFY then MARK, once, at the edge
  Identify traffic where you have the most context — as
  close to the source as possible — and write a DSCP value
  into the header. Everything downstream then acts on the
  marking rather than re-inspecting.

  AND DO NOT TRUST MARKINGS FROM OUTSIDE. A host can mark
  its own traffic as the highest priority, and some
  applications do exactly that. Re-mark at the trust
  boundary.

QUEUEING — what happens when packets arrive faster than
they leave
  PRIORITY QUEUE      always served first. Correct for
                      voice, and it must be POLICED, or a
                      misclassified bulk flow starves
                      everything else.
  WEIGHTED FAIR       each class gets a guaranteed share of
                      the remainder and may use more when
                      it is idle. The workhorse.
  FAIR QUEUEING       a queue per flow, so no single flow
                      monopolises. Excellent default where
                      you have no classification at all.

SHAPING against POLICING — the distinction that matters
  SHAPING   buffers excess traffic and sends it later.
            Smooths bursts, adds latency, and TCP responds
            well to it.
  POLICING  drops excess traffic immediately. No added
            latency, no buffer, and it is brutal to TCP —
            a policer that drops a burst causes a
            retransmission and a congestion backoff, so
            throughput can collapse well below the policed
            rate.
  → Shape your own egress. Expect providers to police your
    ingress, and shape slightly BELOW your contracted rate
    so that your queue, not theirs, is where the decision
    is made.                                        → ch 1

Markings do not survive the internet

A DSCP value is a request, honoured only by devices configured to honour it. Inside your own network that works. The moment traffic crosses to a provider, one of three things happens: the marking is ignored, it is rewritten to zero, or — on a purchased MPLS service with a class-of-service agreement — it is honoured according to a mapping you agreed in the contract. Only the third is a guarantee, and it is one of the few remaining arguments for buying MPLS. So quality of service is something you can implement end to end within a network you control, and something you can only request across one you do not. The practical consequence for a wide-area design: prioritise on your own egress where the congestion usually is, do not expect the far end of an internet path to respect anything, and if jitter must be bounded across a provider network, that has to be in the contract rather than in the configuration.

Why is least-connections often better than round robin, and when is it worse?

Round robin assumes every server is equally capable and every request costs the same, and both assumptions are usually false. Servers drift apart as hardware is replaced piecemeal, and request cost varies enormously — a cached page and a report generation differ by orders of magnitude. Round robin sends the same number of requests to each server regardless, so the slower machine accumulates work it cannot clear and its queue grows while a faster one idles. Least connections is self-correcting: a server that is slow holds its connections open longer, so its count stays high and the balancer naturally sends it less. It adapts to capacity differences and request-cost differences without anyone configuring weights, which is why it is a better default. It fails in one specific and common situation: when connections are long-lived and mostly idle. With websockets, server-sent events, or HTTP/2 where many requests share one connection, the connection count stops correlating with load — a server holding a thousand idle websockets looks maximally busy while doing nothing, so the balancer starves it and overloads the others. In that case use least response time, or count requests rather than connections if the platform supports it. Two related notes. Least response time adapts to real capacity and can oscillate, because everyone piles onto whichever server is currently fastest until it is not — damping or a decay factor matters. And power of two choices — sample two backends at random and send to the less loaded — gets most of the benefit of least-connections without maintaining global state, which is why it is common in large distributed balancers where perfect information is expensive.

Your health check passes but users get errors. What went wrong?

The check is testing the wrong thing — almost certainly something shallower than what the application actually needs to work. A TCP connect check confirms only that a process is listening on a port, which a server with a dead database connection, an exhausted thread pool, an expired certificate or a full disk will do perfectly happily while returning errors to every real request. An HTTP check on a static path is better and still passes when the application's dependencies are broken, because serving a fixed string requires none of them. The fix is a check that exercises the dependencies: an endpoint that verifies the database is reachable, the cache responds, and any critical downstream service is available, returning an unhealthy status if not. Two design cautions that follow immediately. Make the check cheap, because it runs constantly from every balancer and a check that queries the database on every invocation becomes a load source in its own right — cache the result for a few seconds. And be careful what it depends on, because a check that fails when a non-critical service is unavailable will eject healthy servers unnecessarily. The more dangerous failure is the opposite one: if the check depends on a shared resource, then when that resource fails every server fails its check at the same moment and a naive balancer removes the entire pool, turning a degraded service into a complete outage. Most platforms have a minimum-healthy threshold or panic mode that stops ejecting once too many members are down and reverts to distributing across everything — find it and configure it before you need it. And separate the two questions: a liveness check asks whether to restart this instance, and a readiness check asks whether to send it traffic; conflating them causes restart loops during a dependency outage.

Why shape your own traffic rather than let the provider police it?

Because shaping and policing handle excess traffic in completely different ways, and policing interacts badly with TCP. A shaper buffers traffic above the configured rate and releases it later, smoothing bursts into a steady stream; the cost is added latency for the buffered packets, and TCP handles it well because the packets arrive, just slightly late. A policer drops everything above the rate immediately, with no buffer; the cost is loss, and TCP treats loss as a congestion signal, so it halves its window and recovers slowly. The practical result is that a policer set at your contracted rate can deliver substantially less than that rate in useful throughput, because a burst causes drops, the drops cause backoff, and the sender spends its time recovering rather than transmitting. On a long path that effect is severe — the loss-based ceiling from Chapter 9 applies directly. Providers police customer ingress because it is cheap and stateless and protects their network, and they are not optimising your throughput. So the move is to shape your egress slightly below the contracted rate — typically 90 to 95 per cent — so that your own queue makes the decision about what waits, using a queueing discipline you chose, rather than the provider's policer making it by dropping. This is the same argument as bufferbloat in Chapter 1, from the other direction: you want the queue somewhere you control and can manage. It also makes quality of service actually work, because prioritisation is only meaningful at the point where the queue forms — if the congestion happens at the provider's policer, your careful class weights never come into play.

Recap

  1. Round robin assumes identical servers and identical requests. Both are usually false.
  2. Least connections self-corrects for capacity, and breaks with long-lived idle connections.
  3. Power of two choices gets most of the benefit without global state.
  4. The health check is the load balancer. Test the dependencies, not the port.
  5. Protect against ejecting the whole pool when a shared dependency fails.
  6. Layer 7 makes the balancer the client, so carry the original address and trust it only from the balancer.
  7. Quality of service allocates scarcity; it does not create bandwidth.
  8. Classify and mark once at the edge, and re-mark anything arriving from outside.
  9. A priority queue must be policed, or one misclassified flow starves everything.
  10. Shape your egress below the contracted rate so the queue forms where you control it.
