22.2Certifications, honestly

Is it worth the time?

The value varies enormously by career stage, and the honest answer is sometimes no.

What a certification actually signals

It is a filter, not a qualification, and knowing which makes the decision much clearer. Early on it is genuinely valuable because it gets a CV past screening when you have no experience to point at — that is a real problem and a certification is one of the few solutions available. Later it signals much less, because someone with six years of production experience is evaluated on what they have built and fixed, and a certificate adds little to that. The exceptions are worth knowing. Expert-level lab certifications remain respected because the practical exam is genuinely difficult and cannot be memorised; passing one demonstrates something real. Employer requirements around partner status make the calculation somebody else's rather than yours. And a certification in a domain you are moving into — cloud networking, for instance — functions like the early-career case again, because you have no experience to show in the new area. What certifications reliably do provide, regardless of the paper, is a structured syllabus. Studying for one forces you through the corners you would otherwise skip, and that is frequently worth the time even if the exam never happens.

Is network engineering a shrinking profession?

The work moved rather than disappeared, and the shape of it changed more than the volume. Three things are genuinely true. Fewer organisations run their own data centre networks, because the workloads moved — that is a real reduction in one category of role. Configuring devices one at a time is increasingly not a job on its own, since the same work is done from templates by fewer people. And the number of people needed to run a campus network of a given size has fallen, because the tooling improved. Set against that: cloud networking is networking. Virtual private clouds, subnets, route tables, peering, transit gateways, load balancers, private connectivity and hybrid routing are the same concepts from chapters 4 through 8 behind a different interface, and organisations discover they need someone who understands them at roughly the moment their cloud estate becomes non-trivial. Someone still connects everything — offices, factories, retail sites, industrial equipment and the increasingly large number of devices that are not laptops. And the difficult problems got harder: diagnosing a performance issue across a path you partly do not control, with encrypted transport headers, spanning your network and two providers and a cloud region, requires more skill than configuring a router ever did. What has genuinely changed is the expectation. A network engineer who only knows one vendor's command syntax is in a shrinking profession. One who understands the mechanisms, can automate, and can reason about a system spanning several administrative domains is in a growing one. That is the shift the last section is about, and it is why this unit spent its length on why things behave as they do rather than on how to configure them.

Should you learn to code?

Yes, and less than you might fear. The requirement is not software engineering — you are not being asked to build applications, and nobody expects a network engineer to write production software. What the role now assumes is the ability to read code and write scripts: enough to understand what a configuration template is doing, modify a playbook, parse output into something useful, call an API, and put configuration in version control. That is a genuinely modest amount of skill and it changes what you can do disproportionately, because it is the difference between operating a hundred devices individually and describing them once. The practical starting point is narrow. Python, because the networking library ecosystem is there and the language is forgiving. Git, because version-controlled configuration is the foundation of everything in Chapter 21 and it is also the thing that answers "what changed?" during an incident. YAML and a templating language, since that is what most network tooling actually consumes. And one configuration management tool in common use. That set is a few weeks of deliberate practice, not a career change. What not to worry about: you do not need computer science fundamentals, you do not need to be fast, and your code does not need to be elegant — a script that reliably collects configurations from every device and commits them is worth more than a beautiful one that does not exist. The honest framing is that coding is now a component of the job in the way that reading a routing table is, rather than a separate discipline you are being asked to adopt.

What should you do with a lab?

Break things in it, which is the part people skip. Building a working topology by following instructions teaches you the commands and very little else, because the diagnostic skill from Chapter 20 only develops against actual faults. So the exercise worth repeating is: build something that works, verify it, then introduce a fault deliberately — shut an interface, mismatch an MTU, change one IPsec phase 1 parameter, misconfigure a native VLAN, add a more specific route — walk away, and come back and find it without consulting what you did. That loop is the entire skill, and a lab is the only place you can practise it without an audience. A few things make a lab much more valuable. Run a packet capture during every exercise, because seeing real OSPF hellos, a BGP open message or an IKE exchange converts a description into something you recognise instantly in production. Time things yourself rather than trusting the figures in this unit — break a link and measure the reconvergence, then tune the timers and measure it again; the numbers stick when you produced them. Reproduce faults you have actually met at work, since a lab is where you can safely understand the thing that beat you last month. And automate the lab itself, which gets you the Chapter 21 skills for free and makes rebuilding cheap enough that you experiment more. On tooling: containerised network operating systems give you a full topology on a laptop defined in a file, and it is the format worth starting with. But the best lab is the one you actually begin — a three-router topology tonight beats a rack you plan for six months.

Closing

The through-line of these twenty-two chapters is that networks are physical systems obeying rules you can reason about. A packet takes the time it takes because of distance and the speed of light in glass; a queue overflows because arrivals exceeded departures; a loop is catastrophic because Ethernet has no hop count; a flow is capped at fifteen megabits because loss and round-trip time say so. None of that is arbitrary, and none of it depends on whose logo is on the equipment.

That is why the chapters spent their length on mechanisms rather than commands. Command syntax is the least durable thing in the field and the most heavily examined; the reasoning is the opposite on both counts. An engineer who knows why a spanning tree blocks a port can work out the command on an unfamiliar platform in a minute, and one who only knows the command cannot do anything else.

The two habits worth carrying out of here are cheap and rarely practised. Ask what changed before opening any tool, because it resolves more faults than any capture. And bisect rather than searching, because halving the space every test is what turns three hours into ten minutes. Everything else is detail you can look up.

Go and build the three-router lab. Break it on purpose.
