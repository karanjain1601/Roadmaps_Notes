The wire and the frame

The bottom two layers, where most faults actually live. Choosing a medium, how Ethernet delimits a frame, what a MAC address is and is not, and why the error counters on an interface diagnose more problems than any other single source.

3.1Choosing a medium

What will carry this link?

Distance is usually the binding constraint, and it eliminates options faster than anything else.

The distinctions that matter in practice 
COPPER — twisted pair
  100 m is the standard limit and it includes patch leads at
  both ends, not just the run in the ceiling. Susceptible to
  electrical interference; carries power (PoE); cheap to
  terminate; every device has a port for it.
  Cat5e  1 Gbps   ·  Cat6  10 Gbps to 55 m
  Cat6a  10 Gbps to 100 m  ·  Cat8  short data-centre runs

FIBRE — multimode
  Cheap optics, short reach. Sizes quoted as OM3/OM4/OM5;
  the higher the number the further it goes at a given rate.
  Orange or aqua jackets. Data centre and campus distances.
  10 Gbps: OM3 ~300 m, OM4 ~400 m
  Note the rate/distance TRADE — the same fibre carries a
  slower signal much further.

FIBRE — singlemode
  Expensive optics, enormous reach. Yellow jacket. Tens of
  kilometres unamplified, and the same glass supports every
  future rate you are likely to need.
  → For anything between buildings, singlemode is usually
    correct even where multimode would technically reach,
    because the CIVIL WORK dwarfs the fibre cost and you
    will not want to dig again.

DIRECT ATTACH COPPER (DAC)
  A fixed cable with the transceivers moulded on. Cheapest
  option inside a rack, up to a few metres, very low power.
  Not a field-terminated cable — you buy the length you need.

RADIO
  A shared medium with no cable to fault. Everything about
  it is different enough to need its own chapter.     ch 13

AND THE ONE PEOPLE FORGET
  The transceiver must match the fibre AND the far end.
  A singlemode optic on multimode fibre may link and then
  behave badly, which is a memorably annoying fault.

FLAT, NOT HIERARCHICAL
  There is no "MAC subnet" and no way to summarise them.
  A switch must learn every address it needs to reach,
  individually, which is why layer 2 does not scale and
  why we have layer 3 at all.

TWO BITS IN THE FIRST BYTE MEAN SOMETHING
  · least significant bit of byte 0 = 1 → MULTICAST
    ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff is the broadcast address; a switch
    floods it to every port in the VLAN.
  · second-least significant bit = 1 → LOCALLY ADMINISTERED
    Not from a manufacturer. Virtual machines, containers
    and randomised device addresses use this range.

"BURNED IN" IS NO LONGER TRUE
  Any host can set its MAC address in one command, and
  modern phones and laptops RANDOMISE it per network by
  default for privacy. So a MAC address is not an identity
  and not an authentication factor — MAC filtering as a
  security control was always weak and is now close to
  meaningless.                                        ch 16

WHERE IT IS STILL USEFUL
  The OUI tells you the manufacturer, which is genuinely
  handy when you find an unexpected device and want to know
  whether it is a printer or a hypervisor.

3.3Collision and broadcast domains

The single change that made modern networks possible, and the reason hubs disappeared.

Hub against switch

Same cable, same speed, completely different behaviour once more than two devices talk.

A switch splits collision domains; only a router splits broadcast domains

This is the sentence that explains layer 2 scaling limits. Each switch port is its own collision domain, so with full duplex there are no collisions at all and every port gets the full link rate. But a broadcast — ARP, DHCP discovery, service announcements — is flooded to every port in the VLAN, so all those ports remain one broadcast domain no matter how many switches you chain together. Every host must receive, interrupt on, and process every broadcast. That is why VLANs exist (they cut one physical switch into several broadcast domains) and why the practical ceiling on a flat layer-2 segment is a few hundred hosts rather than a few thousand. When someone says a network "doesn't scale at layer 2", this is the mechanism they mean.

3.4Duplex, autonegotiation and the counters

Duplex mismatch: the fault that looks like everything else

If one end is set to full duplex and the other autonegotiates, the autonegotiating end falls back to half duplex — and the link comes up. It works. Small transfers succeed, ping looks fine, and under any real load the throughput collapses to a few percent of the link rate with heavy retransmission. It presents as "the network is slow", which sends people looking at bandwidth, routing and applications for hours. The signature is unmistakable once you know it: late collisions on the half-duplex end, CRC errors on the full-duplex end. The cure is to leave autonegotiation on at both ends — hard-setting one side is what causes it, and the practice survives from a period when negotiation was genuinely unreliable and is now almost always a mistake. Check duplex before you check anything else when a link is inexplicably slow.

Why did switches replace hubs so completely?

Because a hub makes every device share one collision domain, and that arrangement degrades super-linearly with the number of devices. A hub is an electrical repeater: a signal arriving on one port is regenerated out of every other port, so only one device may transmit at a time and the whole segment operates in half duplex with CSMA/CD arbitrating access. When two devices transmit simultaneously the frames collide, both back off for a random interval and retry, and as offered load rises the collision rate rises with it — so throughput peaks somewhere well below the nominal rate and then falls as more traffic is offered, because an increasing share of the wire is occupied by collisions and backoff rather than by data. Twelve moderately busy devices on a shared hub can spend more of the medium on collisions than on frames. A switch changes the structure rather than the speed: it maintains a table of which MAC address lives on which port and forwards each frame only to the port that needs it, so each port is its own collision domain. With full duplex there is no arbitration at all — both directions can transmit simultaneously and collisions cease to exist as a concept. Aggregate capacity becomes the sum of the ports rather than a single shared channel. Two qualifications worth keeping. The switch does not help with broadcasts, which are still flooded to every port in the VLAN, so broadcast domain limits remain and are what VLANs and routers address. And a switch has finite forwarding capacity and buffers, so oversubscription simply moves the contention from the wire into the queue — which is where Chapter 1's queueing arithmetic takes over.

A link is slow but shows no errors in the traffic graphs. What do you check first?

Duplex and speed on both ends, then the interface error counters — and specifically the counters rather than the bandwidth graphs, because the two show different things and the graphs will look unremarkable during exactly this fault. A duplex mismatch is the classic cause: one end hard-set to full duplex while the other autonegotiates and therefore falls back to half. The link comes up, small transfers work, and throughput collapses under load with heavy retransmission, so the complaint arrives as "the network is slow" and sends people to look at applications, routing and capacity. The diagnostic signature is precise: late collisions on the half-duplex end and CRC errors on the full-duplex end, both visible in one command on each device. Late collisions in particular are nearly diagnostic on their own, since on a correctly configured modern link they should be zero forever. Assuming duplex is fine, work down the same counter list. CRC errors without late collisions point at the physical layer — a damaged cable, a dirty or failing optic, interference, or a run exceeding 100 metres including the patch leads at both ends, which people routinely forget to count. Input drops suggest bursts overrunning a receive buffer, which is a congestion story rather than a fault. Interface resets and flaps indicate a marginal connection or a failing transceiver. Two habits make this fast: always check counters at both ends, because the two ends see different symptoms of the same fault, and always clear the counters and re-observe, since large historical totals may date from an event years ago and tell you nothing about now.

Why is MAC filtering not a security control?

Because a MAC address is a self-asserted value that any host can change in a single command, and the network has no way to verify it. Whatever address a device puts in the source field of a frame is what the switch learns and what a filter compares against, so an attacker who wants to appear as a permitted device simply needs to observe one — and observing one is trivial, since MAC addresses travel in clear text in every frame on the segment and are visible to anyone who can capture, plug into a port, or associate with the wireless network. So the control amounts to requiring an attacker to run one command after listening for a moment, which is not a meaningful barrier. Two further points make it worse rather than better. Modern operating systems randomise MAC addresses per network by default for privacy, so filtering now generates a steady stream of legitimate devices being denied, which produces an administrative burden and trains people to add exceptions — and an allow-list maintained under that pressure decays quickly. And maintaining the list at all scales badly, because there is no hierarchy in MAC addressing to summarise: every device is an individual entry, forever. What to use instead is 802.1X, which authenticates the device or user cryptographically before the port is opened, optionally assigns a VLAN based on identity, and can be paired with certificates so the credential cannot simply be observed off the wire. That is a genuine control and it is covered in Chapter 16. MAC filtering retains a small legitimate role as an operational guardrail — catching accidental misconnections rather than deliberate ones — and it should never be described as security.

Recap

  1. The 100 m copper limit includes the patch leads, which is where the extra metres hide.
  2. Between buildings, use singlemode — the civil work costs far more than the fibre and you will not dig twice.
  3. Fibre trades rate against distance: the same glass carries a slower signal much further.
  4. MAC addresses are flat, which is precisely why layer 2 does not scale and layer 3 exists.
  5. A switch splits collision domains; only a router splits broadcast domains.
  6. Broadcasts are processed by every host in the VLAN, which caps a flat segment at a few hundred hosts.
  7. Frames under 46 bytes are padded, so tiny packets waste the wire twice over.
  8. Late collisions mean duplex mismatch, essentially always.
  9. Leave autonegotiation on at both ends. Hard-setting one side is what causes the mismatch.
  10. Check counters at both ends and clear them first — historical totals tell you nothing about now.
