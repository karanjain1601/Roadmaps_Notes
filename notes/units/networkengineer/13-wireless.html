Wireless

Everything so far assumed a wire with two ends and exclusive use of it. Radio has neither: the medium is shared with every other transmitter in earshot, including ones you do not control, and almost every wireless complaint traces back to that single difference.

~30 min read Airtime · channels · standards · roaming 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapter 3

13.1Airtime is the resource

The single most useful shift in thinking: stop measuring wireless in megabits and start measuring it in time on air. Only one device in a cell may transmit at a time, so the medium is a serialised resource, and a slow client occupies it for far longer to move the same data.

One slow client

Five devices want the same amount of data. Watch what the slowest one does to the others.

Why a distant client is everyone's problem

A client at the edge of the cell negotiates a low data rate, because low rates use simpler modulation that survives a poor signal. That is the correct behaviour and it means every frame to or from that client occupies the channel for a long time — a device at 6 Mbps takes a hundred times longer than one at 600 Mbps to move the same bytes. Without airtime fairness the access point serves clients packet by packet, so the slow one consumes almost all the available time and everyone in the cell is dragged down toward its rate. Three implications follow. Enable airtime fairness, which gives each client equal time rather than equal turns. Set a minimum data rate so that clients too far away to be served efficiently are pushed to associate with a nearer access point instead. And disable the ancient low rates entirely — leaving 1 and 2 Mbps enabled means management frames are also sent at those rates, which wastes airtime for every client in the cell.

13.2Channels and interference

Channel width against cell count

Channels × width is the band. Widening helps until you run out of non-overlapping channels, and not after.

THE RULE THAT MATTERS MORE THAN ANY OF THIS
  Two access points on the SAME channel within earshot of
  each other share that channel's airtime, even if no client
  is roaming between them. Co-channel interference is not
  corruption — it is politeness. Each AP defers to the other
  because it can hear it, so the airtime is halved.

  That is why adding access points to fix a slow network
  frequently makes it slower.

AND THE COROLLARY ABOUT WIDTH
  Channels multiplied by width is a constant — it is the
  band. So once every channel already has more than one AP
  on it, choosing a wider channel does not add capacity; it
  redistributes the same spectrum into fewer, faster,
  more-contended cells. Widen until you run out of
  non-overlapping channels for your AP count, and stop.

Two things worth holding onto against the marketing. Headline rates are aggregate across all spatial streams under laboratory conditions, and a real client with two antennas at a realistic distance sees a fraction of them — expect roughly 40 to 60 per cent of the negotiated rate as actual throughput after protocol overhead. And a network is only as capable as its clients: deploying Wi-Fi 6 access points to serve a fleet of Wi-Fi 5 laptops delivers the density improvements to nobody, because OFDMA needs client support at both ends.

13.4Designing coverage

The things that decide whether it works 
DESIGN FOR CAPACITY, NOT COVERAGE
  The old question was "can I hear the AP everywhere?" The
  modern one is "how many clients, doing what, in each
  area?" A lecture theatre and a warehouse of the same size
  need completely different designs, and only the second is
  a coverage problem.

SIGNAL STRENGTH TARGETS
  −67 dBm or better for voice and video
  −70 dBm for general data
  A client typically transmits with far less power than the
  access point, so it can HEAR the AP long before the AP can
  hear it. Design for the client's uplink, not the downlink
  bars on your phone.

SIGNAL TO NOISE RATIO MATTERS MORE THAN SIGNAL
  −65 dBm with a −90 noise floor is excellent. The same
  −65 with a −70 floor is unusable. Any survey that reports
  only signal strength is telling you half the story.

CELL OVERLAP
  Aim for around 15–20% overlap on the coverage contour —
  enough that a client always has somewhere to roam to,
  little enough to limit co-channel interference.

TURN THE POWER DOWN
  The instinct is to raise transmit power to improve
  coverage. It usually makes things worse: it enlarges the
  cell, so more clients associate with one AP, more distant
  clients negotiate low rates, and co-channel interference
  with neighbouring APs increases. It also worsens the
  asymmetry above, since the client's power did not change.

BAND STEERING AND MINIMUM RATES
  Push dual-band clients to 5 or 6 GHz, and refuse
  association below a minimum rate so distant clients move
  on rather than camping.

CHANNEL PLANNING IS NOT OPTIONAL
  Automatic channel selection is better than nothing and it
  reacts to conditions it can measure — it cannot know that
  the neighbouring tenant will install twelve APs next month.

Users complain the Wi-Fi is slow. You add more access points and it gets worse. Why?

Because you added transmitters to a shared medium and did not add spectrum. Two access points within earshot of each other on the same channel do not each get a full channel — they share it, because the carrier-sense mechanism makes each defer while it can hear the other transmitting. That is co-channel interference, and the important thing to understand is that it is not corruption or noise; it is the protocol working correctly, and the cost is that the available airtime in that area is divided between them. Add a third access point on the same channel and each gets roughly a third. So in a band with only three non-overlapping channels — which is exactly the situation in 2.4 GHz — a dozen access points guarantee heavy co-channel overlap and the network gets slower with every one you add. The same happens in 5 GHz if you have chosen wide channels, since 80 MHz channels leave only six non-overlapping cells and 160 MHz leaves two. What to do instead. Establish whether the problem is coverage or capacity, because they have opposite remedies — if clients have good signal and poor throughput, more access points is the wrong direction. Reduce channel width to increase the number of non-overlapping cells, which trades peak speed for capacity and is usually the right trade in a dense space. Lower transmit power, which shrinks each cell so fewer access points hear each other and clients associate with the nearest one. Disable 2.4 GHz radios on most access points, keeping a few for coverage and legacy devices. And check airtime rather than throughput in the monitoring, since airtime utilisation above roughly 50 per cent is where a cell starts to feel slow regardless of what the bandwidth graphs say.

Why does one old device slow down an entire wireless cell?

Because the medium is serialised in time, and a slow client occupies far more of it for the same data. A device negotiating 6 Mbps takes a hundred times longer to send a megabyte than one at 600 Mbps — and while it is transmitting, nobody else in the cell can. Without airtime fairness the access point serves clients packet by packet, which sounds equitable and is not: equal packets means wildly unequal time, so the slow client ends up consuming the overwhelming majority of the airtime. Run the numbers and it is stark — a single 6 Mbps client among four gigabit clients takes over 96 per cent of the available time, and every fast client is dragged down from around 150 Mbps to under 6. The slow client is not necessarily old, incidentally; a modern phone at the far edge of the cell negotiates a low rate for the same reason, so this is as much a coverage problem as a legacy one. Three fixes, in order of effect. Airtime fairness allocates equal time rather than equal turns, so fast clients keep their throughput and the slow one simply takes longer to finish — this is the direct remedy and it is a checkbox on most controllers. Minimum data rates refuse association below a threshold, so a client too distant to be served efficiently is pushed to a nearer access point rather than camping on this one; setting the minimum around 12 to 24 Mbps is common. And disabling the legacy rates matters more than it sounds, because beacons and other management frames are transmitted at the lowest enabled rate so that every client can hear them — leaving 1 Mbps enabled means every beacon, from every access point, wastes airtime for everybody.

Should you use the widest channels your equipment supports?

Only where you have spectrum to spare, which usually means a small number of access points or the 6 GHz band. Channel bonding gives each client more throughput by using more spectrum at once — an 80 MHz channel is roughly four times the capacity of a 20 MHz one for a single transmission — and it takes that spectrum out of circulation for everyone else. The arithmetic is unforgiving: in 5 GHz you might have twenty-five non-overlapping channels at 20 MHz, twelve at 40, six at 80 and two at 160. So a deployment with a dozen access points in earshot of each other can be planned cleanly at 20 or 40 MHz and cannot avoid heavy co-channel interference at 80. Since co-channel overlap halves or thirds the airtime available in the overlapping area, the wide channel that promised four times the speed frequently delivers less than the narrow one would have. The rule that follows is sharper than "narrow is better": widen until you run out of non-overlapping channels for your access point count, and no further. Channels multiplied by width is a constant — it is the size of the band — so beyond that point a wider channel does not create capacity, it just packs the same spectrum into fewer and more contended cells. Run the panel at twenty-five access points and every width converges on the same per-client figure, which is the clearest way to see that the band, not the channel setting, is the ceiling. Three qualifications. In a house or a small office with two or three access points there is plenty of spectrum and wide channels are the right answer. In 6 GHz there is enough room that 80 or even 160 MHz remains viable at moderate density, which is one of that band's genuine advantages. And the decision interacts with DFS: excluding radar-affected channels can halve your usable 5 GHz spectrum, so a plan that works with DFS may not work without it. The measurement that settles the argument is airtime utilisation rather than throughput — if it is high, you need more cells, and more cells need narrower channels.

Recap

  1. Measure wireless in airtime, not megabits. Only one device transmits at a time.
  2. One slow client can consume most of a cell's airtime and drag everyone toward its rate.
  3. Enable airtime fairness, set minimum rates, disable legacy rates. Beacons use the lowest enabled rate.
  4. 2.4 GHz has three non-overlapping channels, and that has never changed. Use it for coverage only.
  5. Co-channel interference is politeness, not corruption — two APs in earshot share the airtime.
  6. Adding access points without adding spectrum makes things slower.
  7. Widen only until you run out of non-overlapping channels. Channels × width is the band, so past that it just redistributes.
  8. Turn transmit power down. Raising it enlarges cells and worsens the uplink asymmetry.
  9. Signal-to-noise ratio matters more than signal strength.
  10. The client decides when to roam, which is why sticky clients cannot be fully fixed from the infrastructure.
