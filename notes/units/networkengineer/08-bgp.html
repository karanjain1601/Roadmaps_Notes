BGP and the internet

Every protocol so far tries to find the shortest path. BGP does not — it finds the path that policy permits, because the internet is not one network but seventy thousand businesses with commercial relationships, and the cheapest path frequently is not the shortest.

~30 min read Autonomous systems · path selection · peering · security 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapters 6–7

THE iBGP RULE THAT CATCHES EVERYONE
  A router does not re-advertise an iBGP-learned route to
  another iBGP peer. That is a loop-prevention rule — there
  is no AS path change internally to detect a loop with — and
  it means iBGP needs a FULL MESH: every speaker peered with
  every other, which is n(n−1)/2 sessions.

  Two ways out, and you will meet both:
    ROUTE REFLECTORS  designate a few routers that ARE
                      allowed to re-advertise. Everyone
                      peers with them instead.
    CONFEDERATIONS    split the AS into sub-ASes. Rarer.

NLRI, ATTRIBUTES, AND WHY IT IS A "PATH VECTOR"
  BGP advertises a prefix plus attributes, the most
  important being the AS_PATH — the list of autonomous
  systems the advertisement has crossed. A router that sees
  its own AS in the path discards the route. That is the
  entire loop-prevention mechanism, and it is why BGP does
  not need to know the topology.

8.2Path selection

When several routes to the same prefix exist, BGP works down a fixed list of tie-breakers and stops at the first one that discriminates. Knowing the order is what lets you influence traffic deliberately rather than by accident.

Which path wins, and at which step

Two routes to the same prefix. The process stops at the first attribute that differs.

Local preference controls what leaves; AS path prepending begs about what arrives

The asymmetry that governs all traffic engineering. Outbound traffic is entirely yours to decide — local preference is set on your own routers, is compared early, and is never sent outside your AS, so you have complete authority over which link your traffic leaves by. Inbound traffic is decided by everyone else, and the only lever you have is making one path look worse in the hope that other people's routers agree: prepending your own AS number several times to lengthen the path, advertising more specific prefixes on the link you prefer, or asking a provider to apply a community that adjusts their local preference. None of these is binding — a network several hops away may have local preference set for commercial reasons that overrides your path length entirely. Plan on this: you control your outbound traffic and you influence your inbound traffic, and any design that requires precise inbound control is going to disappoint.

8.3Peering and transit

The economics that shape the routing

Why the path a packet takes is a commercial decision before it is a technical one.

The relationships, and the rule that follows from them 
TRANSIT      you pay them to reach everywhere.
             They advertise the full table to you.
             You advertise only your own prefixes to them.

PEERING      you and they exchange traffic between YOUR
             customers and THEIRS, usually settlement-free.
             Each advertises only its own and its customers'
             prefixes — not the full table.
             Cheaper and shorter than going via transit,
             which is why exchanges exist.

CUSTOMER     they pay you. You advertise everything to them
             and their prefixes to everyone.

THE VALLEY-FREE RULE
  Traffic should go UP to a provider, ACROSS a peer, then
  DOWN to a customer — never up-across-up. Concretely:

    routes learned from   are advertised to
    ───────────────────   ─────────────────────────────
    a customer            everyone
    a peer                customers only
    a provider            customers only

  Advertise a route learned from one provider to another
  provider and you have told the internet you will carry
  traffic between them for free. That is a ROUTE LEAK, and
  it is how a small network accidentally becomes transit for
  a large one and immediately melts.

  This is not a subtle policy. It is three filter rules, and
  the outages caused by omitting them are famous.

A more specific prefix wins, and that is the entire hijack mechanism

The longest-prefix-match rule from Chapter 6 applies globally and without regard to who is entitled to announce what. If you advertise 203.0.113.0/22 and someone else announces 203.0.113.0/24 — accidentally or deliberately — then every router on the internet that accepts their announcement prefers it for that quarter of your space, because it is more specific. There is no authority in the protocol adjudicating ownership; BGP was designed among parties who trusted each other. Three practical defences. RPKI route origin validation lets you cryptographically state which AS may originate your prefixes and lets others reject announcements that disagree, and adoption is now high enough among large networks to matter. Announce your prefixes at the most specific length you will ever need, since an attacker cannot beat a /24 with a /24 and most networks will not accept anything longer. And monitor your own address space from an external looking-glass service, because you will not see the hijack from inside your own network — the traffic simply stops arriving.

8.5MPLS, briefly

What it is and why it persists 
A label is pushed onto the packet at the edge of the
provider network; every router inside forwards on the LABEL
rather than looking up the IP destination; the label is
popped at the far edge.

WHY IT WAS INVENTED
  Label lookup was much faster than longest-prefix match in
  the 1990s. That reason is long gone — hardware does prefix
  lookup at line rate now.

WHY IT SURVIVED, WHICH IS THE ACTUAL REASON
  · TRAFFIC ENGINEERING. A label-switched path can be
    pinned to a route the IGP would not have chosen, with
    reserved bandwidth. You cannot do that with plain IP.
  · VPNs. Several customers can use overlapping private
    address space across one provider backbone, because
    forwarding is by label and each customer has its own
    routing table on the edge router. This is what an
    "MPLS VPN" you buy actually is.
  · FAST REROUTE. A precomputed backup path can be switched
    to in tens of milliseconds, well below IGP convergence.

WHAT YOU MEET AS A CUSTOMER
  A managed WAN service, where you run BGP or a static route
  to the provider edge and their backbone is opaque to you.
  The interesting decisions are about the service you buy,
  not the labels.                                     → ch 14
Why can you control outbound traffic precisely but only influence inbound?

Because the decision about which path a packet takes is made by whoever is forwarding it, and for inbound traffic that is not you. Outbound is straightforward: your own routers hold several routes to a destination and choose between them, and local preference is the second step in the selection process and is set locally on your equipment. It never leaves your AS, so nobody else's policy interferes, and you can direct all outbound traffic to a preferred provider with one attribute. That is genuine control. Inbound traffic is chosen by every network between the source and you, each running its own selection process with its own commercial priorities, and none of them is obliged to care what you want. The levers you have are all indirect. AS path prepending adds your AS number several times to make one path look longer, and it only works if the receiving networks get as far as the AS-path step — which they will not if they have local preference set for their own reasons, and a network two hops away frequently does. Advertising more specific prefixes on the link you prefer is much more effective, because longest prefix match is evaluated before any policy attribute, but it costs you global table slots and some networks filter prefixes longer than /24. Communities that your provider publishes let you ask them to adjust their local preference, which is the most reliable option because it acts inside a network that has agreed to listen. The design consequence is worth stating plainly: build for outbound control, treat inbound as an approximation, and never design something whose correctness depends on inbound traffic arriving on a specific link.

What is a route leak, and why is it so damaging?

Re-advertising a route beyond the relationship it was learned under — most commonly taking routes learned from one provider or peer and announcing them to another provider. The damage is a matter of scale. When a small network advertises a large network's routes to a transit provider, it has effectively announced "I will carry traffic to these destinations", and traffic engineered for tens or hundreds of gigabits begins arriving at a network sized for a fraction of that. It saturates instantly, so the leaking network goes down, and everyone whose traffic was redirected through it loses connectivity too — which is why individual leaks have caused globally visible outages affecting large parts of the internet for hours. The rule that prevents it is valley-free routing, and it is genuinely simple: routes learned from a customer may be advertised to everyone, while routes learned from a peer or a provider may be advertised only to customers. Three filter rules per neighbour. The reason leaks keep happening despite that is not complexity but operational failure — a session brought up in a hurry without the export policy, a filter that permitted everything as a temporary measure, a configuration template applied to the wrong neighbour type. Defences beyond the export filter are worth layering because that filter is the thing that gets forgotten: a maximum-prefix limit on every session shuts it down automatically when a neighbour starts sending far more than expected, which converts a leak into a dropped session; and the newer peer role signalling lets two routers agree their relationship in the protocol itself and mark routes that would violate it, which catches the case where a human got the filter wrong.

When is connecting to an internet exchange worth it?

When the traffic you can move off transit and onto settlement-free peering saves more than the port, cross-connect and facility costs — and the crossover is usually lower than people expect, because transit is charged per megabit while an exchange port is a fixed monthly cost that does not care how much you push through it. Run the panel above: with a few gigabits of traffic and a well-populated exchange, the payback is often immediate. The financial argument is not the only one, though, and sometimes not the main one. Latency improves, frequently substantially, because peering removes hops and keeps traffic local rather than sending it via a transit provider's aggregation point, which may be in another city or country. Resilience improves, since you gain paths that do not depend on a single transit relationship. And the traffic profile matters more than the total: if a large share of your traffic goes to a handful of content networks and they are present at your local exchange, a small number of peering sessions can move most of your volume, whereas if your traffic is thinly spread across the whole internet the same port moves far less. What makes it not worth it: very low traffic volumes where the port cost dominates; no exchange with useful participants nearby, since cross-connect and transport to a distant facility can cost more than the transit you save; and lacking the operational capability to run BGP properly, because a peering session configured without the export filters from §8.3 is how you leak routes. You also still need transit — peering reaches peers and their customers, not the whole internet — so this is an optimisation on top of transit rather than a replacement for it.

Recap

  1. BGP finds the policy-permitted path, not the shortest one. It has no concept of cost.
  2. Loop prevention is the AS path — a router that sees its own AS discards the route.
  3. iBGP does not re-advertise to iBGP, which is why you need a full mesh or route reflectors.
  4. Selection stops at the first attribute that differs. Know the order or you influence by accident.
  5. You control outbound with local preference; you only influence inbound.
  6. Valley-free is three filter rules per neighbour, and omitting them is how famous outages happen.
  7. A more specific prefix wins globally, which is the entire hijack mechanism.
  8. Announce at the most specific length you will need, and monitor your space externally.
  9. Put a maximum-prefix limit on every session. It turns a router failure into a link failure.
  10. MPLS survived for VPNs, traffic engineering and fast reroute, not for the lookup speed it was invented for.
