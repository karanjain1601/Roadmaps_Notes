The services underneath

Nobody puts DHCP, NTP or syslog on an architecture diagram. Each one is invisible until it fails, at which point it is responsible for everything — and the failures do not look like what they are, which is why they belong in a chapter of their own.

~28 min read DHCP · NTP · syslog · IPAM 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapters 4, 10

11.1DHCP

The exchange, and the part that catches people 
DISCOVER   client broadcasts — it has no address yet
OFFER      server proposes an address
REQUEST    client broadcasts its acceptance, so that any
           other server that also offered can withdraw
ACK        server confirms, with the lease and the options

RENEWAL happens quietly at half the lease time (T1), unicast
to the server that granted it. At 87.5% (T2) the client
starts broadcasting to reach any server. Only at 100% does
it give up the address.

  So a DHCP server can be down for HALF the lease time
  before any existing client notices. That is a genuinely
  useful property and it is also why an outage is discovered
  late and by new arrivals rather than by monitoring.

THE RELAY, WHICH IS THE PART THAT BREAKS
  DISCOVER is a broadcast, and a router does not forward
  broadcasts. So a server on a different subnet needs a
  RELAY AGENT — the router itself — configured to forward
  the request as unicast, stamping in which subnet it came
  from so the server picks the right pool.

  Missing relay = clients on that VLAN get nothing, while
  every other VLAN works perfectly. It is the single most
  common DHCP fault, and it appears whenever a new VLAN is
  created by someone who forgot the one extra line.

OPTIONS worth knowing
  1  subnet mask        3  default gateway
  6  DNS servers        15 domain name
  42 NTP servers        66/67 boot server and filename
  43/60 vendor specific — how phones and access points find
        their controller. Frequently the reason a device
        "does not come up on the new VLAN".

Pool sizing

The lease time, not the device count, is usually what exhausts a pool.

A lease outlives the visit, and that is what fills the pool

The intuition is that a pool needs as many addresses as there are devices present. It needs as many as there are unexpired leases, and a device that leaves does not hand its address back — it simply stops renewing, and the address stays allocated until the lease expires. So for any network where devices come and go faster than the lease time, the number of addresses required is roughly the arrival rate multiplied by the lease duration rather than the visit duration. A guest network seeing two hundred devices an hour who stay two hours needs four hundred addresses with a two-hour lease and sixteen hundred with an eight-hour one, for exactly the same population. The rule that follows: match the lease to the expected dwell time. Short leases for guest and wireless, long leases for desks and servers where the device is present all week and short leases would only generate churn.

Designing it so it does not surprise you 
STRATUM is distance from a reference clock, not quality.
  0  the reference itself — GPS receiver, atomic clock
  1  a server directly attached to one
  2  a server synchronised to a stratum 1
  ...and so on. Each hop adds a little error.

WHAT A SENSIBLE DESIGN LOOKS LIKE
  · Two or three internal servers, synchronised upstream to
    several external sources, serving everything internally.
  · Every device points at the internal servers, not at the
    internet — so you have one place to fix, one egress rule
    and consistent time even if the internet is unreachable.
  · An ODD number of upstream sources, at least three or
    four. With two, a disagreement is unresolvable and NTP
    will refuse to trust either.

THE ONE THAT CATCHES PEOPLE
  A device with a large offset may REFUSE to step its clock
  and just log the discrepancy indefinitely, on the grounds
  that a huge jump could be wrong. So a machine that has
  been off for months comes back with a badly wrong clock
  and never fixes itself, while quietly reporting that NTP
  is configured. Check actual offset, not configuration.

  Also: authentication with wrong time fails in a way that
  looks like a credential problem, so the diagnosis goes to
  the wrong team. When several unrelated services start
  rejecting logins at once, check the clock before anything.

PRECISION TIME PROTOCOL is the other one you may meet —
sub-microsecond, hardware timestamped in the switches, used
where finance or industrial control needs it. Different
problem, much more infrastructure.

11.3What actually breaks, and when

A service fails. What happens, and how soon?

The delay before the symptom appears is what makes these hard to attribute.

The delayed-onset failure is the hard one

A DNS resolver dying produces complaints in seconds, so it is easy to attribute. DHCP dying produces nothing for hours — existing clients renew successfully until half their lease elapses, and only new arrivals fail. So the outage begins in the morning, the symptoms appear at lunchtime as leases roll over, and by the time anyone connects the two the change that caused it is well out of the recent-changes window. NTP is worse: the clock drifts slowly and the failure surfaces days later as authentication problems, which get raised against the identity team. The lesson is not to monitor harder in general but to monitor these services directly and synthetically — obtain a real lease from a probe on each VLAN, resolve a known name through each resolver, compare each device's actual clock offset against a reference. Every one of those catches the fault at the moment it occurs rather than when the consequences surface.

A new VLAN was created and clients on it get no address, while every other VLAN works. What is it?

A missing DHCP relay on the router interface for that VLAN, in nearly every case. The mechanism is worth holding precisely: a client with no address cannot send a unicast packet, so DISCOVER is a broadcast — and routers do not forward broadcasts, which is the whole point of a broadcast domain. So unless the DHCP server sits on that VLAN itself, something must catch the broadcast and forward it. That something is the relay agent, configured on the router's interface in that subnet, which converts the broadcast into a unicast to the server and stamps the request with which subnet it came from so the server selects the correct pool. Create a new VLAN, configure the address and the gateway, and forget the one extra relay line, and you get exactly this symptom: perfect connectivity for anything statically addressed, nothing at all for DHCP clients, and every other VLAN unaffected. Three things to check in order. Confirm the relay is configured on the router interface for that VLAN and points at the right server addresses. Confirm the server has a scope defined for that subnet — the relay stamps the subnet in, and a server with no matching scope simply does not answer, which looks identical from the client. And confirm the return path, since the server replies to the relay address and needs a route back. If all three are right, look for an access list between the relay and the server, and remember to check both directions. A quick confirmation that saves time: a client showing a 169.254 link-local address has definitively received no response, which rules out the server having answered with something wrong.

Why does the lease time affect pool sizing more than the number of devices?

Because an address is occupied for as long as its lease has not expired, not for as long as the device is present — and there is no mechanism by which a device leaving returns the address. A laptop that associates with the guest network, stays two hours and walks out does not send a release; it simply stops renewing, and the server keeps that address reserved for the rest of the lease. So the number of addresses you need is the arrival rate multiplied by the hold time, which is the longer of the visit duration and the lease duration. Run the arithmetic on a realistic guest network: two hundred devices arriving per hour, staying two hours, needs four hundred addresses with a two-hour lease — and sixteen hundred with an eight-hour lease, for an identical population. Four times the addresses, purely from the lease setting. That is why guest networks exhaust their pools while showing only a few hundred devices connected, a symptom that looks inexplicable until you count leases rather than clients. The rule is to match the lease to the expected dwell time: thirty minutes to two hours for guest and public wireless, eight hours or a day for staff wireless where people are in all day, and days for wired desks and servers where the device never moves and short leases would generate pointless renewal traffic. Two caveats. Very short leases increase renewal load and make the DHCP server a more critical dependency, since clients renew at half the lease. And if a pool is already exhausted, shortening the lease does not free anything immediately — existing leases still run to their original expiry.

Several unrelated services start rejecting logins at once. Where do you look?

At the clock, before anything else. Time skew produces authentication failures across systems that share nothing except a dependency on time being roughly correct, and because the symptom is "credentials rejected" the reports go to the identity or application teams rather than to the network. The thresholds are worth knowing because they explain the pattern of what fails first. Around thirty seconds of skew, time-based one-time passwords stop matching, so multi-factor prompts start failing while passwords still work. At five minutes, Kerberos rejects tickets outright — that is the default clock-skew tolerance, and it means domain logins, file shares and anything using integrated authentication all stop together, which is the classic presentation. Further out, TLS certificates appear not-yet-valid or expired, so connections fail with certificate errors that make no sense because the certificate is genuinely fine. The tell is the combination: several unrelated systems, all failing at authentication rather than connectivity, with no common change. Two operational points make this less likely and easier to catch. Monitor actual measured offset on every device rather than whether NTP is configured, because a machine with a large offset may refuse to step its clock at all and will sit there logging the discrepancy while reporting that time sync is enabled — configuration looks perfect and the clock is hours out. And run internal NTP servers that everything points at, synchronised upstream to several external sources, so there is one place to check and time stays consistent even when the internet is unreachable. Use an odd number of upstream sources, at least three, because two disagreeing sources cannot be adjudicated and NTP will trust neither.

Recap

  1. A DHCP server can be down for half the lease time before anyone notices, which is why the outage is found late.
  2. A missing relay on a new VLAN is the most common DHCP fault, and it looks like nothing else is wrong.
  3. Pool size follows the lease time, not the device count. The lease outlives the visit.
  4. Match the lease to the dwell time — short for guest, long for desks.
  5. Kerberos fails at five minutes of skew, and the reports go to the wrong team.
  6. Monitor measured clock offset, not NTP configuration — a device with a large offset may refuse to correct itself.
  7. Use an odd number of upstream time sources, at least three, or a disagreement is unresolvable.
  8. Ship logs off the device. The reboot that lost the buffer is the event you needed.
  9. UTC timestamps with the source name, or correlation during an incident is impossible.
  10. Generate the address inventory from reality and reconcile against the plan. That gap is the useful report.
