Unsupervised learning

No labels, no loss to minimise against ground truth, and no test-set accuracy to report. Unsupervised methods find structure — and the hard part is not running them but deciding whether what came back is real.

— words— min read3 interactivesNeeds: chapters 09, 10

The difficulty, stated up front

In supervised learning you have an unambiguous referee: held-out accuracy. Here you do not. Any clustering algorithm will return clusters — including on uniform random noise. The algorithm cannot tell you whether they mean anything.

What that changes
Validation becomes a matter of argument rather than measurement. You will lean on stability across resamples, on external variables the clustering never saw, and on whether a domain expert recognises the groups.
What it does not change
Preprocessing still matters enormously — arguably more, since every method here is distance- or density-based and therefore sensitive to scale.

k-means

The most used clustering algorithm, and the one whose assumptions are most often forgotten. It minimises the total squared distance from each point to its cluster's centre:

$$J = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\mathbf{x} \in C_k} \|\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\|^2$$

This quantity is called the inertia or within-cluster sum of squares. Minimising it exactly is NP-hard, so Lloyd's algorithm alternates two easy steps and settles for a local optimum.

  1. Initialise K centroids — with k-means++, which spreads the initial picks out rather than choosing at random.
  2. Assign every point to its nearest centroid.
  3. Update each centroid to the mean of the points assigned to it.
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until assignments stop changing. Inertia decreases at every step, so it always converges — just not necessarily to the best answer.

Choosing K

Elbow method
Plot inertia against K and look for the bend. Inertia always falls as K rises — at K = n it is zero — so the shape is what matters, not the value. Often ambiguous.
Silhouette score
For each point, compare its average distance to its own cluster against the nearest other cluster. Ranges from −1 to 1; pick the K that maximises the mean. More decisive than the elbow.
Gap statistic
Compare your inertia to what you would get on uniform random data. Principled, slower, and it can legitimately tell you K = 1.
Domain constraint
Marketing can act on four segments, not forty. Frequently the real answer, and there is nothing wrong with that.
$$s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max\{a(i),\, b(i)\}}$$

a(i) is the mean distance from point i to others in its own cluster; b(i) is the mean distance to the nearest other cluster. Near 1 means well placed, near 0 means on a boundary, negative means it is probably in the wrong cluster.

The alternatives

Hierarchical clustering

Rather than committing to K, build the whole nested family of clusterings. Agglomerative clustering starts with every point as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the closest pair; cutting the resulting tree at a chosen height gives you any K you like.

DBSCAN

Density-based: a cluster is a region where points are packed closely, separated by sparse regions. Two parameters — eps, the neighbourhood radius, and min_samples, how many neighbours make a point "core".

Core point
Has at least min_samples neighbours within eps. Clusters grow outward from these.
Border point
Within eps of a core point but not itself dense. Joins that cluster.
Noise point
Neither. Left unassigned — which is DBSCAN's most useful property, since no other method here admits that some points belong nowhere.

The consequences: DBSCAN finds arbitrarily shaped clusters, does not need K, and handles noise natively. It struggles when clusters have very different densities, since one eps cannot suit both, and it degrades in high dimensions along with every other distance-based method. HDBSCAN removes the fixed eps and is usually the better modern choice.

Gaussian mixture models

Model the data as a weighted sum of K Gaussian distributions and fit by expectation–maximisation. The difference from k-means is that assignments are soft — each point gets a probability of belonging to each cluster — and clusters can be elongated and tilted, because each component has its own covariance matrix.

$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)$$

k-means is in fact a special case: fix every covariance to a small multiple of the identity and force assignments to be hard, and EM reduces to Lloyd's algorithm. Because a GMM is a genuine probability model, you can score new points, sample from it, and use BIC or AIC to choose K in a principled way.

The two checks that actually persuade people

Stability. Cluster ten bootstrap resamples. If the same structure appears each time, you have found something; if the assignments scramble, you have found sampling noise. Cheap, and far more convincing than any internal index.

Profiling on held-out variables. Build segments from behavioural features, then check whether the segments differ on revenue, retention or support contacts — variables the clustering never touched. Differences there mean the structure is real and actionable. Similar profiles mean you have partitioned noise, however good the silhouette looked.

Anomaly detection

Finding the rows that do not belong. It looks like classification with a very rare positive class, and it is not — usually you have no labelled anomalies at all, and the anomalies of next month will not resemble this month's.

The evaluation problem

With no labels you cannot compute precision. What you can do: hold out any known incidents and check they are flagged; measure how many alerts per day the method produces and whether a team can actually process that many; and track what fraction of investigated alerts turn out to be real, which gives you a precision estimate over time. Chapter 4's base-rate arithmetic applies in full — on a 0.1% anomaly rate, even a very specific detector produces mostly false alarms.

Association rules, briefly

"Customers who bought X also bought Y." Rules are scored by support (how often the combination occurs), confidence (how often Y follows X), and lift (how much more often than chance). Lift is the one that matters — high confidence on a popular item is unremarkable, since Y is bought with everything. The Apriori and FP-Growth algorithms find these efficiently, and the main practical difficulty is that a large catalogue produces far more statistically valid rules than anyone can use.

Self-check

  1. You run k-means twice and get different clusters. Is something broken?
    No — the objective is non-convex, so Lloyd's algorithm converges to a local optimum determined by the initialisation. That is why scikit-learn's n_init defaults to running it several times and keeping the lowest-inertia result, and why k-means++ initialisation exists. If results still vary a lot with n_init=10, that is evidence the cluster structure is weak rather than a bug.
  2. Your elbow plot has no clear elbow. What next?
    Look at silhouette scores across K, which are usually more decisive. Consider the gap statistic, which can legitimately conclude K = 1 — no cluster structure. And consider that the data may genuinely be one continuous cloud, in which case the right answer is not to cluster it: use a continuous score or a supervised model instead of manufacturing segments that do not exist.
  3. k-means fails badly on two-moons data. Which algorithm would you try, and why?
    DBSCAN or HDBSCAN. k-means partitions space by proximity to centroids, which always yields convex, roughly spherical regions — a crescent cannot be one of them. DBSCAN grows clusters along connected dense regions, so it follows curved shapes naturally, and it will additionally mark the sparse points between the moons as noise instead of forcing them into a cluster. Spectral clustering is another option, which works by clustering in an eigenvector space rather than the original one.
  4. Why does a GMM handle elongated clusters when k-means cannot?
    Each Gaussian component has its own covariance matrix, so it can be stretched and rotated to match an elongated cloud. k-means implicitly assumes spherical clusters of equal size because it assigns by plain Euclidean distance to a centre — geometrically, its boundaries are always the perpendicular bisectors between centroids. A GMM with covariance_type="full" has the extra parameters to describe shape as well as position.
  5. You have found four customer segments with a silhouette of 0.62. How do you convince a sceptical stakeholder?
    Not with the silhouette, which they will not interpret. Two things work. Stability: re-cluster on bootstrap resamples and show the same groups appear — adjusted Rand index above roughly 0.7 across resamples is persuasive. And profiling on variables the model never saw: if the segments differ markedly in revenue, churn rate or support load, the structure is real and, more importantly, actionable. A segment nobody can do anything differently for is not worth having, whatever its silhouette.
  6. Why is anomaly detection harder to evaluate than rare-class classification?
    Rare-class classification still has labels, so precision and recall are computable. Anomaly detection typically has none, and the anomalies you care about are by definition ones you have not seen before — so even a labelled historical set does not represent the future. You are left with proxy evidence: known incidents that should be caught, alert volume against team capacity, and a slowly accumulating precision estimate from investigated alerts.
