How learning works

Every algorithm in the next ten chapters is a different answer to the same question: how do you pick a rule from a space of possible rules, using a finite and noisy sample, such that it works on data you have not seen? This chapter is the theory that makes the rest make sense.

Learning, formally

Three objects and one problem.

The hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\)
The set of rules your algorithm is allowed to return. All straight lines. All trees of depth ≤ 6. All networks with this architecture. Choosing \(\mathcal{H}\) is your first and most consequential decision, because the algorithm can never return anything outside it.
The loss \(L\)
What a wrong prediction costs, one example at a time.
The data
n samples drawn from some unknown distribution \(P(\mathbf{x}, y)\) — the "true" data-generating process you never observe directly.

What you actually want to minimise is the true risk: expected loss over the whole distribution, including every example you will ever see in production.

$$R(f) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y) \sim P}\big[L(y, f(\mathbf{x}))\big]$$

You cannot compute it — you do not have \(P\). So you minimise the empirical risk instead: the average loss on the sample you do have. This substitution is called empirical risk minimisation, and it is what every training loop is doing.

$$\hat{R}(f) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} L\big(y^{(i)}, f(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\big)$$

The entire difficulty of machine learning lives in the gap between those two quantities:

$$\underbrace{R(f)}_{\text{what you want}} = \underbrace{\hat{R}(f)}_{\text{what you measure}} + \underbrace{\big[R(f) - \hat{R}(f)\big]}_{\text{generalisation gap}}$$

Drive the training loss to zero and the first term vanishes, which tells you nothing — the gap may have grown to swallow the difference. A model that memorises its training set has \(\hat{R} = 0\) and useless \(R\). Learning is the business of keeping both terms small at once, and the tension between them is the subject of this chapter.

The floor you cannot go below

Even a perfect model has non-zero error, because the world is not deterministic. Two identical feature vectors can carry different labels — the same symptoms, different diagnoses; the same weather, different outcomes. This irreducible floor is the Bayes error.

$$R(f) = \underbrace{R^{*}}_{\text{Bayes error}} + \underbrace{\big[R(f) - R^{*}\big]}_{\text{what you can fix}}$$

You never know \(R^{*}\) exactly, but you can estimate it: measure how well an expert human does, or how often two annotators disagree (chapter 7). If humans reach 96% and your model reaches 94%, the remaining headroom is two points, not six. Teams that skip this estimate spend months chasing error that was never available.

Why generative models are having a moment

A discriminative model only learns the boundary between classes, which is all you need to classify and nothing more. A generative model learns how the data itself is distributed, which is harder but lets you sample new examples, handle missing inputs, and detect out-of-distribution data. For pure classification, discriminative usually wins with enough data. Chapters 19 and 20 cover what happens when the generative objective is applied at scale.

Bias and variance

The central decomposition of the field. For squared error, the expected test error at a point splits into exactly three pieces — and you can only trade the first two against each other.

$$\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}))^2\big] \;=\; \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x})] - f(\mathbf{x})\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x})])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}$$

The expectation is over different training sets drawn from the same distribution. f is the true function; f̂ is the model you fit from one particular sample.

Bias
How far the average model — averaged over many possible training sets — sits from the truth. High bias means your hypothesis space cannot represent the real relationship. Fitting a straight line to a curve gives high bias no matter how much data you collect.
Variance
How much the fitted model swings as the training sample changes. High variance means you are fitting the noise: a different sample gives a very different model.
Noise
The Bayes error. Irreducible, and not your fault.

A useful mental image

Bias is a systematically miscalibrated instrument: every measurement is off in the same direction, and repeating the experiment does not help. Variance is a jittery instrument: each measurement is off in a different direction, so averaging many of them does help. That difference is exactly why ensembling works — random forests average many high-variance trees and the errors cancel, which chapter 12 makes concrete.

Two things the classical picture misses

Double descent

The textbook U-curve says test error falls, bottoms out, then rises as capacity grows. For very large models a second phenomenon appears: past the point where the model can exactly interpolate the training data, test error falls again, sometimes below its earlier minimum.

The interpolation threshold — where parameters roughly equal training examples — is the worst place to be. Below it the classical trade-off holds; above it, among the infinitely many parameter settings that fit the data perfectly, gradient descent tends to find one with small norm, which generalises well. This is why enormous over-parameterised networks work at all, contradicting decades of received wisdom about capacity control.

Do not over-apply this

Double descent is real and well documented for large neural networks on large datasets. It is not a licence to abandon regularisation on a 2,000-row tabular problem, where the classical U-curve holds firmly and a gradient boosting model with sensible depth limits will beat an over-parameterised network comfortably.

No free lunch, and inductive bias

Averaged over all possible problems, every algorithm performs identically — including random guessing. That sounds nihilistic but it is not, because real problems are not drawn uniformly from all possible problems. They have structure: smoothness, locality, hierarchy, sparsity. An algorithm works when its assumptions match that structure.

Those assumptions are its inductive bias, and knowing them is how you choose a model rather than trying all of them:

The practical reading of no free lunch

There is no best algorithm, but there are strong priors. For tabular data with a few thousand to a few million rows, gradient boosting wins most of the time — its inductive bias matches the structure of tabular problems. For images, convolutional architectures. For text and sequences, transformers. Start with the family that matches your data's structure, establish the baseline, and only then explore.

Self-check

  1. Training accuracy 99%, validation accuracy 71%. Name three fixes, in the order you would try them.
    Classic high variance. (1) Get more training data if it is available — the most reliable fix and the one that costs no modelling judgement. (2) Reduce capacity or add regularisation: shallower trees, stronger L2, fewer features, early stopping. (3) Ensemble or bag, which averages away variance. Before any of that, though, confirm the gap is not caused by a distribution difference between the two splits, or by leakage inflating the training number.
  2. Training accuracy 68%, validation accuracy 67%. Is this overfitting?
    No — the two agree closely, so variance is low. This is underfitting, or you have hit the Bayes error. Increase capacity, add better features, or train longer, and see whether training accuracy moves. If it will not budge no matter what you do, the signal may simply not be in your features, and the next step is data collection rather than modelling.
  3. Why does averaging many decision trees help, when averaging many linear regressions would not?
    Deep trees are high-variance and low-bias: each one fits its sample closely and differently, so their errors are largely independent and averaging cancels them. Linear regressions on the same data are high-bias and low-variance — they all produce nearly the same fit, so averaging changes almost nothing. Ensembling attacks variance, so it pays off precisely where variance is the problem.
  4. Your learning curve shows train and validation error both flat at 0.30 from n = 200 onward. Should you collect more data?
    No. Converged curves at a high error level mean bias, not variance — more of the same data will land on the same plateau. Spend the effort on better features, a more expressive model, or on establishing whether 0.30 is near the Bayes error, in which case the problem is as solved as it can be.
  5. What is the inductive bias of k-NN, and what does it imply about preprocessing?
    That points close together in feature space share labels, and — crucially — that all features contribute equally to "close". This implies two things: you must scale features, or whichever has the largest units dominates the distance; and you must remove irrelevant features, because each one adds noise to every distance calculation. It is also why k-NN degrades so sharply in high dimensions, as chapter 9's demo showed.
  6. A colleague says their 50-million-parameter network trained on 5,000 rows "cannot overfit because of double descent". Respond.
    Double descent describes what happens well past the interpolation threshold on problems where the phenomenon has been demonstrated — typically large models on large datasets with implicit regularisation from the optimiser. With 5,000 rows the model will interpolate trivially and there is no reason to expect the second descent to rescue it. Test it empirically against a regularised gradient boosting baseline; on tabular data of that size the baseline usually wins by a wide margin.
