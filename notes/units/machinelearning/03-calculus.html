Calculus and optimisation

Training a model means finding the bottom of a surface you cannot see, in a space with millions of dimensions, using only local information. Calculus supplies that local information — which way is downhill, and how steeply. This chapter builds from the definition of a derivative to the optimisers that actually run.

Learning is minimisation

Chapter 1 said learning is a search: pick a family of rules, define what wrong costs, find the cheapest rule. Once you write that down formally, it becomes a single mathematical problem.

$$\theta^{*} = \arg\min_{\theta} \; J(\theta), \qquad J(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} L\big(y^{(i)},\, f(\mathbf{x}^{(i)};\theta)\big)$$

θ is every parameter of the model stacked into one vector. J is the cost — average loss across the dataset. arg min means "the θ that makes this smallest". The star marks the winner.

That is the whole of supervised learning in one line. Everything else is detail about the shape of f, the choice of L, and how you perform the minimisation. For a handful of models — linear regression being the important one — you can solve it exactly with algebra. For everything else, including every neural network ever trained, you have to walk downhill.

The picture to hold

Imagine a landscape whose horizontal position is your parameters and whose altitude is the cost. You are dropped somewhere at random in thick fog. You cannot see the valley, you cannot teleport, and the landscape has as many horizontal directions as your model has parameters — often millions. All you can do is feel the slope under your feet and take a step downhill. Repeat. That is gradient descent, and it is remarkable that it works at all.

Derivatives

A derivative answers one question: if I nudge the input slightly, how much does the output move, and in which direction?

$$f'(x) = \frac{df}{dx} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

The fraction is the slope of the line through two nearby points on the curve — a secant. As those points slide together, the secant becomes the tangent, and its slope is the derivative at that spot. Three readings, all useful:

Slope
How steep the curve is here. Large magnitude means a small input change produces a large output change.
Rate of change
If x is time, the derivative is speed. If x is a weight, it is how fast the loss responds to that weight.
Best local linear approximation
Near x, the curve looks like a straight line of that slope: \(f(x+h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)h\). Every optimiser in this chapter relies on that approximation being decent for small h.

Why cross-entropy pairs with sigmoid

Take the sigmoid's derivative \(p(1-p)\) and cross-entropy's derivative \(\frac{p-y}{p(1-p)}\); multiply them via the chain rule and the awkward factor cancels exactly, leaving \(p - y\). Prediction minus truth. That cancellation is not luck — it is why this pairing is the default for classification, and why pairing sigmoid with squared error instead gives you a loss that learns painfully slowly when the model is confidently wrong.

Partial derivatives and the gradient

Models have more than one parameter, so "the slope" needs an upgrade. A partial derivative measures the slope along one axis while every other variable is held still.

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j} = \text{rate of change of } f \text{ as } x_j \text{ moves, all other inputs frozen}$$

Stack every partial derivative into a vector and you get the gradient:

$$\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \\[4pt] \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} \\[2pt] \vdots \\[2pt] \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_d}\end{bmatrix}$$

Two properties make the gradient the central object of optimisation:

  1. It points in the direction of steepest ascent. Of all the directions you could step from where you stand, the gradient direction increases f fastest. So \(-\nabla f\) is steepest descent — the direction to walk.
  2. Its magnitude is the steepness. \(\|\nabla f\|\) large means a cliff; near zero means flat ground, which is either a minimum, a maximum, a saddle, or a plateau.

A worked example

For \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 3y^2\):

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 6y, \qquad \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix}2x\\6y\end{bmatrix}$$

At the point (3, 1) the gradient is \([6, 6]^{\top}\) — equally steep in both directions at that point, even though the surface is three times more curved along y. That distinction between slope and curvature is exactly what makes optimisation hard, and it is what the Hessian below measures.

The chain rule and computation graphs

Neural networks are functions of functions of functions. The chain rule is how you differentiate them, and backpropagation is just the chain rule applied systematically with the intermediate results cached.

$$\frac{dz}{dx} = \frac{dz}{dy}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}$$

If x affects y, and y affects z, then the effect of x on z is the product of the two local effects. Sensitivities multiply along a path.

Worked all the way through

Take one neuron with one weight: input x, weight w, bias b, sigmoid activation, squared error against target y. We want \(\partial L/\partial w\).

∂L/∂w = −0.4994 · 0.1874 · 2.0        = −0.1871

# one gradient descent step with η = 0.1
w ← 0.5 − 0.1·(−0.1871) = 0.5187      # w increases, p rises toward y ✓

The negative gradient means increasing w reduces the loss, which makes sense: the prediction 0.75 is below the target 1.0, so push it up. Scale this exact procedure to a hundred million parameters and you have modern deep learning. Chapter 16 does the full matrix version.

Multivariate chain rule

When a variable influences the output through several paths, you sum the contributions from each path:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = \sum_{k} \frac{\partial L}{\partial u_k}\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x}$$

This is why residual connections help training: they add a second, short path from the loss back to early layers, so gradients arriving through the deep path can be almost dead and the sum still carries signal.

Jacobian, Hessian and curvature

Jacobian

When a function maps a vector to a vector, \(f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\), the derivative becomes an \(m \times n\) matrix of every output's partial with respect to every input:

$$\mathbf{J} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix}$$

A gradient is just the Jacobian of a scalar-valued function (one row). In practice you rarely materialise a Jacobian — it is enormous — but autodiff frameworks compute Jacobian-vector products constantly, and that is the operation that makes backpropagation cheap.

Hessian

Differentiate twice and you measure curvature: how the slope itself changes. The Hessian is the matrix of second partials, symmetric for any well-behaved function:

$$\mathbf{H}_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}$$

Its eigenvalues classify a stationary point — a place where the gradient is zero:

Hessian eigenvaluesPoint typeShapeIn practice
All positiveLocal minimumBowlWhat you want
All negativeLocal maximumDomeNever encountered in loss minimisation
Mixed signsSaddle pointPringleOverwhelmingly the most common stationary point in high dimensions
Some zeroDegenerate / plateauFlat valley floorSlow going; gradient is tiny but you are not done
Why local minima are not the problem people expect

For a point to be a local minimum, every one of the Hessian's eigenvalues must be positive. In a million-dimensional space that is like flipping a million coins and getting all heads — vanishingly unlikely for a random stationary point. Saddle points, where the surface goes down in some directions and up in others, are far more numerous. This is why deep learning works better than 1990s intuition predicted: the optimiser is rarely trapped, it is merely slowed, and adding momentum or noise gets it moving again.

Condition number: why some problems are just slow

The ratio of the largest to the smallest Hessian eigenvalue is the condition number. A large ratio means a long, narrow valley — steep across, nearly flat along. Gradient descent bounces off the steep walls while creeping along the floor, because the gradient mostly points across the valley rather than down it. Select the "ravine" surface in the demo below and watch it happen.

The practical cure is not a better optimiser first — it is feature scaling. Features on wildly different scales produce exactly this elongated geometry. Standardising them, covered in chapter 8, often turns a ravine back into a bowl and cuts training time by an order of magnitude.

Convexity

A convex function has no local minima other than the global one. If you can express your problem convexly, optimisation is essentially solved.

Formally, a function is convex if the straight line between any two points on its graph never dips below the graph:

$$f(\alpha \mathbf{a} + (1-\alpha)\mathbf{b}) \;\le\; \alpha f(\mathbf{a}) + (1-\alpha) f(\mathbf{b}) \qquad \text{for all } \alpha \in [0,1]$$

Equivalently, in one dimension, \(f''(x) \ge 0\) everywhere; in higher dimensions, the Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere.

ModelConvex loss?Consequence
Linear regression with squared errorYesClosed-form solution exists; gradient descent always finds the global optimum
Logistic regression with cross-entropyYesNo closed form, but any decent optimiser reaches the same answer every run
Linear SVM with hinge lossYesSame — reproducible, provable optimum
Ridge / lassoYesAdding a convex penalty to a convex loss keeps it convex
Any neural networkNoMany minima; results depend on initialisation and random seed
k-means objectiveNoWhy you restart it several times and keep the best — chapter 13
The practical consolation

Non-convexity sounds fatal and mostly is not. Empirically, the many minima of a large neural network tend to have similar loss values, so which one you land in matters less than you would fear. What you lose is reproducibility — change the seed, get a different model with comparable performance — which is why serious experiments report results over several seeds rather than one lucky run.

Gradient descent

One line of maths, repeated until bored or converged.

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_{t} - \eta\,\nabla J(\theta_{t})$$

η (eta) is the learning rate — how big a step to take. The minus sign is what makes it descent rather than ascent.

NaN in your loss

The overwhelmingly likely cause is a learning rate too high, and the standard first response is to divide it by ten. Other candidates: a log(0) in a hand-rolled loss, dividing by a near-zero standard deviation during scaling, or exploding gradients in a recurrent network (chapter 18). Once a single NaN appears it propagates to every parameter within one update, so the run is dead — restart rather than hope.

Batch, stochastic and mini-batch

The formula above averages the gradient over the entire dataset. With ten million rows, one step becomes very expensive, and you get exactly one update per full pass. The fix is to estimate the gradient from a subset.

VariantSamples per updateProsCons
Batch (full)All nExact gradient, smooth convergenceSlow per step; may not fit in memory; gets stuck in saddles
Stochastic (SGD)1Very fast updates; noise escapes shallow minimaExtremely noisy; cannot use vectorised hardware well
Mini-batch32 – 512 typicallyGood gradient estimate, GPU-friendly, useful noiseBatch size becomes another hyperparameter

Mini-batch is what everyone means by "SGD" in practice. The noise from sampling turns out to be a feature rather than a bug: it jostles the optimiser out of sharp, narrow minima, and the minima it settles in instead tend to generalise better.

Rule of thumb for batch size

Start at 32. Powers of two suit GPU memory layouts. Larger batches give smoother gradients and better hardware utilisation but need a proportionally larger learning rate to compensate — the common heuristic is that doubling the batch size lets you roughly double the learning rate, up to a point where it stops holding.

Momentum, and the two-dimensional picture

Plain gradient descent has no memory: each step depends only on where you are now. Momentum accumulates a velocity, so consistent directions build up speed while oscillating ones cancel out.

$$\mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \beta\mathbf{v}_{t} + \nabla J(\theta_t), \qquad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\,\mathbf{v}_{t+1}$$

β is typically 0.9, meaning the velocity retains 90% of its previous value each step — an exponentially weighted average of recent gradients.

Adaptive methods

Momentum fixes oscillation but keeps one global learning rate. Adaptive methods give every parameter its own effective step size, based on the history of gradients it has seen. Chapter 16 derives them; the summary:

OptimiserIdeaUse when

Learning rate schedules

A constant learning rate is a compromise: large enough to make progress early, small enough not to bounce around the minimum late. Schedules resolve the conflict by shrinking η over time.

Step decay — ×0.1 every 30 epochs Exponential — η₀·e^(−kt) Cosine annealing — smooth decay to zero Warmup — ramp up over the first few hundred steps Reduce on plateau — cut when validation stops improving

Warmup deserves a note because it looks backwards. Very early in training the parameters are random and the gradient estimates are correspondingly wild; a large step then can knock the model somewhere it takes thousands of steps to recover from. Ramping up over the first few hundred steps avoids this, and is standard practice for transformers.

How gradients get computed for you

Nobody derives gradients by hand for a real model. There are three ways a computer can produce them, and only one is used in practice.

MethodHowVerdict
Numerical\(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}\) for tiny hSimple but needs two evaluations per parameter and suffers rounding error. Useless for training; invaluable for testing
SymbolicManipulate the formula algebraicallyExact, but expressions blow up exponentially for deep compositions
Automatic (reverse-mode)Record the operation graph on the forward pass, walk it backwards applying the chain ruleWhat every framework does. Exact to machine precision, and the entire gradient costs about the same as one forward pass

The cost property is the crucial one. Reverse-mode autodiff computes the gradient with respect to all parameters in roughly two forward passes' worth of work, no matter how many parameters there are. Forward-mode does the opposite — cheap for many outputs and few inputs. Since a loss is one scalar output and a model has millions of inputs, reverse mode is exactly the right fit, and that asymmetry is why training deep networks is computationally feasible at all.

python — by hand, then by autodiff
import numpy as np

# ---------- gradient descent from scratch ----------
def fit_linear(X, y, lr=0.1, epochs=200):
    n, d = X.shape
    w, b = np.zeros(d), 0.0
    history = []
    for _ in range(epochs):
        y_hat = X @ w + b                    # forward, shape (n,)
        err   = y_hat - y
        loss  = np.mean(err ** 2)             # mean squared error
        grad_w = 2 / n * (X.T @ err)          # ∂J/∂w, shape (d,)
        grad_b = 2 / n * err.sum()             # ∂J/∂b, scalar
        w -= lr * grad_w                     # the update
        b -= lr * grad_b
        history.append(loss)
    return w, b, history

# ---------- gradient checking: trust but verify ----------
def check_grad(f, x, analytic, h=1e-5):
    """Compare an analytic gradient against a numerical one."""
    numeric = np.zeros_like(x)
    for i in range(x.size):
        step = np.zeros_like(x); step[i] = h
        numeric[i] = (f(x + step) - f(x - step)) / (2 * h)
    rel = np.abs(numeric - analytic) / (np.abs(numeric) + np.abs(analytic) + 1e-12)
    return rel.max()          # want < 1e-6; > 1e-3 means a real bug

# ---------- what you actually write ----------
import torch
w = torch.zeros(5, requires_grad=True)
X = torch.randn(100, 5); y = torch.randn(100)

loss = ((X @ w - y) ** 2).mean()
loss.backward()                              # fills w.grad via reverse-mode autodiff
print(w.grad)                                 # the same numbers, derived for you

opt = torch.optim.AdamW([w], lr=1e-3)
opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()                  # zero_grad matters — gradients accumulate
The forgotten zero_grad()

PyTorch adds to .grad rather than replacing it, so forgetting to zero between steps means each update uses the sum of every gradient so far. Training does not crash — it just behaves as though the learning rate is growing without limit, and the loss quietly diverges. If a from-scratch PyTorch loop misbehaves, check this line first.

Self-check

  1. Why does gradient descent use minus the gradient?
    Because the gradient points in the direction of steepest increase. Minimising means walking the opposite way. Flip the sign and you have gradient ascent, which is what you use when maximising a likelihood or a reward.
  2. Your loss is NaN at step 40. Give three plausible causes in the order you would check them.
    (1) Learning rate too high — divide by ten and rerun, it is the cause most of the time. (2) A log(0) or division by zero in the loss, often from a probability that reached exactly 0 or 1; add a small ε or use the framework's numerically stable version (BCEWithLogitsLoss rather than sigmoid followed by BCE). (3) Exploding gradients, especially in a recurrent model — clip the gradient norm. A fourth, easily missed: a feature column with zero variance producing a division by zero during standardisation.
  3. What does the chain rule have to do with backpropagation?
    Backpropagation is the chain rule, applied to a computation graph in reverse topological order, with intermediate values cached from the forward pass so nothing is recomputed. The "algorithm" part is the bookkeeping — the calculus is one rule.
  4. Explain why momentum helps on the ravine surface but barely changes anything on the bowl.
    On a ravine the gradient mostly points across the valley, so consecutive steps have opposing across-valley components that cancel in the running average, while the small consistent along-valley component accumulates. Net effect: less zigzag, faster progress toward the minimum. On a bowl the gradient already points nearly at the minimum every step, so there is no oscillation to cancel and little to gain — momentum can even overshoot slightly.
  5. Why are saddle points a bigger concern than local minima in deep learning?
    A local minimum requires every Hessian eigenvalue to be positive; with millions of parameters that is extraordinarily unlikely for a random stationary point. Mixed-sign Hessians — saddles — dominate. They are not traps in the strict sense, since a descent direction exists, but the gradient near them is tiny so progress stalls. Momentum and the noise in mini-batch gradients are what carry the optimiser through.
  6. You standardise your features and training suddenly gets much faster. What happened geometrically?
    Features on different scales produce a loss surface with very different curvature along different axes — a ravine, with a large Hessian condition number. Standardising equalises the scales, which makes the contours closer to circular, so the negative gradient points much more directly at the minimum and a single learning rate suits every direction at once.
  7. A colleague reports their model gets 91% accuracy with seed 0 and 88% with seed 1, and asks which is the real number.
    Neither on its own. Non-convex optimisation means the seed changes initialisation, shuffling and any dropout masks, so runs land in different minima. Report the mean and standard deviation across several seeds — say five — and treat any difference smaller than that spread as noise. If the spread is large, that is itself worth investigating: it usually signals too high a learning rate, too small a dataset, or an unstable architecture.
