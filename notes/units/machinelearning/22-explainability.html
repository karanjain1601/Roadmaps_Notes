Explainability and fairness

Two questions the accuracy number cannot answer: why did it decide that, and for whom does it work. Both have real methods behind them, both have more subtlety than the tooling suggests, and one of them ends in a mathematical impossibility you have to choose your way around.

— words— min read2 interactivesNeeds: chapters 09, 12, 15

Why explain a model

To debug it
The most common and least discussed reason. If a model relies on a feature that should not matter, an explanation surfaces the leak faster than any metric. Chapter 8's "which feature is doing the work?" is an explainability question.
To decide whether to trust it
A model with the right accuracy for the wrong reasons will fail as soon as the reason changes. Explanations are how you check the reasoning, not just the score.
Because someone has a right to know
Credit, employment, insurance and healthcare decisions carry legal obligations to explain in many jurisdictions.
To find who it fails
Aggregate accuracy hides subgroup failure. Explanations and sliced evaluation are how you find it before your users do.
To learn something
Occasionally a model discovers a pattern nobody knew about, and the explanation is the actual deliverable.

ScopeGlobal — how the model behaves overall. Local — why this one prediction

Global methods

Intrinsically interpretable models

The cheapest explanation is a model that does not need one. Linear and logistic regression give per-feature coefficients (chapters 11 and 12); a shallow decision tree gives a rule path you can print; generalised additive models fit a separate smooth curve per feature and sum them, giving nonlinear flexibility with a plottable shape per variable. On tabular problems the accuracy gap to gradient boosting is often smaller than people assume — measure it before paying for opacity.

Permutation importance

Shuffle one feature's values and measure how much performance drops. Covered in chapter 9, with its two traps: compute it on validation data, not training, and remember that correlated features hide each other's importance.

Partial dependence and ICE

$$\text{PD}(x_j = v) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f\big(v,\; \mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{-j}\big)$$

Set feature j to value v for every row, keeping the other features as they are, and average the predictions. Sweep v and you get a curve showing the model's average response to that feature.

PlotShowsLimitation
PDPThe average effect of one featureAveraging hides opposing effects in subgroups; assumes independence between features
ICEOne line per row rather than the averageReveals heterogeneity a PDP flattens away — always plot both
ALELocal effects computed within small windows of the featureCorrect when features are correlated, where PDP is not
Why correlation breaks partial dependence

PDP sets a feature to each value across the whole dataset, including combinations that never occur — a house of 20 m² with 8 bedrooms, a person aged 22 with 30 years of experience. The model is being evaluated far outside its training distribution, where its behaviour is arbitrary, and those arbitrary predictions get averaged into the curve. ALE avoids this by looking only at small local changes within realistic regions. Check the correlation between your features before trusting a PDP.

Local methods

LIME

Fit a simple interpretable model — usually sparse linear — to the black box's behaviour in a small neighbourhood around the point you care about. Perturb the instance, get predictions, weight the perturbations by proximity, fit. The coefficients of that local surrogate are your explanation.

Fast and intuitive, with a real weakness: the result depends on how you define the neighbourhood, and running it twice can produce noticeably different explanations. Useful for a quick look; not something to put in a regulatory filing.

SHAP

SHAP borrows Shapley values from cooperative game theory. The question it answers is precise: how should the difference between this prediction and the average prediction be divided fairly among the features?

$$\phi_j = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{j\}} \frac{|S|!\,(|F|-|S|-1)!}{|F|!}\Big[f\big(S \cup \{j\}\big) - f(S)\Big]$$

Average the feature's marginal contribution over every possible ordering in which features could be added. The combinatorial weight makes each ordering equally likely.

The properties are what distinguish it. Efficiency: the contributions sum exactly to the prediction minus the baseline — nothing unattributed. Symmetry: two features contributing identically get identical credit. Dummy: a feature that never changes the output gets zero. Shapley values are the unique attribution satisfying all of them, which is a stronger foundation than any competing method has.

Counterfactuals

Often the most useful explanation for the person affected: what would have to change for a different outcome? "Your application would have been approved with income £4,000 higher, or with one fewer recent default." A good counterfactual is close to the original, changes few features, and stays realistic — you cannot advise someone to reduce their age.

Four things explanations do not give you
  1. Causation. SHAP says the model used a feature, not that the feature causes the outcome. Chapter 5's confounding applies to explanations too.
  2. Correctness. A faithful explanation of a badly wrong model tells you exactly how it is wrong, which is useful — but the explanation being coherent does not make the model right.
  3. Stability under correlation. When features are correlated, credit gets split between them in ways that can shift with small data changes.
  4. Plausibility equals faithfulness. An explanation that reads sensibly may not reflect what the model actually did. This is why attention weights (chapter 19) are so misleading — they look like explanations and behave like something else.

Removing the protected attribute does not work

Dropping race or gender from the feature set produces "fairness through unawareness", and it fails because other features are correlated with the protected one. Postcode encodes race in many countries; purchase history encodes gender; name and school encode both. The model reconstructs the attribute from proxies and uses it anyway — while you have lost the ability to measure whether it did. Measuring disparity requires knowing group membership, which is why fairness work often involves collecting the very attribute people's instinct says to delete.

The impossibility result

When base rates genuinely differ between groups, no classifier can simultaneously satisfy calibration, equal false-positive rates and equal false-negative rates — except a perfect classifier or a useless one. This is a theorem, not an engineering shortfall. It surfaced publicly in the 2016 dispute over the COMPAS recidivism tool, where the vendor demonstrated predictive parity and journalists demonstrated unequal error rates. Both were correct. They had chosen different definitions, and the definitions were mathematically incompatible given unequal base rates. There is no technical resolution; you must decide which harm matters more, state it, and be able to defend it.

Mitigation

StageApproachTrade-off
Pre-processingReweight or resample training data; remove proxy correlationsModel-agnostic; may not survive a powerful learner rediscovering the pattern
In-processingAdd a fairness constraint or an adversarial head to the objectiveMost effective; requires control of training and careful tuning
Post-processingGroup-specific thresholdsSimplest and often the most effective — but explicitly treats groups differently, which may itself be unlawful depending on jurisdiction and domain
Non-technicalChange the label, the population, the intervention, or abandon the projectFrequently the correct answer, and the one least often considered

Governance

Model card
A short document per model: intended use, out-of-scope uses, training data and its provenance, evaluation results broken down by subgroup, known limitations, and contact for issues. Takes an hour and answers most questions people will ask later.
Sliced evaluation as standard
Every evaluation report should include performance by the subgroups that matter, plus the worst-performing group. An aggregate number alone should not pass review.
Human oversight that is real
"A human reviews every decision" is only meaningful if the reviewer has time, information and genuine authority to disagree. Automation bias is well documented: people defer to the machine, especially under time pressure.
Appeal and recourse
A route for people to contest an outcome, and an actionable explanation of what would change it. Counterfactuals earn their keep here.
Monitoring after launch
Fairness is not a launch checkbox. Populations shift, and a model fair at deployment can drift. Chapter 23.

The regulatory picture is moving. The EU AI Act introduces risk tiers with documentation, human oversight and transparency obligations for high-risk uses including employment, credit, education and essential services. Sector rules already exist in many places — lending and insurance in particular. If your model makes consequential decisions about people, the compliance question is a design input, not a final review step.

Self-check

  1. Why do SHAP values sum to the prediction minus the baseline?
    Because of the efficiency axiom, which is one of the properties that uniquely define Shapley values. The full attribution of the gap between this prediction and the average is divided among the features with nothing left over and nothing invented. That completeness is what lets you present a waterfall chart as a genuine decomposition rather than a rough indication — and the demo's "additivity check" verifies it numerically.
  2. Your PDP for "number of rooms" is flat, but you know it matters. What might be wrong?
    Two likely causes. Averaging: if the effect is positive for small properties and negative for large ones, the mean cancels out — plot ICE curves and you will see the two populations. Or correlation: rooms and floor area move together, so PDP evaluates impossible combinations like 8 rooms in 20 m², where the model's behaviour is arbitrary, and those predictions pollute the average. Use ALE, which restricts to realistic local changes.
  3. You remove gender from the features. Is the model now fair?
    No. Other features act as proxies — purchase categories, job titles, names, part-time status — so the model can reconstruct and use the attribute regardless. Worse, you have lost the ability to measure disparity, because auditing requires group membership. Keep the attribute for measurement, decide deliberately whether it enters the model, and evaluate outcomes by group either way.
  4. A vendor says their tool is fair because precision is equal across groups. A journalist says it is unfair because false positive rates differ. Who is right?
    Both, and that is the point. When base rates differ, predictive parity and equalised odds cannot hold simultaneously — it is a theorem. This was exactly the COMPAS dispute. The real question is not which measurement is correct but which harm the system should minimise: wrongly flagging someone who would not have reoffended, or failing to flag someone who would. That is a values decision, it should be made explicitly and in advance, and it should be documented.
  5. Why is a plausible-looking explanation not necessarily a faithful one?
    Plausibility measures whether an explanation matches human expectations; faithfulness measures whether it matches what the model actually computed. They come apart routinely — attention weights read like reasoning and can be substantially altered without changing the output. Test faithfulness directly: remove the features an explanation credits and check the prediction moves as predicted. If it does not, the explanation is a story rather than an account.
  6. What would you put in a model card that an accuracy report leaves out?
    Intended and out-of-scope uses; the training data's provenance, time range and known gaps; performance broken down by subgroup including the worst group; known failure modes and the conditions that trigger them; the decision threshold and the cost reasoning behind it; how to contest an outcome; and a named contact. Most of that takes an hour to write and answers the questions that arrive six months later when nobody remembers the details.
