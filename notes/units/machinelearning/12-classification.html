Classification

Predicting a category. Six model families, each with a different idea about what a decision boundary should look like — and one of them, gradient boosting, wins most tabular problems you will meet. This chapter is about knowing which to reach for and why.

The setup

Classification is regression with a categorical target, and almost every model outputs a score that gets thresholded into a class. Keeping those two steps separate in your head prevents a great deal of confusion later.

The model produces
A score, ideally a calibrated probability \(P(y=1 \mid \mathbf{x})\).
You then choose
A threshold. The default 0.5 is a convention, not a law, and it is the wrong choice whenever the classes are imbalanced or the two error types cost differently. Chapter 15 covers picking it properly.
The decision boundary
The surface in feature space where the score crosses the threshold. Its shape is what distinguishes one model family from another.

Logistic regression

Linear regression cannot output a probability — it will happily predict 1.4 or −0.3. Logistic regression fixes that by pushing a linear score through a squashing function.

$$z = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b, \qquad p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$$

The sigmoid maps any real number into (0, 1). Rearranged, it says something more interesting:

$$\log\frac{p}{1-p} = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b$$

The left side is the log-odds. So logistic regression is a linear model — of the log-odds, not of the probability. That is why its decision boundary is a straight line, and why coefficients are interpreted multiplicatively.

The loss, and why not squared error

Chapter 4 derived binary cross-entropy from the Bernoulli likelihood:

$$J = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y^{(i)}\log p^{(i)} + (1-y^{(i)})\log(1-p^{(i)})\Big]$$

Two reasons squared error is the wrong choice here. Statistically, it assumes Gaussian noise on a variable that is 0 or 1. Practically, combining sigmoid with squared error produces a non-convex loss with a nasty property: when the model is confidently wrong, the sigmoid has saturated, its derivative is nearly zero, and the gradient vanishes exactly when you most need it. Cross-entropy's gradient is \(p - y\) — proportional to the error, largest when the model is most wrong. It also happens to be convex, so there is one global optimum.

Interpreting the coefficients

Exponentiate to get an odds ratio: \(e^{w_j}\) is the multiplicative change in odds for a one-unit increase in \(x_j\).

Odds are not probability

Doubling the odds moves a probability from 0.10 to 0.18, but from 0.50 to 0.67 and from 0.90 to 0.95. The same coefficient produces a different change in probability depending on where you start — which is exactly what "linear in the log-odds" means, and the source of most misreadings of logistic output.

Multiclass

$$P(y=k \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}} \qquad \text{(softmax)}$$

One weight vector per class, exponentiate the scores, normalise so they sum to one. The alternatives — one-vs-rest (train K binary classifiers) and one-vs-one (train K(K−1)/2) — are what you use when the base model has no natural multiclass form, such as SVMs.

k-nearest neighbours

No training at all. To classify a point, find its k closest neighbours in the training set and take a vote.

k = 1
Maximum capacity. Training accuracy is always 100% — every point is its own neighbour — and the boundary is jagged. Pure variance.
Large k
Smoother boundary, more bias. At k = n it predicts the majority class everywhere.
Choosing k
Cross-validate. Odd values avoid ties in binary problems. Values around \(\sqrt{n}\) are a reasonable starting point.

Naive Bayes

Apply Bayes' rule directly, with one wildly false simplifying assumption that makes it tractable.

$$P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \propto P(y)\prod_{j=1}^{d} P(x_j \mid y)$$

The product is the "naive" part: it assumes every feature is conditionally independent given the class. Without it you would need to estimate a joint distribution over all d features, which is hopeless.

VariantAssumes each feature isUse for
Gaussian NBNormally distributed within each classContinuous features
Multinomial NBA countText — word or n-gram counts
Bernoulli NBBinary presence or absenceShort text, binary indicators
Complement NBAs multinomial, corrected for imbalanceImbalanced text classification
Why it works despite being wrong

Words in an email are obviously not independent, so the estimated probabilities are badly miscalibrated — naive Bayes routinely outputs 0.9999 when it means "probably". But classification only needs the ranking of classes to be right, not the magnitudes, and the errors from the independence assumption often push all classes in the same direction, leaving the argmax intact. It remains an excellent, near-instant baseline for text, and a poor choice whenever you need the probability itself.

Support vector machines

Of all the lines that separate two classes, an SVM picks the one with the widest gap — the maximum margin boundary. Only the points nearest the boundary matter; those are the support vectors, and moving any other point changes nothing.

$$\min_{\mathbf{w},b}\; \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\big(0,\; 1 - y^{(i)}(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}+b)\big)$$

The first term widens the margin; the second is hinge loss, which charges nothing for points safely on the correct side and grows linearly for violations. C sets the exchange rate between them.

The kernel trick

Data that is not linearly separable in its original space often becomes separable in a higher-dimensional one. The trick is that the SVM's optimisation only ever needs dot products between points — so you can replace the dot product with a kernel function that computes what the dot product would be in the higher space, without ever constructing it.

Scaling, and the size limit

SVMs measure distances, so unscaled features destroy them. They also scale poorly: training is between \(O(n^2)\) and \(O(n^3)\), which makes kernel SVMs impractical much beyond about 50,000 rows. Below that they are excellent, particularly with few samples and many features. Above it, use LinearSVC or move to boosting.

Decision trees

Repeatedly split the data on one feature at a time, choosing the split that most reduces impurity, until a stopping rule fires.

$$\text{Gini} = 1 - \sum_{k} p_k^2 \qquad\qquad \text{Entropy} = -\sum_{k} p_k \log_2 p_k$$

pk is the proportion of class k in the node. Both are 0 when a node is pure and maximal when classes are evenly mixed. Gini is slightly cheaper; the two almost always choose the same splits.

$$\text{Gain} = I(\text{parent}) - \frac{n_L}{n}I(\text{left}) - \frac{n_R}{n}I(\text{right})$$

Bagging and random forests

Train many deep trees, each on a bootstrap resample of the data, and average their votes. Deep trees are high-variance and each errs differently, so the errors partially cancel. Random forests add a second source of decorrelation: at each split, only a random subset of features is considered, which stops every tree from latching onto the same dominant feature.

More trees
Never causes overfitting — it only reduces variance further, with diminishing returns after a few hundred.
Out-of-bag score
Each tree omits about 37% of the data, so those rows give a free validation estimate with no separate split.
Main knobs
max_depth, min_samples_leaf, max_features. The defaults are usually sane.

Boosting

Train a weak model, look at what it got wrong, train the next model to fix those errors, and add it to the running total. Gradient boosting makes this precise: each new tree is fitted to the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions — for squared error, that is simply the residuals.

$$F_{m}(\mathbf{x}) = F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}) + \nu\, h_m(\mathbf{x})$$

hm is the new tree fitted to the current errors; ν is the learning rate, typically 0.01 to 0.1. Small ν with many trees beats large ν with few, almost always.

A workflow that works
  1. Majority-class baseline, so you know what a useless model scores.
  2. Logistic regression in a pipeline. Fast, interpretable, and occasionally the winner.
  3. Gradient boosting with sensible defaults and early stopping. This is usually the answer.
  4. Only then: tune, try alternatives, consider ensembling the best two.

If step 3 does not clearly beat step 2, that is informative — either the relationship really is close to linear, or your features are doing all the work and the model choice barely matters.

Self-check

  1. Why is squared error a poor loss for logistic regression?
    Two reasons. It is the maximum-likelihood loss for Gaussian noise, which is the wrong assumption for a 0/1 target. And composing it with the sigmoid gives a non-convex loss whose gradient vanishes when the model is confidently wrong — precisely when you need a large update. Cross-entropy's gradient is p − y, largest when the error is largest, and the resulting objective is convex.
  2. Random forest with 500 trees versus 50 — can the larger one overfit more?
    No. Bagging reduces variance by averaging, and averaging more independent estimates cannot increase variance. More trees give a better estimate of the same underlying average, with diminishing returns. Overfitting in a random forest comes from the depth of individual trees, not their number. Boosting is the opposite: each added tree increases capacity, so the number of rounds must be controlled by early stopping.
  3. Your SVM with RBF kernel gets 100% training accuracy and 62% on validation. Which hyperparameter do you change first?
    Gamma — lower it. A high gamma makes each training point's influence extremely local, so the boundary wraps tightly around individual points and memorises the training set. Lowering C also helps by widening the margin and tolerating more misclassification. Tune the two together on a log grid; they interact strongly.
  4. You need to explain each individual decision to a customer. Which models are viable?
    A shallow decision tree gives a literal rule path you can print. Logistic regression gives per-feature contributions that sum to the log-odds, which is explainable with care. Beyond those, use a complex model plus SHAP (chapter 22) to produce per-prediction attributions — legally acceptable in many contexts, though a genuinely interpretable model is safer where regulation is strict. Note that a shallow tree is often less accurate than boosting by a meaningful margin, and that trade-off should be a stated decision rather than an accident.
  5. Naive Bayes outputs 0.9998 for a case that turns out wrong. Is the model broken?
    No — it is behaving exactly as expected. The independence assumption means correlated evidence gets counted repeatedly, so probabilities are pushed toward 0 and 1 far harder than the evidence justifies. Its ranking is often still useful, which is why it works for classification. If you need the probability to mean something — for thresholding by expected cost, say — calibrate it, or use a model that produces calibrated output. Chapter 15 covers calibration.
  6. Gradient boosting at learning rate 0.5 with 100 trees, or 0.05 with 1,000 trees — which would you expect to perform better?
    Almost always the second. A small learning rate makes each tree a modest correction, so the ensemble explores the space of fits more finely and lands in a better place; large steps overshoot and each individual tree does too much work. The cost is training time, which is linear in the number of trees. Use early stopping on a validation set so you do not have to guess the tree count.
