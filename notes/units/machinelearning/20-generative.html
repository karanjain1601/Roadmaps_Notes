Generative models

Every model so far has consumed data and emitted a label. These models go the other way: they learn what the data looks like well enough to produce more of it. Four families, three of which are still in active use, and one idea — gradually removing noise — that turned out to beat the rest.

What generating requires

A discriminative model learns \(P(y \mid \mathbf{x})\) — enough to draw a boundary. A generative model learns \(P(\mathbf{x})\), the distribution of the data itself, which is a much harder object: it must know not just how cats differ from dogs but what makes an image look like a photograph at all.

Autoencoders

An encoder squeezes the input through a bottleneck, and a decoder reconstructs it. Train by minimising reconstruction error, with no labels required.

$$\mathbf{z} = f_{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{x}), \qquad \hat{\mathbf{x}} = f_{\text{dec}}(\mathbf{z}), \qquad L = \|\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}\|^2$$

The bottleneck is the whole point: forced to pass through k numbers, the network must discover what matters. With linear layers and squared error, an autoencoder recovers exactly the PCA subspace from chapter 9 — nonlinear layers are what let it do better.

Denoising autoencoder
Corrupt the input, ask for the clean version. Prevents learning the identity function and produces more robust features. The direct ancestor of diffusion.
Anomaly detection
Train on normal data only; anomalies reconstruct badly, so reconstruction error becomes an anomaly score. Chapter 13's method for high-dimensional data.
Not a generator
Pick a random z and decode it and you usually get nonsense, because nothing forced the latent space to be filled in — training only ever visited the codes of real inputs, leaving gaps between them.

Variational autoencoders

The fix for that last point: make the encoder output a distribution rather than a point, and penalise it for straying from a standard Gaussian. The latent space becomes continuous and samplable.

$$\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}\big[\log p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p(\mathbf{z})\big)}_{\text{stay close to } \mathcal{N}(0,I)}$$

Maximise this evidence lower bound. The first term wants faithful reconstruction; the second wants the encoder's output to look like a standard Gaussian. They pull in opposite directions, and the balance is the model.

The reparameterisation trick

There is a problem: you cannot backpropagate through a random sampling step. The trick is to move the randomness out of the path:

$$\mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathbf{x}) + \boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}) \odot \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$$

Now the sampling happens in \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\), which has no parameters, and \(\mathbf{z}\) is a deterministic differentiable function of \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\). Gradients flow. It is a small idea and it is what made VAEs trainable.

VAEs are no longer the state of the art for image generation, but they are far from obsolete: the "latent" in latent diffusion is a VAE's compressed space, which is what makes high-resolution image generation affordable.

Generative adversarial networks

Two networks in competition. A generator turns noise into samples; a discriminator tries to tell real from fake. Each improves by defeating the other.

$$\min_{G}\max_{D}\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log D(\mathbf{x})\big] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_{z}}\big[\log(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z})))\big]$$

At the theoretical optimum the generator's distribution equals the data distribution and the discriminator outputs 0.5 everywhere — it cannot do better than guessing. Reaching that point is another matter.

Where GANs still win

Single-pass sampling makes them fast, and StyleGAN-family models still produce exceptional faces with fine control over attributes. They remain strong for super-resolution, image-to-image translation and any application where inference latency matters. What they lost to diffusion is general-purpose, text-conditioned, high-diversity generation — precisely the areas where mode collapse and training instability hurt most.

Diffusion models

Destroy the data with noise in small steps until nothing is left, then train a network to undo one step. Generation is running that reversal from pure noise. It is a denoising autoencoder taken to its logical conclusion, and it turned out to be far more stable to train than anything adversarial.

The forward process

$$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\; \beta_t\mathbf{I}\big) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$$

Adding Gaussian noise repeatedly is itself Gaussian, so you can jump to any timestep t in one step — which is what makes training cheap. ᾱt falls from 1 to nearly 0 across the schedule.

The training objective

Pick a random image, a random timestep, and a random noise vector. Noise the image to that timestep. Ask the network which noise was added. That is the entire loss:

$$L = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0,\,t,\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\Big[\big\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_t,\, t)\big\|^2\Big]$$

A plain regression problem with a stable target — no adversary, no balancing act, no collapse. That simplicity is most of why diffusion won.

Sampling speed
The original formulation needed 1,000 network passes per image. DDIM and other deterministic samplers reduce this to 20–50 with little quality loss; distillation pushes it to a handful.
Classifier-free guidance
Train the model both with and without the text condition, then at sampling time extrapolate away from the unconditional prediction. A guidance scale of 5–10 sharply improves prompt adherence at some cost to diversity — the single most important knob in text-to-image systems.
Latent diffusion
Run the whole process in a VAE's compressed latent space rather than pixel space. A 512 × 512 image becomes a 64 × 64 latent, cutting compute by roughly 48×. This is what made high-resolution generation runnable on consumer hardware.
Conditioning
Text via cross-attention from an encoder; images and depth maps via ControlNet-style side channels; classes via embeddings added to the timestep signal.

Responsibilities that come with this
  • Memorisation is real. Diffusion models have been shown to reproduce training images near-verbatim, particularly for examples duplicated in the corpus. Check before deploying, and deduplicate training data.
  • Consent and provenance. Whether training data was licensed, and whether depicted people agreed, is a live legal and ethical question rather than a settled one.
  • Synthetic media. Non-consensual imagery and impersonation are the dominant real-world harm from this technology so far. Content provenance standards and watermarking exist; they are partial mitigations, not solutions.
  • Bias is inherited and often amplified. A generator reflects the distribution it was trained on, including its skews in who and what is represented.

Self-check

  1. Why can a plain autoencoder not be used to generate new samples?
    Nothing constrains the shape of its latent space. Training only ever produces codes for real inputs, leaving the regions between them unvisited and meaningless — decode a random point and you get an artefact. A VAE fixes this by forcing the encoder's output distribution toward a standard Gaussian, so the space is filled in and any sample from the prior decodes to something plausible.
  2. What is the reparameterisation trick for?
    Backpropagating through a sampling operation, which is otherwise not differentiable. Writing \(z = \mu + \sigma\epsilon\) with \(\epsilon\) drawn from a fixed standard normal moves the randomness outside the computational path, so z becomes a deterministic differentiable function of the parameters μ and σ. Gradients then flow to the encoder as normal.
  3. Your GAN produces one excellent image regardless of the input noise. What is happening?
    Mode collapse. The generator has found a single output the discriminator cannot reject and has stopped varying — it is winning the game without modelling the distribution. Mitigations include minibatch discrimination (letting the discriminator see batch diversity), Wasserstein loss with gradient penalty, and unrolled updates. The 1-D demo in this chapter shows the same failure with a two-mode target.
  4. Why is diffusion easier to train than a GAN?
    Because the objective is a plain regression: predict the noise that was added, with a fixed, well-defined target. There is no adversary, so no balancing of two networks, no oscillation and no collapse. The cost is at sampling time — you need many sequential passes rather than one, which is why so much recent work is about reducing the step count.
  5. You are told a model achieves FID 8.2. What does that not tell you?
    Whether it memorised the training set — FID would look excellent for a model that simply reproduces training images. It also says nothing about coverage of rare modes, since the metric compares aggregate feature statistics, and it depends on the particular pretrained network used to extract features, so numbers are not comparable across papers using different setups. Pair it with a precision/recall analysis and a nearest-neighbour memorisation check.
  6. What is the role of the VAE inside a latent diffusion model?
    Compression. Running diffusion directly on 512×512 pixels is enormously expensive, so a pretrained VAE encodes images into a much smaller latent grid — typically 64×64 — the diffusion process runs entirely there, and the decoder converts the result back to pixels. Roughly a 48× reduction in the spatial elements the expensive part has to handle, which is what brought high-resolution generation within reach of ordinary hardware.
