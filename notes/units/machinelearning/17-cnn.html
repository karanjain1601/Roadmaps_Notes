Convolutional networks

A dense layer treats pixel (0,0) and pixel (0,1) as unrelated inputs. Convolution builds in the two facts every image obeys — signal is local, and a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears — and that single change is what made computer vision work.

— words— min read2 interactivesNeeds: chapter 16

Why not just use a dense layer

Take a modest 224 × 224 colour image and connect it to a single hidden layer of 1,000 units:

$$224 \times 224 \times 3 \times 1000 \;=\; 150{,}528{,}000 \text{ parameters — in one layer}$$

Beyond the memory cost, this arrangement has no idea that pixels near each other are related, and it must learn what a vertical edge looks like separately for every position in the image. A cat shifted three pixels to the right is, to a dense layer, an entirely new input.

Convolution fixes both with two ideas:

Local connectivity
Each output looks at a small patch of the input, not all of it. A 3 × 3 kernel has 9 weights regardless of image size.
Weight sharing
The same kernel slides across every position. Learn "vertical edge" once and it applies everywhere — which is translation equivariance, and it is why the parameter count stops depending on image size.

The convolution operation

Slide a small grid of weights over the input, and at each position compute the sum of elementwise products. The result is a feature map — one number per position saying how strongly that patch matched the pattern in the kernel.

$$O_{ij} = \sum_{m}\sum_{n} I_{i+m,\;j+n}\cdot K_{m,n} \;+\; b$$

$$O = \left\lfloor \frac{I + 2P - K}{S} \right\rfloor + 1$$

I input size, K kernel, P padding, S stride. Worth memorising — it is the source of most shape errors in vision code.

Channels, and what a filter really is

A colour image has three channels, so a 3 × 3 kernel is really 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 weights plus a bias, and it produces one output channel. A convolutional layer applies many such filters in parallel:

$$\text{parameters} = K \times K \times C_{\text{in}} \times C_{\text{out}} + C_{\text{out}}$$

Each output channel is one learned pattern detector. Early layers converge on edges and colour blobs — remarkably close to what the human visual system's first stages do. Middle layers combine those into textures and corners. Late layers respond to object parts. Nobody designs this hierarchy; it falls out of training.

Pooling

Max pooling
Take the largest value in each 2 × 2 window. Halves the spatial dimensions, keeps the strongest response, and adds a little translation invariance — the feature is detected whether it sits in the top-left or bottom-right of the window.
Average pooling
Take the mean. Smoother, used less often for downsampling.
Global average pooling
Collapse each channel to its mean across the whole map. Modern architectures use this instead of flattening into a dense layer, which removes millions of parameters and makes the network accept variable input sizes.

Pooling has fallen somewhat out of favour: a stride-2 convolution downsamples too, and it learns how to do so rather than applying a fixed rule. Many recent architectures use no pooling at all except the global average at the end.

Residual connections, and why they mattered so much

$$\mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{x}$$

Add the block's input to its output. Before ResNet, deeper networks performed worse than shallower ones even on training data — not overfitting, but an optimisation failure: gradients could not reach the early layers through dozens of multiplications.

The skip connection provides a path along which the gradient is multiplied by 1. Chapter 3's multivariate chain rule explains it: when a variable influences the loss through several paths, the contributions sum. Even if the deep path contributes almost nothing, the identity path carries a clean signal. It also reframes what each block learns — the residual, the correction to the identity, which is an easier function to learn than the whole mapping.

Transfer learning

The single most useful technique in applied computer vision. Someone has already trained a network on millions of images; its early layers learned edges and textures that are useful for almost any visual task, and you can keep them.

Your dataSimilar to the pretraining data?Approach
Small (< 1k images)YesFreeze everything, train a new classifier head only
SmallNoFreeze the early layers, retrain the later ones. Hardest case — lean on augmentation
Large (> 100k)YesFine-tune the whole network at a low learning rate
LargeNoFine-tune everything, or train from scratch if you have the compute

Three transfer-learning mistakes
  1. Wrong normalisation. A pretrained model expects the exact channel means and standard deviations it was trained with. Feed it raw 0–255 pixels and accuracy collapses for no visible reason.
  2. Fine-tuning at the default learning rate. 1e-3 will destroy carefully learned features in the first few batches. Use 1e-5 to 1e-4 for pretrained weights, and a higher rate only for the new head.
  3. Augmenting the validation set. Random crops and flips belong in training only. Applying them at evaluation makes your metric noisy and usually pessimistic.

Augmentation

Label-preserving transformations multiply your effective dataset and are the strongest regulariser available in vision. Standard: random crop, horizontal flip, small rotation, colour jitter, random erasing. Stronger: mixup (blend two images and their labels) and CutMix (paste a patch of one image into another and blend the labels proportionally).

The one rule: the transformation must not change the correct answer. Horizontal flips are fine for cats and catastrophic for digit recognition, where a flipped 2 is not a 2 — and for medical images where left and right are clinically distinct.

$$\text{IoU} = \frac{|\text{prediction} \cap \text{ground truth}|}{|\text{prediction} \cup \text{ground truth}|}$$

Intersection over union: the overlap between predicted and true regions, divided by their combined area. A detection usually counts as correct at IoU ≥ 0.5, and mAP averages precision across classes and across several IoU thresholds.

Practical notes
  • Start with a pretrained backbone. Training a vision model from scratch is rarely the right first move.
  • Look at your data. Vision datasets are full of mislabelled images, duplicates and near-duplicates across the train/test boundary — the group-leakage problem from chapter 7, in visual form.
  • Use mixed precision (torch.amp). Roughly double the speed and half the memory for almost no accuracy cost.
  • Check the input pipeline before blaming the model. Data loading is the bottleneck more often than the GPU.

Self-check

  1. A 3×3 convolution with 64 input and 128 output channels — how many parameters?
    3 × 3 × 64 × 128 = 73,728 weights, plus 128 biases, so 73,856. Note that this is independent of image size, which is the whole point: the same layer works on a 32×32 or a 512×512 input with identical parameters.
  2. Why do two stacked 3×3 convolutions often beat one 5×5?
    They cover the same 5×5 receptive field but use fewer parameters — 2 × 9 = 18 per channel pair versus 25 — and they insert a nonlinearity between the two, making the composition more expressive than a single linear filter of the same reach. VGG made this argument and it became standard.
  3. Your ImageNet-pretrained model gets 30% accuracy on a simple 5-class problem. What is the first thing to check?
    Preprocessing. The model expects inputs resized to its training resolution and normalised with the ImageNet channel means and standard deviations. Feeding raw 0–255 pixels, or normalising to [0,1] without the per-channel statistics, degrades accuracy dramatically while raising no error. After that, check that the learning rate for the pretrained weights is small enough not to have wrecked them.
  4. What problem do residual connections solve, and how?
    Degradation with depth — beyond a few dozen layers, deeper networks performed worse even on training data, because gradients could not propagate back through so many multiplications. A skip connection adds an identity path whose derivative is 1, and since contributions from multiple paths sum, the early layers keep receiving usable gradient however weak the deep path becomes. It also makes each block learn a correction rather than a full mapping, which is easier.
  5. When would horizontal flip augmentation be harmful?
    Whenever the flip changes the correct label or destroys meaning: text and digit recognition, where mirrored characters are wrong; medical imaging, where left and right are clinically distinct; anything with directional semantics such as road signs or gauges. Augmentation is only valid when the transformation preserves the label, and that is a domain judgement rather than a default.
  6. Why has global average pooling largely replaced flatten-then-dense at the end of a CNN?
    Flattening a 7×7×512 feature map into a dense layer of 4,096 units costs about 100 million parameters, most of a VGG-sized model, and it forces a fixed input resolution. Global average pooling collapses each channel to one number — 512 values regardless of spatial size — which removes those parameters, reduces overfitting, and lets the network accept variable input dimensions.
