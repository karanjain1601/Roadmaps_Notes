Chapter 21 · Beyond the model

Natural language processing

Text has no natural numeric representation, no fixed length, and a meaning that depends on order, context and things left unsaid. This chapter covers how it gets turned into numbers, what those numbers have looked like over three generations of methods, and how to choose among them now that a pretrained model is usually a good answer.

What makes text hard

Discrete and unordered
Words are symbols with no inherent numeric relationship. "Cat" is not 3.7, and nothing about the spelling tells you it is closer to "dog" than to "carburettor".
Variable length
A tweet and a novel must both become fixed-size inputs, or the model must handle sequences directly.
Ambiguity everywhere
"Bank", "lead", "left" — resolvable only from context, which is precisely what the earliest representations threw away.
Compositional
"Not bad" is positive. "Not very bad" is more positive. Word counts cannot see this.
A brutal long tail
Zipf's law: a handful of words dominate and most vocabulary appears once or twice. Whatever your training corpus, real input will contain words it has never seen.

Most of this is legacy advice

Aggressive preprocessing made sense when features were word counts and vocabulary size was the binding constraint. With a pretrained transformer, the tokeniser was trained on raw text and expects raw text — lowercasing, stripping punctuation and removing stopwords all move your input away from the distribution the model knows, and reliably hurt. Normalise unicode, strip HTML, and otherwise leave it alone.

Tokenisation

Splitting text into units the model can index. The choice determines vocabulary size, sequence length, and what happens when an unseen word arrives.

GranularityVocabularySequence lengthUnknown words
Word100k+, still incompleteShortBecome <UNK> — information destroyed
Character~100Very longNever any
Subword30k – 100kModerateSplit into known pieces

Subword tokenisation is the answer everywhere. Common words stay whole; rare words break into meaningful fragments; nothing is ever unrepresentable. Byte-pair encoding builds the vocabulary greedily: start from characters, repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair, and stop at the target size.

Consequences worth knowing
  • Token count is not word count. English runs roughly 1.3 tokens per word; code, chemical names and non-Latin scripts run far higher. This is what you pay for per API call, and what fills your context window.
  • Non-English text is more expensive. A tokeniser trained mostly on English fragments other languages more aggressively — sometimes three or four times the tokens for the same content.
  • Character-level tasks are hard for token models. Counting letters or reversing a string is awkward when the model sees "strawberry" as two or three opaque chunks rather than ten characters.
  • Always use the model's own tokeniser. Vocabulary and merge rules are part of the trained artefact; mixing them produces indices that mean nothing.

Counting words: bag of words and TF-IDF

The oldest representation, and still a strong baseline. Represent each document as a vector of term counts, ignoring order entirely — the "bag". Raw counts over-reward common words, so weight each term by how rare it is across the corpus:

$$\text{tf-idf}(t,d) = \underbrace{\text{tf}(t,d)}_{\text{count in this document}} \times \underbrace{\log\frac{N}{1 + \text{df}(t)}}_{\text{rarity across the corpus}}$$
TF-IDF and document similarity
Turn IDF off to see why it exists

Bag-of-words plus a linear model remains an excellent baseline for text classification: it trains in seconds, needs no GPU, is fully interpretable, and on topic-driven tasks with plenty of labelled data it is frequently within a few points of a fine-tuned transformer. Its ceiling comes from what it discards — word order, negation scope, and any notion that "excellent" and "superb" are related.

Embeddings

Static word vectors

word2vec learns a dense vector per word by predicting context: given a word, guess its neighbours (skip-gram), or given neighbours, guess the word (CBOW). Words used in similar contexts end up with similar vectors — the distributional hypothesis, made computational.

The famous result is that the geometry encodes relationships: king − man + woman ≈ queen. Analogies work because the difference vector between "man" and "woman" is roughly consistent across gendered pairs. It is a genuine finding and it is also frequently overstated — many published analogies only work because the query word itself is excluded from the answer.

Two limits of static vectors

One vector per word, whatever it means. "Bank" gets a single representation averaging the river and the financial senses, which serves neither.

They absorb the corpus's biases. Word embeddings trained on web text place occupations near gendered terms in ways that reflect historical patterns rather than anything about the jobs. Debiasing methods exist and are partial at best — the association is distributed through the geometry, not stored in one place. Chapter 22 takes this up properly.

Contextual embeddings

A transformer produces a different vector for each occurrence of a word, computed from its actual sentence. "River bank" and "savings bank" now receive different representations, which resolves the ambiguity problem that static vectors could not touch.

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

baseline = make_pipeline(
    TfidfVectorizer(
        ngram_range=(1, 2),      # unigrams and bigrams — bigrams catch "not good"
        min_df=3,                 # ignore terms in fewer than 3 documents
        max_df=0.9,               # and terms in over 90% of them
        sublinear_tf=True,        # log-scale the counts; usually helps
        strip_accents="unicode",
    ),
    LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000, class_weight="balanced"),
)
baseline.fit(train_texts, train_labels)

# and it is interpretable — inspect what the model keyed on

vec, clf = baseline[0], baseline[1]
names = vec.get_feature_names_out()
for i, cls in enumerate(clf.classes_):
    top = np.argsort(clf.coef_[i])[-10:][::-1]
    print(cls, [names[j] for j in top])

Build an evaluation set before you build the system

A hundred hand-written examples with expected outputs, covering the ordinary cases and the awkward ones, is worth more than any benchmark score. It catches regressions when you change a prompt, lets you compare two models honestly, and forces you to define what "correct" means before you have a system whose behaviour might shape that definition. This is chapter 1's advice about agreeing the metric first, applied to a setting where it is skipped even more often.

Self-check

  1. Why does subword tokenisation solve the unknown-word problem?
    Because the vocabulary contains characters and character fragments as well as whole words, so any string can be decomposed into pieces the model knows. A word never seen in training becomes several known subwords rather than a single <UNK> token that carries no information. It also captures morphology for free — "unhappiness" splitting into "un", "happi", "ness" gives the model usable structure.
  2. You lowercase and remove stopwords before feeding text to BERT. What have you done?
    Degraded it. The tokeniser was trained on raw text, so lowercasing shifts input away from the distribution the model learned — and case carries real signal, distinguishing proper nouns and emphasis. Removing stopwords is worse: "not" and "no" appear in most stopword lists, and deleting them inverts the meaning of any negated sentence. This preprocessing was correct for bag-of-words features and is actively harmful for pretrained models.
  3. Why weight terms by IDF rather than using raw counts?
    Because a word appearing in every document distinguishes nothing. Raw counts make "the" the strongest feature in every document; IDF multiplies each term by the log of how rare it is across the corpus, so ubiquitous words are crushed toward zero and distinctive ones dominate. Turn the checkbox off in the demo and watch document similarity collapse toward uniform, because every pair now shares the same common words.
  4. What can a contextual embedding do that word2vec cannot?
    Give the same word different representations in different sentences. word2vec assigns one fixed vector per word type, so "bank" carries a blend of the river and financial senses that fits neither. A transformer computes each token's representation from its actual context, so the two uses are separated — which resolves lexical ambiguity and is most of why contextual models replaced static ones.
  5. Your document says 1,200 words but the API reports 2,900 tokens. What happened?
    The text is fragmenting more than typical English prose does. Likely causes: a non-English language on a tokeniser trained mostly on English; code, URLs or identifiers; chemical or medical terminology; unusual formatting and whitespace. English averages roughly 1.3 tokens per word, so 2.4 indicates one of these. It matters because tokens are what you pay for and what fills the context window.
  6. When would you still choose TF-IDF plus logistic regression over a fine-tuned transformer?
    When latency or cost is tight — it is milliseconds on a CPU against tens of milliseconds on a GPU. When you need to explain each decision, since coefficients map directly to words. When labelled data is plentiful and the task is topical rather than nuanced, where the gap is often only a few points. And always as the first baseline, because knowing what a simple model achieves is what tells you whether the complex one is worth its operational cost.
