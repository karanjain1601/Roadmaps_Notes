Practice and reference

Reading twenty-three chapters produces recognition, not skill. This one is built for use rather than reading: projects with defined success criteria, checklists for each stage, a formula sheet, a searchable glossary, and a self-quiz that draws on the whole unit.

— words— min read3 toolsThe last chapter

How the skill is actually acquired

By finishing things. A completed mediocre project teaches more than three abandoned ambitious ones, because the lessons are concentrated at the end — in evaluation, in the parts that break, and in discovering what you assumed.

Work on data you care about
Your own running data, your football league, your library, your energy bills. Domain knowledge is what makes feature engineering possible, and caring is what gets you past the boring middle.
Define done before you start
"Beats the baseline of predicting last week's value by 10% RMSE on held-out months." Without a number, projects drift until you lose interest.
Write the evaluation first
Before any modelling. It forces you to confront the split, the metric and the baseline while those decisions are still cheap.
Keep a lab notebook
One line per experiment: what you changed, what happened, what you concluded. Three weeks later it is the difference between knowledge and vague impressions.
Explain it to someone
Writing up a project surfaces every hole in your understanding. A README with a chart and a paragraph is enough.
Read other people's code
Competition solution write-ups are unusually dense in real technique. Read the winners' notes, not just their scores.

    Where to go from here

    Depth in the maths

    Strang for linear algebra, Boyd for optimisation, Blitzstein for probability, MacKay for information theory. Read them alongside projects rather than before.

    Depth in the methods

    The Elements of Statistical Learning for classical ML, Deep Learning by Goodfellow et al. for the foundations, and papers with released code for anything current.

    Depth in the engineering

    The MLOps unit for pipelines and infrastructure; Huyen's Designing Machine Learning Systems; Kleppmann for the data layer underneath it all.

    Adjacent units

    AI Engineer for building on hosted models, Data Engineer for the pipelines feeding you, Prompt Engineering and AI Agents for the LLM-native path.

    The short version of the whole unit
    1. Frame the problem, name the metric, and build the baseline before anything else.
    2. Spend your time on data and features, not architecture.
    3. Split honestly — by group, by time, before any statistic is computed.
    4. Start with the simplest model that could work; gradient boosting for tabular, pretrained for everything else.
    5. Measure with a metric that matches the decision, and report the uncertainty.
    6. Suspect leakage whenever the number looks good.
    7. Know who the model fails, not just how often.
    8. Shipping is the middle of the project, not the end.

    That is the unit. Twenty-four chapters, from the definition of a derivative to a drift monitor. If you have worked through the demos and attempted the self-checks, you have covered more ground than most people who describe themselves as knowing machine learning — and the remaining distance is closed by projects, not chapters.
