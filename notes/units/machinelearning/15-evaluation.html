Evaluating models

The highest-leverage skill in the unit. Most bad machine learning is not badly modelled — it is badly measured, and nobody noticed until it reached production. This chapter is about measuring honestly, choosing a metric that matches the decision, and knowing what your number does not tell you.

Splits and cross-validation

A single train–test split gives you one number with unknown uncertainty. Cross-validation gives you several, and the spread is as informative as the mean.

Validation-set overfitting

Evaluate two hundred hyperparameter configurations against one validation set and pick the best, and you have run two hundred tests and selected the winner — chapter 5's multiple-comparisons problem exactly. Some of that "best" score is luck, and it will not reproduce. Nested CV measures this properly; a genuinely untouched final test set is the cheap approximation. If your test score is consistently worse than your validation score by a stable margin, that gap is the size of the effect.

Precision and recall trade against each other, always

They are not independent quality measures — they sit at opposite ends of one dial, and that dial is the threshold. Lower the threshold and you catch more positives (recall up) at the cost of more false alarms (precision down). Any claim to have improved both at once means you changed the model, not the threshold. Quote them together, or quote F1, never one alone.

The independence from class balance sounds like an advantage and is the reason ROC misleads on rare positives. The false-positive rate divides by the huge negative count, so ten thousand false alarms among a million negatives is an FPR of 1% — invisible on a ROC curve, and catastrophic for the team reviewing the alerts. Precision divides by the number of alerts, so it shows the problem immediately. On imbalanced problems, quote average precision, not ROC-AUC. Set the positive-class slider above to 2% and watch the two curves disagree.

Multiclass averaging

Macro
Compute the metric per class, then take an unweighted mean. Every class counts equally, so rare classes have full influence. Use when they matter.
Weighted
Per-class metric weighted by class frequency. Dominated by the big classes.
Micro
Pool all TP, FP and FN, then compute once. For single-label problems this equals accuracy.

Choosing the threshold

0.5 is a default, not a decision. The right threshold comes from the costs of the two error types, which are a business input rather than a modelling one.

$$\text{Expected cost} = C_{FP}\cdot FP + C_{FN}\cdot FN$$

Sweep the threshold, compute expected cost at each, and take the minimum. If a missed fraud costs £200 and reviewing a false alarm costs £4, then the break-even point is where an extra alert has at least a 4/200 = 2% chance of being fraud — so the optimal threshold sits near 0.02, nowhere near 0.5.

python
from sklearn.metrics import precision_recall_curve, confusion_matrix
import numpy as np

proba = model.predict_proba(X_val)[:, 1]

# --- option A: minimise expected cost ---
C_FP, C_FN = 4, 200
thresholds = np.linspace(0.001, 0.999, 400)
costs = []
for t in thresholds:
    tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(y_val, proba >= t).ravel()
    costs.append(C_FP * fp + C_FN * fn)
best_t = thresholds[int(np.argmin(costs))]

# --- option B: hit a required recall, maximise precision within it ---
prec, rec, thr = precision_recall_curve(y_val, proba)
ok = rec[:-1] >= 0.90                       # "we must catch 90% of fraud"
best_t = thr[ok][int(np.argmax(prec[:-1][ok]))]

# --- option C: fixed alert budget ---
# the team can review 200 cases a day, so take the top 200 scores
best_t = np.sort(proba)[-200]

⚠ pick the threshold on VALIDATION data, then report performance on TEST.
   Choosing it on the test set is another form of leakage.
Ask for the costs

Stakeholders rarely volunteer them, and often say "both matter". Make it concrete: "if we flag a hundred extra transactions and catch one more fraud, is that worth it?" A few questions of that shape usually pin down a ratio, and a ratio is all the arithmetic needs. Doing this converts an argument about model quality into an arithmetic problem with an answer.

Calibration

A model is calibrated if, among all the cases it scores 0.7, about 70% really are positive. Ranking quality and calibration are separate properties — a model can rank perfectly and still have meaningless probability values.

Reliability diagram
Perfect calibration is the diagonal
ModelTypical calibration
Logistic regressionGood — it optimises log loss directly
Neural networks with cross-entropyModern ones are overconfident, more so as they get larger
Gradient boostingReasonable; benefits from recalibration
Random forestUnderconfident — averaging votes pulls values toward the middle
SVMNo probabilities at all; distances need converting
Naive BayesBadly overconfident — chapter 12 explains why
Platt scaling
Fit a one-dimensional logistic regression mapping raw score to probability. Two parameters, works on small calibration sets, assumes a sigmoidal distortion.
Isotonic regression
Fit any monotonic mapping. More flexible, needs more data — roughly a thousand calibration points — and can overfit below that.
Expected calibration error
Bin the predictions and average the gap between predicted and observed frequency, weighted by bin size. The usual single-number summary.
Calibrate on data the model has not seen

Fitting the calibrator on training predictions produces a calibrator tuned to the model's overfitted, overconfident training scores, which does nothing for new data. Use a dedicated calibration split, or CalibratedClassifierCV, which cross-fits internally. And note that calibration does not improve ranking at all — AUC is unchanged by any monotonic transform — so it matters exactly when you use the probability as a number rather than as a rank.

Search sensibly
  • Sample learning rates and regularisation strengths on a log scale. Uniform sampling between 0.001 and 1 puts 99.9% of your budget above 0.001.
  • Tune the parameters that matter first. For boosting: learning rate, then tree size, then subsampling. For networks: learning rate, then everything else.
  • A wide random search of 60 configurations usually beats a fine grid of 60 — with high probability it lands within the top 5% of the space.
  • Track every run. Optuna, MLflow or a CSV; anything beats scrollback.

Comparing models honestly

Model A scores 0.842, model B scores 0.838. Is A better? Almost certainly you cannot tell.

python
from sklearn.model_selection import RepeatedStratifiedKFold, cross_val_score
from scipy import stats

cv = RepeatedStratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, n_repeats=4, random_state=0)
a = cross_val_score(model_a, X, y, cv=cv, scoring="average_precision")
b = cross_val_score(model_b, X, y, cv=cv, scoring="average_precision")

print(f"A {a.mean():.4f} ± {a.std():.4f}")
print(f"B {b.mean():.4f} ± {b.std():.4f}")

# paired test — same folds for both models, so pair the scores
t, p = stats.ttest_rel(a, b)
print(f"difference {a.mean()-b.mean():+.4f}, p = {p:.3f}")

# a bootstrap interval on the test-set difference is more robust

Beyond the headline number
  • Slice the evaluation. Overall AUC of 0.85 can hide 0.62 for new customers, who may be the segment the project exists for. Report performance by segment, and specifically the worst group.
  • Look at the errors. Pull the fifty worst mistakes and read them. Patterns show up quickly — a data quality issue, a missing feature, a mislabelled subgroup.
  • Weigh the operational cost. A 0.3% gain that triples inference latency and adds three upstream dependencies is usually not worth taking.
  • Check stability across seeds. Chapter 3: if the spread across seeds exceeds the difference between models, you have not measured a difference.

Self-check

  1. Your fraud model has ROC-AUC 0.94 and average precision 0.31. Which is the honest number, and why do they differ?
    Average precision. Fraud is rare, so the false-positive rate on the ROC axis divides by an enormous negative count and stays tiny even when the alert queue is mostly noise. Precision divides by the number of alerts, which is what the review team actually experiences. Both numbers are correct; only one describes the operational reality.
  2. You improved recall from 0.62 to 0.78 by lowering the threshold. Is the model better?
    The model is unchanged — you moved along the same curve, and precision fell. Whether the new operating point is better depends entirely on the cost ratio between misses and false alarms. To claim the model improved you need a threshold-independent measure such as average precision or ROC-AUC, or the same metric at a matched operating point.
  3. Cross-validation gives 0.88, the held-out test set gives 0.81. What are the two most likely explanations?
    (1) Validation-set overfitting: you compared many configurations against the CV score and picked the best, so some of the 0.88 is selection luck. (2) Leakage inside the CV loop — preprocessing fitted on all folds, or a split that ignores grouping or time. Check the pipeline first, since it is the easier of the two to rule out, then look at how many configurations were compared.
  4. What does calibration give you that a good AUC does not?
    Probabilities you can do arithmetic with. AUC only measures ranking, and is unchanged by any monotonic transform of the scores — so a model can rank perfectly while reporting 0.9 for cases that are positive 60% of the time. You need calibration whenever the number feeds a decision: expected-cost thresholds, expected revenue, risk aggregation, or anything a human reads as a probability.
  5. A colleague reports their model is better by 0.004 AUC. What do you ask for?
    The spread. Repeated cross-validation scores for both models on the same folds, so the comparison is paired, and the standard deviation across folds. If the fold-to-fold spread is 0.02, a 0.004 difference is noise. Also ask what changed operationally — a difference that small rarely justifies added complexity, and the seed-to-seed variation alone may exceed it.
  6. Why is a single hold-out split risky on 800 rows?
    A 20% test set is 160 rows, so the estimate has a standard error of a couple of percentage points at best, and which rows happened to land in the test set matters a great deal. You get one number with no sense of its uncertainty. Repeated stratified k-fold gives you a distribution of scores from the same data, and the spread tells you how seriously to take the mean.
