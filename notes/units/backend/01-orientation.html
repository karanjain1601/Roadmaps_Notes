What backend engineering is

The job in one sentence: turn requests into correct responses, quickly, safely, and keep doing it when the traffic multiplies and something is on fire. Everything in this unit is one of those five words. The fastest way to see how they fit together is to follow a single request all the way through.

What the role covers

Front-end work is judged on what a person sees. Backend work is judged on what happens when nobody is watching — at four in the morning, under ten times the expected load, with one dependency already down.

Correctness under concurrency
A single user is easy. A thousand simultaneous users reading and writing the same rows is where the interesting bugs live: double charges, lost updates, orders confirmed against stock that was already sold. This is chapters 10 and 18.
Latency and throughput
Two different things that get confused. Latency is how long one request takes; throughput is how many you finish per second. You can improve one and destroy the other — batching is the obvious example. Chapters 4, 9, 13 and 19.
Data that outlives the code
Services get rewritten; the database is still there ten years later with everyone's data in it. Schema decisions are the most expensive ones you will make, which is why five chapters go on them.
Security you cannot bolt on
Authentication, authorization, injection, secrets, transport. Chapters 14 and 15, and the reason they sit before architecture rather than after.
Operability
Can you tell what the system is doing right now? Can you deploy without downtime? Can you find the cause of an incident in minutes rather than hours? Chapters 20 and 21.
The thing that surprises people arriving from the front end

Almost none of the difficulty is in the code you write. A typical endpoint is thirty lines of routing, validation and a query. The difficulty is in everything the thirty lines touch: a network that drops packets, a database with a lock queue, a cache that just went cold, a third-party API that answers in four seconds today. Backend engineering is mostly about the behaviour of systems you did not write and cannot fully control.

In the cold run, the application code — routing, validation, serialisation — accounts for a handful of milliseconds out of roughly one hundred and fifty. The network and the database are the request. This is why "optimise the code" is so often the wrong first instinct, and why chapter 20 puts measurement before tuning: on most slow endpoints the time is somewhere you did not guess.

You cannot reason about a design without a feel for what each operation costs. These span nine orders of magnitude, which is impossible to picture — so scale them up until a nanosecond is a second.

The three conclusions
  1. Crossing a network costs about as much as five thousand memory reads. One extra service call in a request path is not free, however small the service. This is the single strongest argument against splitting a system into microservices too early — chapter 17.
  2. Distance is physics, not engineering. A round trip from London to Sydney cannot be optimised below about 150 ms because light does not go faster. The only fix is to not make the trip — a CDN, an edge cache, or a replica nearer the user.
  3. A cache hit and a database query differ by roughly an order of magnitude; a cache hit and a disk seek by two. That gap is why chapter 13 exists and why nearly every system at scale is mostly caching machinery.

What does matter more than the language
  • Whether it is boring. A stack with ten years of Stack Overflow answers behind it will cost you less than a faster one nobody has debugged in anger.
  • Whether your team already knows it. Rewriting in a faster language usually loses more to unfamiliarity than it gains in throughput.
  • Whether the ecosystem covers your dependencies. A mature client for your database, queue and payment provider is worth more than a benchmark.
  • Whether you can hire for it. Uncomfortable, and true.

Getting value out of it

Build one thing alongside. A URL shortener or a small task API is enough to make every chapter concrete — add caching in chapter 13, add auth in chapter 14, add a queue in chapter 18. Reading about connection pools teaches you far less than exhausting one.

Attempt the self-checks before revealing them. They are written as the questions an interviewer or a senior colleague would actually ask, and recalling an answer badly is worth more than reading a good one.

Self-check

  1. Latency and throughput — what is the difference, and how can improving one hurt the other?
    Latency is how long a single request takes; throughput is how many complete per second. Batching improves throughput by amortising fixed costs over many items, but every item now waits for the batch to fill, so latency rises. Adding threads can raise throughput until contention makes each request slower. They are separate goals and a system usually has to choose which one it is optimising.
  2. In the cold request trace, roughly what fraction of the time was your application code?
    A few milliseconds out of about 150 — under five per cent. The rest was DNS, the TCP and TLS handshakes, two network crossings and the database. That ratio is why profiling before optimising matters so much: the instinct to speed up the code you wrote is usually aimed at the smallest slice of the total.
  3. Why can a cross-continent round trip not be optimised below roughly 150 ms?
    Because it is bounded by the speed of light in fibre, which is about two-thirds of light in a vacuum, plus routing and switching overhead. London to Sydney is roughly 17,000 km each way, so the physics alone accounts for well over 100 ms. No amount of engineering removes it. The only fix is to avoid the trip: serve from a CDN, cache at the edge, or put a replica near the user.
  4. Why does one extra internal service call in a request path deserve scrutiny?
    Because a same-datacentre round trip is around half a millisecond — roughly five thousand memory reads — before that service does any work. It also adds a new failure mode and a new timeout to configure. It is often the right call, but "it is just a small service" understates the cost, and a chain of five such calls turns a fast endpoint into a slow one with five ways to fail.
  5. Someone reports an endpoint is slow. What do you do before changing any code?
    Measure where the time goes, because the answer is very rarely where you would guess. Break the request into its stages — network, auth, cache, each query, serialisation — and find the one that dominates. Chapter 20 covers the tooling. Optimising without measuring usually means spending a day on the five per cent of the request that was your code while the real cost sits in an unindexed query.
  6. Why does "choose a boring stack" appear as advice in a technical unit?
    Because operational cost dominates over a system's lifetime, and a mature stack has known failure modes, existing answers to your questions, mature client libraries and people who have already debugged the hard parts. A faster or more elegant technology that nobody on the team has run under load transfers cost from the benchmark to the incident. The exceptions are real, but they should be argued for rather than assumed.
