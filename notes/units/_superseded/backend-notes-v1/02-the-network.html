The network beneath

Before your application sees a single byte, three protocols have already run: a name was resolved, a connection was established, and a session was encrypted. On a cold connection that accounts for more time than everything your code does, and every one of the three has a failure mode that produces a confusing production incident. This chapter is what each does, what it costs, and which parts you control.

~30 min read DNS · TCP · TLS 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapter 1

2.1Why a backend engineer needs this

Three practical reasons, and none of them is completeness.

  1. It is where cold-start latency lives. DNS, TCP and TLS cost three round trips before your request moves. At 90 ms that is 270 ms of nothing happening, which is more than most requests spend doing work.
  2. The failure modes are distinctive and confusing. "It works from my laptop but not from the server", "it works in the browser but curl fails", "it broke for some users four hours after we changed something" — each has a specific cause in this chapter.
  3. Several of your levers live here. TTL values, keep-alive settings, connection pool behaviour, whether you get session resumption — all decided by how these protocols are configured.

2.2DNS

DNS turns api.shop.com into an IP address. It is a distributed hierarchy with aggressive caching at every level, and the caching is both what makes it fast and what makes its failures strange.

CNAME at the apex

You cannot put a CNAME on shop.com itself, because the apex must carry SOA and NS records and a CNAME excludes all others. So the standard pattern — pointing at a load balancer's hostname — does not work at the root domain. Every DNS provider offers a proprietary workaround (ALIAS, ANAME, CNAME flattening) that resolves the target and serves an A record. It works, and it means your apex is only as fast and as correct as your provider's resolution of that target, which is worth knowing when the apex behaves differently from www.

TTL is the only control you have, and it is a commitment

Once a resolver has cached an answer, you cannot recall it. Not by deleting the record, not by contacting anyone — the answer is out there until it expires. That makes TTL a pre-commitment to how long a mistake or a failover will take, decided before you need it.

Choosing a TTL
TTLFailover takesQuery loadUse for
30–60 sUnder a minuteHigh, and you pay per query on most providersAnything you may need to move quickly; during a planned migration
300 sUp to 5 minutesModerateThe sensible default for a service endpoint
3600 sUp to an hourLowStable records: MX, TXT, things that do not move
86400 sUp to a dayVery lowNS records, and nothing you will regret

The operational rule that follows: lower the TTL before you make a change, not during it. Drop it to 60 seconds at least one old-TTL period ahead, make the change, confirm, then raise it again. Lowering it at the moment you need to fail over does nothing, because the old high TTL is already cached.

The failure modes

  • Negative caching. A NXDOMAIN answer is cached too, for the duration in the SOA record. Create a record after someone has already looked it up and they will keep getting "does not exist" until that expires — a very common "it works for me" report.
  • Stale answers after a change. Some resolvers ignore short TTLs and impose a floor. You cannot control this, which is why DNS-based failover is never instant.
  • Resolution inside containers. A container's resolver, its search domains and its ndots setting can turn one lookup into five failed ones. A surprising number of "the network is slow" reports in Kubernetes are this.
  • Connection-level caching in your own application. Many runtimes cache DNS for the life of the process, so a database failover that changes an IP is not picked up until you restart. Check your runtime's policy — the JVM's default was famously infinite.

2.3TCP

TCP gives you an ordered, reliable, error-checked byte stream between two endpoints. Everything it provides costs something, and the costs are visible in your latency numbers.

What you get, and what each guarantee costs 
ORDERED       bytes arrive in the order sent
              cost: a packet lost at position 5 blocks 6, 7, 8
                    even though they arrived — head-of-line blocking

RELIABLE      lost packets are retransmitted
              cost: detecting loss takes at least one round trip,
                    and often a timeout

CONNECTION-   both ends agree the conversation exists
ORIENTED      cost: a three-way handshake before any data,
                    and state held on both ends afterwards

FLOW-         the sender does not overwhelm the receiver
CONTROLLED    cost: the receive window bounds throughput —
                    on a high-latency link this is the limit

CONGESTION-   the sender does not overwhelm the network
CONTROLLED    cost: SLOW START. A new connection begins with a
                    small window and grows over several round
                    trips, so early transfers are throttled

Slow start is the one that surprises people. A brand-new TCP connection cannot use the available bandwidth immediately — it starts with a congestion window of about ten packets, roughly 14 KB, and doubles each round trip until it hits loss. A 200 KB response on a fresh connection therefore takes several round trips to deliver regardless of how fast the link is. Reusing a warmed connection avoids this entirely, which is a second, less obvious reason keep-alive matters.

Connection reuse

The single largest lever in this chapter. A handshake per request is three round trips of pure overhead plus a cold congestion window; a reused connection is zero. Concretely:

2.4TLS

TLS provides three things, and it is worth separating them because people conflate the first with the whole: confidentiality (nobody in the middle can read it), integrity (nobody can alter it undetected), and authentication (you are talking to who you think). The third is the one that produces production incidents.

The handshake

TLS 1.3 — one round trip 
Client → ClientHello
           supported versions, cipher suites, a key share
           (guessing the group the server will pick), and SNI —

           the right certificate

Server → ServerHello + key share

           …all encrypted from this point except the hello

Client → Finished, then application data

ONE round trip. TLS 1.2 needed two, because the key exchange
and the cipher negotiation happened in separate flights.

0-RTT resumption: on a repeat visit the client can send data
in the first flight. It is fast, and that early data is
REPLAYABLE — so it must only carry idempotent requests.

Certificates, chains, and the trap

A certificate binds a public key to a name, signed by an issuer. Your server's certificate is signed by an intermediate, which is signed by a root that is already in the client's trust store. The client must be able to build the chain from your certificate up to a root it trusts.

The missing intermediate

Servers must send the full chain — leaf plus intermediates — not just the leaf. If you send only the leaf, browsers often still work, because they cache intermediates from other sites or fetch them via the AIA extension. But curl, your language's HTTP client, mobile apps and other servers frequently do not, and they fail with an unhelpful "unable to get local issuer certificate".

The symptom is unmistakable once you know it: the site works in a browser and fails from everything else, and it may work on your machine and not on a colleague's depending on what each browser happens to have cached. Test with openssl s_client -connect host:443 -showcerts from a machine that has never visited the site, and check the chain length.

The other TLS failures worth recognising on sight

Where TLS terminates

A decision with real consequences. Terminating at the load balancer or CDN is simplest: one place manages certificates, and the application speaks plain HTTP internally. Terminating at the application means the traffic is encrypted end to end, which some compliance regimes require, at the cost of managing certificates everywhere. The middle option — re-encrypting between the proxy and the application — gets both, and is what a service mesh automates.

Whichever you choose, the practical consequence to remember: once TLS terminates, the application no longer knows the client's IP or protocol unless the proxy tells it. That is what X-Forwarded-For and X-Forwarded-Proto are, and trusting them blindly is a security bug, since a client can send them too. Configure your framework to trust them only from your own proxy's address.

You lower a record's TTL from 3600 to 60 and immediately fail over. Why is traffic still going to the old server twenty minutes later?

Because the resolvers that already had the answer cached it with the old TTL of 3600 seconds, and lowering it now does not reach them. A TTL is attached to an answer at the moment it is handed out; changing the record changes what future queries receive, not what has already been distributed. So resolvers that looked up the name shortly before your change will keep serving the old address for up to an hour, and there is no mechanism to recall it. This is why TTL is a pre-commitment: to fail over within a minute you must have been running a 60-second TTL for at least an hour beforehand. The operational pattern is to lower the TTL one full old-TTL period ahead of a planned change, verify from several networks that the short TTL is being served, make the change, then raise it again afterwards. And for unplanned failover, do not rely on DNS at all — put a load balancer or anycast address in front, so the IP never changes and the failover happens at a layer you control in seconds.

Your API works in Chrome and fails from a Python client with "certificate verify failed". What is the most likely cause?

An incomplete certificate chain — your server is sending only the leaf certificate without the intermediates. Browsers frequently paper over this: they cache intermediates encountered on other sites, and they will fetch a missing one using the Authority Information Access extension in the certificate. Most non-browser clients do neither, so they cannot build a path from your leaf to a trusted root and reject the connection. The tell is exactly the pattern described — works in a browser, fails everywhere else, and may work on one developer's machine and not another's depending on browsing history. Confirm it with openssl s_client -connect api.example.com:443 -showcerts and count the certificates returned: you should see your leaf plus at least one intermediate. The fix is server configuration — point at the full-chain file your certificate authority provides rather than the leaf-only one; with Let's Encrypt that is fullchain.pem, not cert.pem. Two other candidates worth ruling out: the client's CA bundle being missing or stale, which is common in slim container images and is fixed by installing ca-certificates, and the certificate not covering the hostname in its SAN list.

Why is a fresh TCP connection slow even on a fast network with no packet loss?

Slow start. TCP has no way to know how much bandwidth is available, so it begins conservatively — an initial congestion window of about ten segments, roughly 14 KB — and doubles it each round trip until it detects loss. That means the first round trip can carry only 14 KB no matter how fast the link is, the second 28 KB, and so on. A 200 KB response therefore needs four or five round trips just to get its congestion window large enough, entirely independently of bandwidth. On a 90 ms link that is close to half a second of ramp-up on a connection that could in principle have delivered the whole thing in one. Add the three handshake round trips and a cold connection is dramatically worse than a warm one for reasons that have nothing to do with your server. This is the second, less-cited argument for connection reuse: you keep not only the handshake but the warmed congestion window. It is also why HTTP/2 multiplexing helps so much — one warmed connection carrying all the page's requests, rather than six cold ones each restarting slow start.

Recap

  1. DNS, TCP and TLS run before your code sees anything, and on a cold connection they cost three round trips.
  2. DNS is a cached hierarchy; the caching is why it is fast and why its failures are confusing. Negative answers are cached too.
  3. TTL is a pre-commitment. Lower it a full old-TTL period before a change, not during one.
  4. CNAME cannot live at the apex, so every provider has a proprietary workaround with its own behaviour.
  5. TCP's guarantees each cost something: ordering causes head-of-line blocking, and congestion control causes slow start.
  6. Connection reuse is the biggest lever here — it removes the handshakes and keeps the warmed congestion window.
  7. TLS 1.3 is one round trip; resumption is zero, and 0-RTT early data is replayable so it must be idempotent.
  8. Send the full chain. Working in a browser and failing elsewhere is the signature of a missing intermediate.
  9. After termination the app is blind to the client unless the proxy forwards it — and those headers must only be trusted from your own proxy.
