Making it stick

Everything in this unit is achievable and most of it decays. This last chapter is about the part that determines whether any of it is still true in two years: what you measure, who carries it, and where to start when all of it needs doing at once.

~28 min read Metrics · champions · sequencing · the role 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: the rest of the unit

22.1Measuring the right thing

Metrics change behaviour whether or not they measure anything useful, which makes a badly chosen one actively harmful rather than merely uninformative.

What does this metric make people do?

For each one: what it rewards, and how it is gamed without anyone lying.

Measure the system, not the security team's activity

The distinction that sorts good metrics from bad ones. Activity metrics — findings raised, scans run, reviews performed, training delivered — measure how busy the security function is, and improving them does not require the organisation to become safer; several of them improve when it gets worse. System metrics — how long a fix takes, what proportion of teams are on the paved road, how much of the estate is covered by a control, how fast you contain — measure properties of the organisation, and the only way to improve them is to actually improve it. A useful test for any proposed metric: could this get better while the organisation gets more dangerous? If yes, it is an activity metric and it will eventually be optimised at your expense.

22.3Maturity models, used honestly

They are useful for one thing and routinely used for another. As a checklist of things that exist — capabilities you might not have thought of, arranged roughly by dependency — a maturity model is a decent survey of the field. As a score to improve, it becomes the activity metric problem in institutional form: teams work on whatever raises the number, which is whichever practice is cheapest to nominally adopt rather than whichever reduces the most risk.

The three that get skipped

Inventory and ownership, because it is unglamorous and produces no demo — and then every finding for the next two years lands in a queue with no owner. Deleting controls, because nobody is rewarded for it, so the policy set, the scanner rules and the exception list only ever grow, and each addition costs a little attention from everyone forever. And rehearsal, because it takes a day and finds nothing when things go well — except that it does find something, every time: a runbook referencing a decommissioned system, a pager rota with a person who left, an isolation command nobody has permission to run. Every one of those is cheap to find on a Tuesday and expensive to find at 3am.

Why is "number of open vulnerabilities" a bad metric?

Because it can be improved by making the organisation less safe, and it gets worse when you do the right thing. Adding a scanner, onboarding more repositories, or turning on a deeper analysis mode all raise the number while your actual exposure falls — so a team that invests in visibility is penalised, and a team that scans less looks better. It is also improvable by suppression, by narrowing scope, by excluding a noisy repository, and by arguing findings down in severity, none of which changes anything about the estate. And it is dominated by noise you do not control: a busy month for disclosures in a popular library moves every team's number at once, so the trend line carries almost no signal about anything anyone did. Underneath all of that is the deeper problem — it is an activity metric about the security function's inbox rather than a property of the system. Better alternatives are ones that only improve when the organisation improves. Age of the oldest reachable finding is hard to game, because the only ways down are fixing it or formally accepting it with a dated exception, and it tracks the thing that actually hurts, since the finding behind an incident is rarely the one raised yesterday. Median time to remediate shows whether the normal case works. Paved-road adoption measures whether the safe path is winning. And defects reaching production measures the outcome directly. Apply the first two only to reachable, fixable findings, or they inherit the same noise. The general test: could this number improve while the organisation becomes more dangerous? For open vulnerability count the answer is plainly yes.

Your paved road has 30% adoption after a year. What is wrong?

Almost certainly that it is not the fastest way to ship, and much less likely that teams are being difficult. Adoption of an optional path is set by the comparison a team makes when starting work: if using your template, module or pipeline gets them to production sooner than assembling their own, they use it without being asked, and if it does not, no amount of advocacy will hold. So the diagnosis starts by measuring that honestly — time from empty repository to deployed service, on the paved road and off it — and it is common to find the paved road is slower because of a mandatory review step, a slow pipeline, or a template that needs three tickets to other teams before it works. The second cause is fit: a road built for the median service that does not accommodate the streaming workload, the batch job or the legacy integration forces those teams off it, and once off they stay off. The response is to widen the road rather than to insist — every workaround you see is a requirement you missed. Third is discoverability, which is unglamorous and real: teams cannot adopt something they do not know exists or cannot get running from the documentation in an hour. Two things not to do. Do not mandate it while it is slower, because you will get compliance in form — teams using the template and immediately overriding everything in it — plus a lasting reputation for imposing cost. And do not treat 30% as a communications problem; talk to five teams who are not using it and ask what they did instead, which usually produces the answer in an afternoon. Mandate only once it is genuinely the easiest path, at which point the mandate is uncontroversial because nearly everyone is already there.

If you could only do three things from this whole unit, what would they be?

First, inventory and ownership. It is the least interesting item in the unit and everything else depends on it: findings route nowhere without an owner, policies about production cannot identify production, incident response spends its first half-hour working out who to wake, and audits fail on it more reliably than on anything sophisticated. Enforce owner and environment tags at resource creation, generate the service catalogue from what is actually deployed, and treat untagged resources as failing rather than exempt. Second, eliminate static credentials. OIDC federation for pipelines and workload identity for services remove the single most valuable thing an attacker can steal, and they do it by construction rather than by discipline — no secret exists to leak from a repository, an image layer or a log, and nothing needs rotating. This is consistently the largest reduction in real risk available for the effort, and it is configuration rather than a purchase. Third, the paved road: a vetted base image, a service template with authentication and logging wired in, and infrastructure modules with secure defaults. It converts every future security decision from something each team makes independently into something they inherit, and it means a fix is one change rather than two hundred. If a fourth were allowed it would be automated dependency upgrades, and a fifth would be one incident rehearsal — the rehearsal because it reliably finds a broken runbook, a stale rota or a permission nobody has, and each of those is cheap on a Tuesday and expensive at 3am. Notice what is absent: no detection product, no scanner, no vendor. Those have their place and they come after the three above, because none of them compensates for not knowing what you own.

Recap

  1. Ask of any metric: could this improve while the organisation gets more dangerous?
  2. Measure the system, not the security team's activity. Findings raised is the worst common metric.
  3. Champions need allocated time, a real job and recognition, and must be volunteers.
  4. Adoption is set by whether the paved road is faster, not by whether it is required.
  5. Every workaround is a requirement you missed. Widen the road rather than insisting.
  6. Maturity models are a checklist of ideas, not a score to raise.
  7. Inventory and ownership first. It is unglamorous and everything depends on it.
  8. Then remove static credentials, which is the largest real risk reduction available.
  9. Delete controls that no longer earn their place. Nobody is rewarded for it and it matters.
  10. Rehearse once a year. It always finds something, and finding it on a Tuesday is cheap.

And that is the unit

Twenty-two chapters, following software from an idea to production and back again. If one thing survives from all of it, let it be the argument in Chapter 1: security work succeeds when it shortens the loop between causing a problem and learning about it, and fails when it adds a queue. Every control in this unit should be judged against that — what it catches, how fast it says so, and what it costs the person waiting.

The companion Cyber Security unit covers the same field from the defender's side — attacks, cryptography, detection, forensics and the profession — and the two are designed to be read together.
