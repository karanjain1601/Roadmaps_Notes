Identity and certificates at scale

Human identity is the Cyber Security unit's subject. This chapter is about the identities that outnumber humans by two orders of magnitude — services, jobs, pipelines and functions — and about the certificate expiry that will, at some point, take your entire estate down.

19.1What a workload identity has to solve

The bootstrap problem, which is the whole difficulty 
A human proves who they are with something they know, have
or are. A workload has none of those — it is a process that
started thirty seconds ago on a machine you also do not
inherently trust. So:

  HOW DOES A NEW WORKLOAD GET ITS FIRST CREDENTIAL?

THE WRONG ANSWER, AND THE COMMON ONE
  Bake one into the image or the configuration. Now the
  credential is in a registry, in version control, in every
  running copy, and it never changes. You have replaced the
  problem with a worse one.                          → ch 7

THE ANSWER: ATTESTATION BY SOMETHING THAT ALREADY KNOWS
  The platform placed the workload, so the platform can
  vouch for it. A node agent asks the orchestrator "what is
  this process?", gets back "the checkout service in the
  payments namespace", and issues an identity for that.
  Trust chains back to the platform's own knowledge rather
  than to a secret anyone could copy.

  · In Kubernetes: the projected service account token,
    audience-scoped, verified against the API server.
  · In a cloud: the instance identity document, and
    federation for anything outside.             → ch 8, 14
  · Across environments: SPIFFE, which standardises the
    identity format and the issuance flow so an identity
    means the same thing in a cluster, on a VM and in
    another cloud.

WHAT COMES OUT
  A SHORT-LIVED CERTIFICATE naming the workload, renewed
  automatically for as long as the workload keeps passing
  attestation — and expiring on its own if it stops.
The property that matters most

A workload identity is not a secret that exists before the workload does. There is nothing to leak from a repository, nothing to find in an image layer, nothing to rotate on a schedule, and nothing that outlives the process it belongs to. That single property removes most of Chapter 7's problems by construction rather than by discipline — and it is why "get rid of the static credential" is the highest-value change available in almost every estate, ahead of any detection or scanning work.

19.2What actually causes certificate outages

The folk claim is that short lifetimes are dangerous because there is more to go wrong, and the counter-claim is that they are safer. Both are wrong in an interesting way: lifetime is not the variable that decides outages — automation and inventory coverage are. What short lifetimes do is remove the option of doing it by hand.

Certificate lifetime against outage risk

Renewal frequency and renewal reliability move in opposite directions.

Retries are why automated renewal almost never fails

The reason an automated short-lifetime certificate is safe is not that the procedure is more practised — though it is. It is that renewal begins long before expiry, so a single failure is not an outage. A twenty-four-hour certificate renewed at the sixteen-hour mark has eight hours of retry window; at one attempt an hour, an outage requires eight consecutive failures, and the probability of that is negligible even with a mediocre success rate. A one-year certificate renewed by a human has one attempt, possibly two if an alert fires, and a per-attempt failure rate of roughly a third — because it is performed by someone who has never done it, from a document written by someone who has left, against a system that has changed. So the ordering is: automate, verify the retries work, then shorten. Shortening first is the disaster the intuition warns about, and it is a real one.

Do shorter certificate lifetimes cause more outages?

Not if renewal is automated — and catastrophically yes if it is not. The variable that decides outage risk is not lifetime, it is whether a failed renewal gets retried before the certificate expires. Automated renewal starts well before expiry: a twenty-four-hour certificate renewed at sixteen hours has an eight-hour window, so at one attempt an hour an outage requires eight consecutive failures, and even a mediocre per-attempt success rate makes that vanishingly unlikely. A one-year certificate renewed by hand gets one attempt, or two if an alert fires, against a per-attempt failure rate around a third, because the procedure is performed by someone who has never done it from a document written by someone who has left. Run the panel and the point is visible: with automation, annual and daily lifetimes both produce essentially no outages from managed certificates; with manual renewal, shortening the lifetime multiplies the failures roughly in proportion to how often you now have to do it. So the honest ordering is automate, verify the retry behaviour, then shorten. What short lifetimes genuinely buy you is elsewhere. They bound exposure from a leaked key to the lifetime, which is the security argument and the real one. They force automation, because nobody can renew by hand at that cadence, and automation is what makes the whole thing reliable — an indirect benefit that matters more than it sounds. And they make revocation unnecessary, which §19.5 argues is worth a great deal given how unreliable revocation is in practice. Two things the panel also shows: certificates outside your inventory dominate the outage count regardless of what lifetime policy you set, because nobody issues a one-hour certificate by hand — unknown certificates are long-lived by construction. And with short lifetimes the issuer becomes critical infrastructure, since a failed issuance service breaks nothing for one lifetime and then everything at once.

What problem does workload identity solve that a secret manager does not?

The bootstrap problem. A secret manager solves storage and distribution well — the secret is encrypted, access-controlled, audited, and rotatable — but the workload still has to authenticate to the secret manager in order to get anything, and whatever it uses for that is a credential that must exist before the workload runs. In practice that becomes a token in the image, an environment variable in the deployment, or a file on the host, and it is long-lived because nothing can rotate it without the same problem recurring. So the estate's most sensitive credential is the one protecting all the others, and it is stored the least well. Workload identity attacks that directly: instead of the workload holding a secret, the platform attests to what the workload is, because the platform is what placed it there. The orchestrator knows this process is the checkout service in the payments namespace; a node agent asks, gets a signed answer, and exchanges it for a short-lived certificate. Nothing secret existed before the workload started, so there is nothing to leak from a registry, nothing in version control, nothing in an image layer, and nothing to rotate. The two are complementary rather than alternatives, and the right arrangement is to use workload identity as the authentication to the secret manager, which removes the bootstrap credential while keeping the manager for the things that genuinely must be stored — third-party API keys, legacy database passwords, anything issued by someone else. That combination also improves the audit trail, since access is attributed to a specific workload identity rather than to a shared token that could have been used by anything holding a copy.

Why prefer short lifetimes to revocation?

Because revocation is unreliable in a way that is structural rather than incidental, and short lifetimes achieve the same goal without depending on it. For revocation to work, the verifier has to check a list or make a status query at the moment of validation, and every part of that is fragile: the lists get large, the status responders add latency to every connection, they become an availability dependency, and — critically — most implementations soft-fail, meaning that when the check cannot complete they proceed rather than refuse. An attacker who can interfere with the check therefore defeats revocation entirely, and even without an attacker, a revoked certificate frequently keeps working for the cache lifetime or longer. On top of that, revocation is a manual action requiring someone to notice a compromise and act, so its latency is measured in the time to detect, which is usually the long pole. A short lifetime makes the question moot: a certificate that expires in an hour is self-revoking, needs no distribution mechanism, no verifier participation and no human decision, and the exposure from a leaked key is bounded by construction. That is why the industry has moved this way generally, with public certificate lifetimes falling steadily for exactly this reasoning. Two qualifications worth keeping. You still want a revocation path for the rare severe case, particularly for intermediates and for anything long-lived you could not eliminate — a plan you never use is cheap. And short lifetimes shift the dependency onto the issuer's availability, so the issuance service becomes critical infrastructure that needs redundancy and monitoring; that is a real trade, and it is a better one because an issuance outage is visible and fixable, whereas revocation silently not working is neither.

Recap

  1. The bootstrap problem is the whole difficulty: how a workload gets its first credential without one being baked in.
  2. The platform that placed the workload can attest to it, which is what removes the pre-existing secret.
  3. A workload identity is not a secret that exists before the workload does.
  4. Automation and inventory decide outages, not lifetime. Retries before expiry are the mechanism.
  5. Automate, verify the retries, then shorten. The reverse order is genuinely dangerous.
  6. Monitor the issuer, or a failure breaks nothing for one lifetime and then everything at once.
  7. Root offline, intermediates online, and plan the intermediate rotation before you need it.
  8. Overlap validity when rotating, so no instant exists where half the estate cannot talk to the other half.
  9. Prefer expiry to revocation — soft-fail makes revocation unreliable in practice.
  10. Short lifetimes bound leaked-key exposure and force automation — that is their real value.
  11. Put unavoidable static keys behind one proxy, and write them down as owned, dated exceptions.
