What DevSecOps changes

The name is unfortunate and the idea underneath it is sound: security work should happen where software is made, at the speed software is made, by the people making it. This chapter is what that means concretely, and why the two things it depends on — short feedback loops and low friction — are the whole of it.

1.1The problem it responds to

The arrangement DevSecOps replaced was a gate at the end: teams built for months, then a security review happened, then findings came back. It failed for reasons that are structural rather than cultural.

Why the end-of-line review does not work
ProblemConsequence
The findings arrive after the decisionsAn architectural flaw found in week twenty was fixable in week one and is now a rewrite. The review can only recommend accepting it
The reviewer is outnumberedA handful of security people against hundreds of engineers shipping continuously. The queue is the bottleneck, and it is not fixable by hiring
Release pressure winsFindings raised the week before a launch get accepted as risks. Everyone knows this, which makes the review theatre
Nobody learnsThe team receives a list, fixes it, and produces the same defects next quarter, because the feedback arrived detached from the act that caused it
It creates an adversarySecurity becomes the function that says no at the worst moment, so teams route around it — and the workaround is always worse than the control
"Shift left" is a slogan for something specific

It does not mean "do security earlier" as a matter of enthusiasm. It means shortening the interval between causing a defect and learning about it, so that the person who wrote it is still holding the context needed to fix it and still able to change the decision that produced it. Everything in this unit is downstream of that: automated checks in the editor and the pull request, guardrails that refuse a bad configuration at deploy, and a team that owns the security of what it ships. When shift-left fails, it is almost always because someone moved the gate earlier without shortening the loop — which produces the same queue, now blocking earlier.

1.2Why feedback latency is the mechanism

You will see a chart claiming a defect costs one unit to fix in design and a hundred in production. Treat the specific multipliers with suspicion — they trace back to a small number of studies from the 1970s and 1980s, the underlying data is thin, and the numbers get repeated far more confidently than they were ever established. The direction is real and the mechanism is worth understanding properly, because it tells you what to optimise.

What a slow feedback loop actually costs

The mechanism is context loss and rework scope, not a magic multiplier.

1.3Friction decides whether a control exists

The second mechanism, and the one that is consistently underestimated. Saltzer and Schroeder called it psychological acceptability; in a delivery context it has a sharper form: a control that slows people down gets bypassed, and a bypassed control has zero coverage regardless of how good it is.

Friction against coverage

A control's real coverage is how good it is multiplied by how often it is actually used.

Enforcing a control you have not made cheap

The instinctive response to bypassing is to remove the bypass. That works only if the control is fast and accurate; if it is slow or noisy, removing the escape hatch does not produce compliance, it produces a queue, an exception process that becomes rubber-stamped, and a team that treats the security function as an obstacle to be managed. The order that works is the reverse: make it fast, make it accurate, then make it mandatory. A check that takes eight seconds and is almost always right does not need enforcement — people leave it on because it is useful, and that is a far more durable state than compliance.

The phrase that captures it is that security becomes a platform function rather than a review function. The productive question changes from "is this change safe?" — which does not scale past a few hundred changes a week — to "is the default path safe, and how many teams are on it?" A team using vetted base images, a vetted service template, a vetted CI configuration and vetted infrastructure modules is inheriting decisions made once by people who thought about them, which is an enormously better position than each team reasoning independently and correctly.

Why be sceptical of the "100× more expensive in production" chart?

Because the specific numbers are far weaker evidence than their ubiquity suggests, and treating them as measurements leads to bad decisions. They originate largely in work from the 1970s and 1980s on waterfall projects at a small number of organisations, with methodology that would not survive modern scrutiny — small samples, inconsistent definitions of what counted as a defect or a phase, and no controls for the obvious confounder that defects found late are systematically different defects, being the harder ones that survived earlier detection. The figures have since been repeated so widely that they acquired the appearance of a settled result, and they are frequently cited with a precision nobody ever established. That said, the direction is real and worth acting on, and it is better to argue it from the mechanism than from a chart: a late finding costs more because the author has lost context and must reconstruct it, because other work has accumulated on top of the flawed decision, and because the process overhead of a production fix is genuinely larger than a comment on a pull request. Arguing from mechanism also tells you what to optimise, which the chart does not. It implies that loop latency matters more than coverage — a check running in eight seconds catching 60% of a class beats a nightly one catching 95%, because the fast one is acted on while the author is still in the file. A chart that only says "earlier is cheaper" would suggest moving the same slow review earlier, which reproduces the queue.

A security scan takes fifteen minutes in CI and developers have started skipping it. Do you remove the skip flag?

Not first, because removing the escape hatch on a control you have not made cheap converts a coverage problem into a delivery problem and an organisational fight. Work out what the fifteen minutes is buying and what it is costing: at forty engineers hitting it several times a day, the aggregate is substantial, and the skipping is a rational response to a control whose cost they experience and whose benefit they do not. Three things to try before enforcement. Make it fast: most slow scanners are slow because they analyse everything every time, and incremental analysis of the diff, caching, and running the deep scan asynchronously rather than blocking the merge frequently reduce the blocking portion to seconds. Make it accurate: a scan with a high false positive rate teaches people that its output is noise, and once that lesson is learned they will skip it even when it becomes fast — tuning precision is usually a bigger win than tuning speed. Split it: a fast high-confidence subset blocks the merge, and the slow comprehensive pass runs nightly against the main branch and raises tickets. Then, once it is quick and trusted, make it mandatory — and expect that step to be uncontroversial, because a control people find useful does not need enforcing. The general principle is the ordering: fast, then accurate, then mandatory. Doing it in the other order produces a rubber-stamped exception process and a security function that teams manage around, which is a worse position than the one you started in.

What does it mean for security to be a platform function rather than a review function?

It means the security team's output is systems that make the safe path the easy path, rather than opinions on individual changes. The review model does not scale: a handful of security engineers cannot examine hundreds of changes a week, so the queue becomes the bottleneck, reviews get skipped under release pressure, and the team's calendar fills with work whose value expires the moment the change ships. The platform model produces artefacts that keep working without anyone present — vetted base images so that container hardening is inherited rather than re-derived, service templates with authentication and logging already wired in, infrastructure modules with secure defaults, a CI configuration that includes the checks, guardrails that refuse a dangerous configuration at deploy, and libraries that make the safe call the obvious one. Each of those is built once and applies to every team that adopts it, including teams the security function has never met. Two consequences follow. The metric changes: measuring a security team on findings raised rewards them for a world with more defects, whereas measuring them on adoption of the paved road and on the disappearance of whole defect classes rewards them for making findings unnecessary. And recurring findings become their problem, not the team's — the same defect appearing across five teams is evidence that the default path is wrong, and fixing the template removes it everywhere at once. Review does not disappear entirely; it becomes reserved for genuinely novel or high-risk designs, which is a volume a small team can actually handle well.

Recap

  1. The end-of-line review fails structurally, not culturally — findings arrive after the decisions they should have influenced.
  2. Shift left means shortening the loop, not moving the gate. A gate moved earlier is the same queue, blocking sooner.
  3. The 100× chart is folklore. Argue from context loss, accumulated dependency and process overhead instead.
  4. Optimise loop latency over coverage. Eight seconds at 60% beats overnight at 95%.
  5. A bypassed control has zero coverage, however good it is.
  6. Fast, then accurate, then mandatory. Enforcing an expensive control produces exceptions, not compliance.
  7. Delivery teams own the security of what they ship; the security team owns the systems that make that achievable.
  8. Do not measure a security team on findings raised — it rewards a world with more defects.
  9. A recurring defect across teams is a broken default, not five careless teams.
  10. The question becomes "is the default path safe, and who is on it?" — which scales, unlike reviewing each change.
