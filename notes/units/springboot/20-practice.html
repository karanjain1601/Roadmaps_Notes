    Things worth building

    An order API, end to end

    The running example of this unit. Records at the boundary, JPA underneath, one advice class for errors, a resource server for auth, Testcontainers in CI, a layered image with a preStop hook. Small enough for a weekend and it touches sixteen of the twenty chapters.

    Cause every JPA exception on purpose

    LazyInitializationException, MultipleBagFetchException, OptimisticLockException, UnexpectedRollbackException, a detached-merge null overwrite. Turn on the SQL log and watch each one arrive. An afternoon here is worth more than re-reading chapters 9 to 11.

    Prove the self-invocation bug to yourself

    Write the two-method class from chapter 6, insert five hundred rows, throw on the last one, and look at the table. Then move the method to another bean and do it again. Nothing else makes the proxy real in the same way.

    Measure your own test suite

    Turn on logging.level.org.springframework.test.context.cache: debug, count the contexts, then consolidate to a shared base class. Most suites drop by half, and you will know exactly why.

    Break the deploy, then fix it

    Deploy under load with neither server.shutdown: graceful nor a preStop hook and watch the 502s. Add them one at a time and watch the number fall. Chapter 19 in ten minutes of actual evidence.

    Write one auto-configuration

    A small internal starter with @AutoConfiguration, @ConditionalOnMissingBean, properties and an AutoConfiguration.imports file — then test it with ApplicationContextRunner. The framework stops being magic permanently.

    Where to go from here

    Read Spring's own source

    spring-boot-autoconfigure for conditions, TransactionInterceptor for proxies, SimpleJpaRepository for Spring Data. All shorter than expected, and they are the best worked examples available.

    One book on persistence

    Vlad Mihalcea's High-Performance Java Persistence. Chapters 8 to 11 here are a summary of what it covers properly, and JPA is where the difficulty in Spring actually lives.

    Go wider

    The Backend Developer unit for the framework-neutral concepts, System Design for what happens past one service, and DevOps for the platform underneath chapter 19.

    Be on call for it

    Chapters 15 to 19 exist because people learned them at three in the morning. Owning the pager for something you built is the fastest route to caring about probe groups and pool sizes.

    The short version of the whole unit
    1. Everything is a container, a conditional bean, or a proxy. Ask which.
    2. Constructor injection, always. It buys you fast tests and honest dependencies.
    3. Run --debug once. Auto-configuration stops being magic.
    4. A @Transactional method called from the same class does nothing.
    5. Map every association LAZY and decide fetching per query.
    6. Project on read paths. Fetch entities when you intend to modify them.
    7. Count your queries in a test, or the N+1 will ship.
    8. Checked exceptions commit. Say rollbackFor or make them unchecked.
    9. Set open-in-view: false and treat the exceptions as a map.
    10. instances × pool size must stay under the database's limit.
    11. Security rules are ordered, and a broad rule above a specific one silences it.
    12. Validate the token's audience. Nothing else will.
    13. A scheduled job runs on every instance you deploy.
    14. graceful and preStop. Both, or you drop requests on every release.

    That is the unit. Twenty chapters, from what the word "Spring" means to a pod terminating cleanly under load. The remaining distance is closed by building and operating something real.
