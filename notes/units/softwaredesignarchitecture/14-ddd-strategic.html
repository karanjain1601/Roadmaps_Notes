Domain-driven design: strategic

The tactical patterns get all the attention and the strategic half is where the value is. One idea does most of the work: there is no single correct model of a business, and trying to build one produces the sprawling shared model that makes large systems unchangeable. Bounded contexts are the alternative, and everything else here is a consequence of them.

~30 min read Language · contexts · subdomains · maps 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapters 8, 9

14.1The problem DDD is answering

A system starts with a Customer class. Sales needs a lead score on it. Billing needs a VAT number and payment terms. Support needs a ticket history and a satisfaction rating. Shipping needs delivery preferences and an address that has been validated by a carrier. Marketing needs consent flags per channel.

Eighteen months later Customer has ninety fields, five teams change it, most consumers use a tenth of it, and no one can alter anything without checking with everyone. It sits at maximum afferent coupling and zero abstractness — the zone of pain — and it got there one reasonable pull request at a time.

The insight

The mistake was assuming there is one Customer. There is not. Sales, billing, support and shipping each have a coherent, complete and different notion of what a customer is, and each is correct within its own work. The unified model is not a better model — it is the union of five models with the contradictions papered over, and papering over contradictions is what makes it fragile.

14.2Ubiquitous language

The first practice, and the cheapest: the code uses the same words the domain experts use, and the domain experts use words the code can support. Not a translation layer between "business terms" and "technical terms" — the same terms, in conversation, in the tests, in the class names.

Why it earns its place: every translation step is a place meaning is lost, and the losses are silent. When a developer hears "cancel an order" and implements setStatus("cancelled"), the fact that cancellation after dispatch is a recall with different consequences is lost — and it will surface as a defect eighteen months later. Shared vocabulary makes that gap visible during the conversation, which is enormously cheaper.

Signals that the language is not shared
SymptomWhat it means
Developers say "record", experts say "policy"Two vocabularies; every conversation has a translation step
A class called OrderData or UserInfoNo domain concept was found, so a technical noun was used
Experts cannot read a test name and say whether it is rightThe tests do not describe the domain, so they cannot be reviewed by the people who know it
The same word means different things in two meetingsYou have found a context boundary — see §14.3
A concept exists in conversation but not in the codeMissing abstraction; it is implicit in flags and conditionals
A concept exists in code but not in conversationAccidental complexity, or a leaked technical detail

The last two are the useful diagnostic pair. If experts routinely talk about a "provisional booking" and the code has booking.confirmed === false, the concept is real and unnamed — and naming it usually collapses several conditionals into one type.

14.3Bounded contexts

A bounded context is an explicit boundary within which a model applies and its terms have one agreed meaning. Outside it, the same word may mean something different, and that is fine — it is expected, and it is the point.

One word, four models

What "customer" means in each context. Notice how little overlaps, and that the shared part is essentially an identifier.

Three consequences worth stating explicitly, because each contradicts an instinct people bring to modelling:

  1. Duplication across contexts is correct. Sales having a Customer and billing having a Customer is not a DRY violation — they are different concepts that share a name. DRY is about knowledge, and these are different pieces of knowledge.
  2. The shared part is usually just an identity. Contexts agree that customer c_8f21 exists; they agree on almost nothing else. That makes integration a matter of exchanging identifiers and a few facts, not of sharing a model.
  3. A context boundary is a deployment and team boundary in waiting. If you ever split into services, these are the lines. If you never do, they are still the lines along which a modular monolith should be organised.

14.4Subdomains, and where to spend effort

A bounded context is a solution-space concept — a boundary you draw in the software. A subdomain is a problem-space concept — a part of the business. They usually align one to one, and the reason to distinguish them is that subdomains come in three kinds that deserve very different investment.

Where the design effort belongs
KindWhat it isExamplesStrategy
CoreWhat makes this business different from its competitors. The reason the software existsA logistics firm's routing; an insurer's underwriting; a marketplace's matchingBuild it. Your best people. Rich model, deep collaboration with experts, and it is worth refactoring repeatedly
SupportingNecessary and specific to you, but not a differentiatorA bespoke approvals workflow; internal reporting; a partner onboarding flowBuild it simply. Transaction scripts and CRUD are fine. Do not gold-plate
GenericEvery business needs it and nobody wins by doing it betterAuthentication, payments, email delivery, accounting, search infrastructureBuy it. Integrate behind an anti-corruption layer. Custom-building these is where budgets die
The most expensive mistake in this chapter

Applying the full tactical machinery — aggregates, domain events, repositories, rich models — uniformly across all three kinds. It is right for core, wasteful for supporting, and actively harmful for generic, where the honest answer is usually a vendor. Meanwhile teams frequently do the reverse: they build a bespoke authentication system with an elegant model and leave the routing algorithm — the thing the company actually sells — as a 3 000-line procedure nobody dares touch. Identify the core subdomain first, and check that it is where your design effort is going.

14.5Context maps

Contexts must integrate, and how they integrate is a design decision with organisational consequences. Evans catalogued nine relationships; they are best read as a spectrum of coupling plus a statement about who has power.

The nine relationships

The anti-corruption layer, since it is the one you will use most

An ACL is a translation layer at a context boundary that converts a foreign model into yours, and refuses to let foreign concepts through. It is the adapter pattern promoted to an architectural role, and its value is not convenience but containment: without it, the legacy system's or vendor's concepts spread into your domain, and eventually your model is theirs.

What the layer actually does 
Vendor's model                ACL                    Your model
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CustomerRecord         translate identity     Customer (id only)
  .cust_type = "B2"    map codes to concepts    .tier = Tier.Business
  .flags = 0x0C        decode bit flags         .canInvoice = true
  .addr1..addr4        parse into a value obj   .address: Address
  .status = 7          map to your states       .state = Active
  .balance_pence       wrap in a type           .balance: Money

Rules the layer enforces:
  · Vendor types never appear outside this package.
  · Unmappable values fail loudly here, not silently downstream.
  · Your model has no field that exists only because the vendor has it.
  · When the vendor changes, exactly one package changes.

Which integration relationship?

The choice follows from power, model quality and how many consumers there are.

14.6Finding the boundaries

The hard part is not the theory but knowing where to draw the lines. Five techniques, in rough order of usefulness.

Listen for the same word meaning different things
The single most reliable signal. When two groups use "shipment" or "account" or "policy" and mean different things, you have found a boundary and it will hold. Conversely, when a word means exactly one thing everywhere, you are inside one context.
Event storming
Put domain experts in a room and write every significant business event on a sticky note, in past tense, along a timeline: order placed, payment authorised, stock reserved, shipment dispatched, delivery confirmed. Then cluster them. Clusters that share vocabulary and actors are candidate contexts, and the pivotal events — where the language changes and a different group takes over — are candidate boundaries.
Follow the organisation, cautiously
Conway's law says the system will mirror the communication structure whether you plan it or not. Aligning contexts with teams makes ownership clear and reduces coordination. The caution: organisations reorganise for reasons unrelated to the domain, so align with stable structures, and be willing to say that a proposed reorg cuts across a real boundary.
Look at what changes together
The cohesion test, applied at scale. Pull the change history: if two areas are always in the same commit, they are one context; if an area is only ever touched by one team for one class of reason, it is its own.
Follow the data ownership
Every piece of data should have exactly one context that owns it and can change it; everyone else holds a copy or a reference. When you cannot say who owns a field, the boundary is wrong or missing.
Getting it wrong is normal and survivable

You will not find the right boundaries first time, because you do not yet understand the domain — and understanding is what the exercise produces. The practical guidance is to start with fewer, larger contexts and split as the seams become clear, because merging two contexts is far easier than splitting one that has grown across a bad line. This is also the strongest argument for a modular monolith before microservices: the boundary is real and enforced, but moving it is a refactoring rather than a migration.

14.7When not to do this

DDD is a substantial investment — in expert time, in modelling effort, in structure that a small system does not need. It is worth being direct about where it does not pay.

Fit
Worth itNot worth it
Complex business rules that experts argue aboutCRUD over forms, where the rules are field validation
A long-lived system that will be changed for yearsA prototype, an internal tool, a migration script
Domain experts available to talk to regularlyNobody to model with, so the "ubiquitous language" would be invented by developers
Several teams needing clear ownership boundariesThree people who all know everything
A genuine core subdomain worth differentiating onEverything you are building is generic and buyable

And note that the strategic half is far cheaper than the tactical half. Ubiquitous language and bounded contexts are worth adopting in almost any system of moderate size, including ones where aggregates and domain events would be overkill — they cost conversation and module structure rather than code.

Two contexts both have a Product class with overlapping fields. Is that duplication to be removed?

Almost certainly not. Apply the DRY test from Chapter 9: if a business rule changed, would both have to change? For a catalogue Product (name, description, images, categorisation, search keywords) and a warehouse Product (SKU, dimensions, weight, hazard class, storage requirements), the answer is no — merchandising and logistics change these for entirely separate reasons, on separate schedules, at separate people's request. Sharing a class would couple two teams through a file neither fully owns, and the overlap will shrink over time as each side grows fields the other ignores. What they genuinely share is an identity: both agree that product p_44b1 exists. So the integration is an identifier plus, at most, a handful of facts published as events — not a shared type. The one case where merging is right is when the two are genuinely the same concept and the "two contexts" were drawn wrongly, which is worth checking, but the symptom of that is not overlapping fields; it is that every change to one requires a change to the other.

Your team spent six months building a custom authentication system with an elegant domain model. What went wrong strategically?

Effort went into a generic subdomain. Authentication is something every business needs and no business wins by doing better — customers do not choose you because your session handling is elegant. Six months of your best modelling attention produced something you could have bought or adopted from an open-source provider in days, and you now own it forever: the security patches, the compliance requirements, the password reset edge cases, the eventual passkey migration. Meanwhile the core subdomain — whatever your company actually differentiates on — got whatever attention was left. The corrective is a subdomain classification exercise: list every context, mark each core, supporting or generic, and then compare that against where engineering time went last quarter. The mismatch is usually stark, and the reason is that generic subdomains are technically interesting and well-specified while core subdomains are ambiguous and require arguing with domain experts. The right response to a generic subdomain is to buy it and put an anti-corruption layer in front so the vendor's model does not leak into yours.

When is "conformist" the right relationship rather than an anti-corruption layer?

When the upstream model is good enough for your purposes and the translation layer would cost more than it saves. Conformist means you adopt their model wholesale — their types, their vocabulary, their concepts flow into your code — and in exchange you write no translation and stay automatically compatible when they change. That is a reasonable trade in three situations: the upstream is a well-designed industry standard you would have modelled similarly anyway; your context is small and shallow, so there is not much of your own model to protect; or the relationship is temporary and you plan to replace it. It is the wrong choice when the upstream model is legacy or vendor-shaped, when your context is a core subdomain whose model you need to control, or when the upstream changes frequently and their changes would ripple through your whole codebase. The failure mode to watch is drifting into conformism by default — starting with "we'll just use their DTOs for now" and finding two years later that your domain speaks the vendor's language and cannot be separated from it.

Recap

  1. There is no single correct model of a business. The unified model is the union of several with the contradictions hidden.
  2. Ubiquitous language means one vocabulary, shared by code and experts, with no translation step.
  3. A concept in conversation but not in code is a missing abstraction; a concept in code but not in conversation is accidental complexity.
  4. A bounded context is where a model applies and its terms have one meaning. Duplication across contexts is correct.
  5. What contexts share is usually an identity, which makes integration an exchange of ids and facts rather than of models.
  6. Core, supporting, generic. Build, build simply, buy — and check that your effort is going to the core.
  7. Nine context-map relationships, with anti-corruption layer as the default for anything you do not control.
  8. Boundaries are found by listening for a word that changes meaning, by event storming, by what changes together, and by data ownership.
  9. Start with fewer, larger contexts and split as seams appear — merging is easier than splitting.
  10. The strategic half is cheap and widely applicable; the tactical half is not, and belongs in the core subdomain.
