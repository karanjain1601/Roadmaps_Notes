Coupling and cohesion

Two words from 1974 that still describe the whole problem: how much a module depends on others, and how much its own contents belong together. This chapter gives them the precision they usually lack — the graded scales, connascence as the sharper modern replacement, and the metrics that make component-level coupling something you can actually plot.

~32 min read Coupling scale · cohesion scale · connascence · metrics 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapters 1, 5, 7

8.1The two properties

Coupling is the strength of the connection between modules: how much you must know about B to change A, and how likely it is that changing B forces a change in A. Cohesion is how strongly the contents of one module belong together: whether its parts serve one purpose or were merely placed in the same file.

The standard advice — low coupling, high cohesion — is right and unhelpfully vague, so two clarifications are worth making up front.

  1. They are the same property at two scales. High cohesion within a module means the connections are inside; low coupling means few connections across. Improving one usually improves the other, because moving related things together simultaneously removes cross-module links. When they conflict, prefer cohesion: a module that holds everything about one concept is easier to work with than a scattering of technically-decoupled fragments.
  2. Zero coupling is not the goal. Modules that do not interact do not form a system. The goal is that the couplings you have are weak, few, explicit and pointed in a chosen direction. A design with one clear dependency per module is better than one with none, because the second is not a design.

8.2The coupling scale

Constantine and Yourdon graded coupling from worst to best. The grades still map onto real code, and naming the grade turns "this feels tangled" into a specific finding.

1ContentOne module reaches into another's internals — private fields, monkey-patching, reflection into implementation. The callee cannot change anything without breaking the caller.
2CommonBoth share global mutable state. Any writer can affect any reader, invisibly and at a distance.
3ExternalBoth depend on an externally imposed format or protocol — a file layout, a wire format, a shared database schema. Changing it means coordinating deployments.
4ControlOne passes a flag telling the other what to do, so the caller knows the callee's internal branches. Boolean parameters live here.
5StampA whole structure is passed where only two fields are used. The caller is now coupled to fields it does not care about.
6DataOnly the data actually needed is passed, as parameters. The normal, healthy case.
7MessageCommunication only by message or event, with no shared types beyond the message. The loosest form, and the one distributed systems force on you.

Two of these deserve comment. External coupling through a shared database is the one that most often goes unnoticed, because it does not appear in any import graph — two services with no code dependency can be unable to deploy independently because they both read the same column. And stamp coupling is the argument for passing (price, quantity) rather than the whole Order: the narrower signature makes the dependency honest, makes the function testable with two numbers, and stops it quietly starting to use a third field later.

8.3The cohesion scale

1CoincidentalParts are unrelated. Utils, Helpers, Common — grouped because they had nowhere else to go.
2LogicalParts are the same category of thing but do different jobs — "all the validators", "all the handlers" — and are selected by a flag.
3TemporalParts run at the same time and share nothing else. initialise() doing eight unrelated set-up steps.
4ProceduralParts run in a required order, but operate on unrelated data.
5CommunicationalParts operate on the same data. Getting closer — the data is the reason they belong together.
6SequentialEach part's output is the next part's input. A pipeline; the connection is real.
7FunctionalEvery part contributes to exactly one well-defined task. The module has a name that describes it with no "and".
The one-sentence test

Describe what the module does without using "and" and without listing. "Prices an order" is functional cohesion. "Prices an order and sends the confirmation and updates stock" is three modules in a trench coat. "Contains helper functions used in several places" is coincidental — the lowest grade, and the most common name for it is utils.ts.

8.4Connascence: the sharper tool

The 1974 scales are useful but coarse — they grade a whole relationship on a single dimension. Meilir Page-Jones's connascence is more precise, and it is the vocabulary worth adopting. Two pieces of code are connascent if changing one would require changing the other to preserve correctness. That is coupling restated, but the taxonomy is finer and each kind has a known way to weaken it.

The taxonomy is ordered: name is weakest, identity is strongest, and static is always weaker than dynamic because a compiler or a search can find it. That ordering makes refactoring directional — you can always ask "can I convert this to a weaker form?" and the answers are in the tables above.

The three properties, and the three rules

Any instance of connascence has three attributes, and they interact:

  • Strength — how hard it is to detect and change. Name is easy; identity is not.
  • Degree — how many places are involved. Two callers agreeing on a positional order is minor; two hundred is not.
  • Locality — how far apart they are. Strong connascence within one small class is fine. Weak connascence spread across a codebase can still be a problem at high degree.

Which gives Page-Jones's three rules, and they are the most actionable thing in this chapter:

  1. Minimise overall connascence by breaking the system into encapsulated elements.
  2. Minimise connascence that crosses boundaries — this is what "low coupling" means, made precise.
  3. Maximise connascence within boundaries — this is what "high cohesion" means, and it explains why strong connascence inside one class is not a defect. Two fields that must change together should be in the same object, precisely so that the strong link is short.

8.5Measuring coupling between components

At the component level — packages, modules, deployable units — coupling becomes countable, and a small set of metrics from Robert Martin turns architecture into something you can plot on a graph.

The four numbers 
Ca  afferent coupling   things OUTSIDE that depend on this component
                        → high Ca means many people rely on you:
                          you are hard to change, i.e. STABLE

Ce  efferent coupling   things INSIDE that depend on the outside
                        → high Ce means you rely on many people:
                          you are easily forced to change, i.e. UNSTABLE

I   instability         I = Ce / (Ca + Ce)         range 0 … 1
                        0 = maximally stable, 1 = maximally unstable

A   abstractness        A = abstract types / all types    range 0 … 1

D   distance            D = | A + I − 1 |
                        0 = on the "main sequence", the healthy line

The insight linking them: a component that many others depend on should be abstract, and a component that depends on many others should be concrete. Stability and abstractness should move together. Plotting A against I produces a square with a diagonal running from (A=1, I=0) to (A=0, I=1), and the two corners off that line are both pathological.

The two bad corners

Zone of uselessnessA ≈ 1, I ≈ 1Abstract and nobody depends on it. Interfaces with no implementations, or an abstraction layer nothing usesSpeculative abstractions; leftover interfaces after a refactor

Two principles that follow

Stable dependencies principle
Depend in the direction of stability. A component should only depend on components more stable than itself — lower I. If a volatile component is depended upon by a stable one, every change to the volatile thing propagates upstream into something that was supposed to be settled. When you find such an edge, invert it with an interface owned by the stable side, which is dependency inversion doing architectural work.
Stable abstractions principle
A stable component should be abstract, so that it can be extended without being modified. This is what keeps a heavily-depended-upon component from becoming rigid: everything depends on it, but what they depend on is an interface, so new behaviour arrives as new implementations rather than as edits.

8.6What to actually do about it

Diagnosis is easier than treatment, so a short list of moves that reliably reduce coupling, in rough order of how often they apply:

  1. Narrow the signature. Pass the two fields rather than the whole object — stamp coupling becomes data coupling, and the function's real dependencies become visible.
  2. Replace a flag with two functions. Control coupling becomes none.
  3. Name the magic value. Connascence of meaning becomes connascence of name, which tooling can handle.
  4. Combine values that change together. Connascence of value becomes internal to one object, satisfying rule 3.
  5. Introduce an interface owned by the caller. Points the dependency where you want it, per Chapter 7.
  6. Move the code to the data. Feature envy is coupling that disappears entirely when the method moves to the class it keeps reaching into.
  7. Replace shared state with a parameter or a message. Common coupling is the worst grade that is genuinely easy to fix, and usually the fix is passing the thing explicitly.
  8. Duplicate, deliberately. Two modules sharing a helper for incidental reasons are coupled for no benefit. Two copies that can diverge are sometimes the correct answer — see Chapter 9 on DRY.
The shared-utility trap

A common or shared package is where coupling goes to hide. Everything depends on it, so it sits at I ≈ 0; it is full of concrete helpers, so A ≈ 0; which puts it in the zone of pain by construction. It also accumulates, because "shared" is the answer to "where do I put this?" whenever the answer is unclear. Two habits keep it under control: a shared module must have a name describing a concept (money, dates, http) rather than a role (common), and code used by exactly two modules is not shared code — it belongs in one of them, or duplicated, until a third case shows what it really is.

Two services have no code dependency but cannot be deployed independently. What kind of coupling is that?

External coupling, almost certainly through a shared database schema or a shared message format — and it is the most dangerous grade in practice precisely because no tool shows it. In connascence terms it is usually connascence of meaning or algorithm across deployable units, at high degree and terrible locality: both sides agree on what a column means or how a payload is parsed, and neither can change unilaterally. The fixes, in ascending order of effort: version the shared format and support both versions during a transition, so deployments decouple in time; give each service its own tables and replicate between them, converting a shared schema into an owned one plus a feed; or make one side the owner and the other a consumer of an explicit, versioned API rather than of the storage. The general point is that shared data is a shared interface whether you designed it as one or not.

Why is strong connascence inside one class acceptable?

Because locality is one of the three properties, and it moderates strength. Connascence of value — two fields that must always change together, such as a start and end date — is strong, but if both fields are private to one small class, then everything that must agree is within a few lines, visible at once, and changeable in one edit by one person. That is not a design problem; it is what a class is for. Page-Jones's third rule states this directly: maximise connascence within encapsulation boundaries. The same connascence of value spread across four modules, where each independently maintains its own copy of the relationship, is a serious defect — same strength, catastrophic locality. So the productive question is never "is this coupling strong?" but "is this strong coupling short?", and the standard fix for strong-and-distant is to gather the participants into one object so the link becomes strong-and-local.

Your shared/utils package has 200 functions and every module imports it. What do you do?

Do not try to fix it in one pass. Start by measuring: for each function, how many modules actually call it? The distribution will be extreme — a handful used everywhere, a long tail used once or twice. Then treat the two ends differently. The long tail is not shared code at all: move each function into its single caller, which removes it from the shared surface entirely and is a pure win with no design debate. For the genuinely shared head, group by concept and extract named modules — money, dates, strings — each with a coherent purpose and an interface you would defend. Anything left over that fits no concept is the real question, and the honest answer is usually that it belongs to a domain concept nobody has named yet. Meanwhile, add the rule that nothing new goes into utils: the package stops growing on the day you decide it does, and everything after that is gradual.

Recap

  1. Cohesion and coupling are one property at two scales. When they conflict, prefer cohesion.
  2. Zero coupling is not the goal — few, weak, explicit and directed is.
  3. The coupling scale runs content, common, external, control, stamp, data, message. External coupling through a shared schema is the one no tool shows you.
  4. The cohesion test: describe the module without "and" and without listing.
  5. Connascence is the sharper vocabulary: name, type, meaning, position, algorithm — then execution, timing, value, identity, which are all worse because they are invisible until runtime.
  6. Strength, degree, locality. Strong-and-local is fine; weak-and-everywhere can still hurt.
  7. Minimise overall, minimise across boundaries, maximise within. Those three rules are "low coupling, high cohesion" made actionable.
  8. I = Ce/(Ca+Ce), A = abstract/total, D = |A + I − 1|. Depend towards stability; make stable components abstract.
  9. Concrete and depended-upon is the zone of pain, which is precisely where a shared/utils package sits.
