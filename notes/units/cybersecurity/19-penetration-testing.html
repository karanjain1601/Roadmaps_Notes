Penetration testing

Testing your own defences by attacking them, under authorisation and with a scope. The technical work is the part everyone pictures and the least of what determines value — scoping decides what can be found at all, and the report decides whether anything changes.

~28 min read Scoping · methodology · phases · reporting 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: Parts 2 and 4

19.1The assurance ladder

Five distinct activities that get called "a pen test" and answer different questions. Buying the wrong one is the most common way organisations waste this budget.

What each actually answers
ActivityQuestionEffortLimitation
Vulnerability scanWhat known issues are present?Hours, automated, continuousNo chaining, no logic flaws, false positives
Vulnerability assessmentThe same, verified and prioritised by a humanDaysStill breadth-first; does not prove exploitability
Penetration testCan these findings be chained into real impact?1–3 weeks per scopeBounded by scope and time; a sample, not coverage
Red team engagementWould we detect and stop a determined adversary pursuing a goal?Weeks to months, expensiveTests the whole organisation, and needs maturity to be worth it
Purple team exerciseDoes each specific detection actually fire?Days, repeatableNot adversarial; measures coverage rather than realism — ch 20
Buy the one that matches your maturity

A red team engagement against an organisation with no detection capability produces a predictable and expensive report saying they got in and nobody noticed — which you could have established for free. The sequence that gets value: fix what a scanner finds, then get a penetration test to see what chains together, then build detection, then run purple team exercises to verify the detections fire, and only then commission a red team to test whether the whole apparatus holds against someone trying. Buying a red team first is the security equivalent of hiring an examiner before doing any revision.

19.2Scoping

What will this test find?

Scope decisions determine the findings before anyone starts. Set the constraints and see what is excluded by construction.

Rules of engagement — the document that keeps everyone out of trouble 
AUTHORISATION
  Signed by someone with the authority to grant it, naming
  the systems, the addresses, the dates and the testers.
  This is the document that distinguishes the work from a
  criminal offence, and it must exist BEFORE anything starts.
  If a third party hosts the systems, their authorisation
  is needed too — yours does not cover their infrastructure.

SCOPE
  In scope: exact addresses, domains, applications, accounts.
  Out of scope: stated explicitly, not left implied.
  Note that "everything on this domain" often includes SaaS
  belonging to someone else.

WINDOWS
  When testing may occur, and when it must not.

RULES
  · Permitted and forbidden techniques
  · Denial of service: usually forbidden
  · Data handling: what may be accessed, exfiltrated,
    retained, and how it is destroyed afterwards
  · Pivoting: whether reaching an out-of-scope system
    through an in-scope one is permitted

CONTACTS AND STOP CONDITIONS
  A 24-hour contact on both sides, and an agreed signal to
  pause. Define what triggers an immediate stop: evidence
  of a REAL intrusion already in progress, unexpected
  instability, or a finding severe enough to report at once.

DECONFLICTION
  Who knows the test is happening. If the blue team does not,
  agree in advance how to confirm that an alert is you and
  not someone else — a real incident during a test is a
  situation you want a plan for.

19.3Methodology and phases

Structured methodologies exist so that coverage is repeatable rather than dependent on what the tester happens to think of: PTES for general penetration testing, the OWASP Web Security Testing Guide for applications, NIST SP 800-115 as a technical assessment framework, and OSSTMM for a measurement-oriented approach. A test without one produces findings shaped by the tester's habits.

The phases, and what each produces
PhaseWorkOutput
1 PlanningScope, rules, authorisation, success criteriaThe rules of engagement
2 ReconnaissancePassive OSINT, then active enumeration — ch 5An attack surface inventory, often more complete than the client's own
3 Scanning and enumerationServices, versions, users, shares, endpointsA candidate list of weaknesses
4 ExploitationProving that candidates are realVerified findings, with evidence
5 Post-exploitationEscalation, lateral movement, reaching what mattersThe impact story — the part that changes minds
6 ReportingWriting it up for two audiencesThe actual deliverable
7 RetestVerifying the fixes workClosure, and the thing most often skipped
A list of findings is not a penetration test

The difference between an assessment and a test is phase 5. Reporting "SMB signing is disabled" and "this service account has a weak password" as two medium findings buries the actual result, which is that those two together give domain administrator in about twenty minutes. Individually they get scheduled for next quarter; chained, with a screenshot of the domain admin shell, they get fixed this week. Impact is what moves people, and demonstrating the chain is the value a human tester adds over a scanner. Ask for it explicitly when scoping, because a test constrained to "identify vulnerabilities" will give you a scanner's output with a person's rate card.

                sqlmap where injection is suspected

                relay paths from chapter 6; CrackMapExec or

                highest-value single tool against a Windows
                estate; certipy for certificate-service abuse

EXPLOITATION    Metasploit for the commodity path; manual
                exploitation for anything that matters

POST-EXPLOIT    Command-and-control frameworks (Cobalt Strike,
                Sliver, Mythic) for realistic operations

PASSWORDS       hashcat, John — for cracking what you captured,
                offline

PLATFORMS       Kali or Parrot as a distribution, mostly for
                convenience rather than capability

THE HONEST NOTE
  Tools find the commodity findings. The findings worth
  paying for come from understanding the system well enough
  to see what the tool cannot: business logic, trust
  relationships between components, and the chain that turns
  three mediums into a critical. A tester who runs tools and
  transcribes the output is selling you a scan.

19.5The report is the product

Does this finding get fixed?

The same technical issue, written up in different ways. What changes is whether anyone acts.

The structure that works, for two audiences 
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY — one page, no jargon
  · What we tested and what we did not
  · What an attacker could achieve, in business terms
  · The three things to fix first
  · Whether the posture is better or worse than last time

TECHNICAL FINDINGS — one per issue
  · Title, and where it is
  · Risk in context, not just a CVSS number
  · Reproduction: exact steps, request and response,
    a screenshot of the result
  · Impact: what this specifically allows
  · Remediation: specific to THIS system, with a reference
  · An owner, and a suggested priority

ATTACK NARRATIVE — the part that changes behaviour
  A chronological account of the chain: how three medium
  findings became domain admin, with the evidence at each
  step. This is what people read and remember.

AND STATE THE LIMITATIONS
  What was out of scope, what could not be tested, where
  time ran out, and what a longer test would likely cover.
  A report that implies completeness it does not have is
  worse than one that is honest about its bounds.
Why is a black-box test usually worse value than a grey-box one?

Because you pay the tester to spend the first third of the engagement discovering things you could have simply told them, and the realism you buy in exchange is largely illusory. A black-box test withholds architecture, credentials and documentation on the theory that this simulates an external attacker — but a real attacker is not working to a two-week deadline, and they will spend months on reconnaissance you are paying by the day for. The practical result is a test that finds the perimeter issues and runs out of time before reaching the interesting internal ones, so you learn what an attacker with two weeks would find rather than what one with two months would. Grey box gives the tester what a moderately-informed adversary would eventually establish anyway — network diagrams, a low-privilege account, an understanding of what the application does — and spends the whole budget on depth. That surfaces authorisation flaws, privilege escalation paths, business logic problems and chained findings that a black-box test rarely reaches. White box adds source code and configuration, which is the best value per day for application testing specifically, because a reviewer can find in an hour what would take days to discover by probing. Two qualifications. Black box is genuinely appropriate when the question is specifically "what is discoverable from outside", such as an attack surface assessment. And the choice is separable from whether the defenders know: a grey-box test can still be run without telling the security operations team, which preserves the detection-testing value while not wasting time on discovery.

A test report contains 140 findings. Why might that be a problem?

Because a report of that size is unlikely to produce action, and it usually signals something about how the test was conducted. The practical issue is capacity: 140 findings arriving at once exceeds any team's ability to triage, so they get imported into a tracker and worked through in whatever order the severity column suggests — which means the genuinely important chain gets the same treatment as a missing HTTP header. The important finding is not more visible for being in a longer document; it is less visible. The likely causes are worth distinguishing. If most of the findings are scanner output that has been verified and pasted in — missing headers, TLS configuration notes, informational disclosures — then you have bought a vulnerability assessment at penetration test rates, and those items should be in an appendix rather than the body. If they are genuine distinct issues, that itself is a finding about the environment, and the report should say so as a theme rather than enumerating instances. And if the same underlying cause appears forty times across forty hosts, it is one finding with forty affected systems, not forty findings. What a good report does instead is lead with a small number of things that matter, group instances under causes, put the long tail in an appendix clearly marked as such, and include an attack narrative showing how the important ones chain. Three findings that get fixed are worth more than 140 that get imported into a backlog, and the retest — which is the step most often skipped — is what proves which of the two happened.

During a test, the tester finds evidence of a real, ongoing intrusion. What should happen?

Stop, and escalate immediately through the pre-agreed channel — and the fact that this is pre-agreed is the point of the rules of engagement. Testing must pause because continuing does three harmful things: it contaminates the evidence a real investigation will need, it makes the responders' job much harder because they cannot distinguish the tester's activity from the intruder's, and it risks the tester's own actions being attributed to the attacker or vice versa. The escalation has to go to a named contact with authority, using a channel agreed in advance, and it should be out-of-band — if there is an attacker in the email system, that is not where to raise it. This is exactly why the rules of engagement should define stop conditions and a 24-hour contact on both sides rather than leaving it to be worked out in the moment. Several practical points follow. The tester should preserve their own logs and a precise record of everything they did with timestamps, because that record becomes essential for the responders to subtract the test from the timeline. The engagement's own tooling and infrastructure may need to be reviewed, since a tester's command-and-control server appearing in the logs alongside a real one is an obvious source of confusion. Whether testing resumes is the client's decision and usually comes after containment. And this scenario is a good argument for deconfliction planning in general: agreeing in advance how the blue team confirms that an alert is the test rather than something else, because the alternative is either a real intrusion dismissed as "probably the testers" or a full incident response mobilised against an authorised engagement.

Recap

  1. Five different activities get called a pen test. Buy the one matching your maturity, in sequence.
  2. A red team against no detection capability produces an expensive report telling you what you already knew.
  3. Authorisation must exist before anything starts, from someone with the authority — including any third-party host.
  4. Scope decides the findings before anyone touches a keyboard.
  5. Post-exploitation is where the value is. Three mediums chained into domain admin is the finding.
  6. Grey box usually beats black box — you pay for depth rather than for rediscovering your own architecture.
  7. Tools find commodity issues. Logic flaws, trust relationships and chains need a person.
  8. The report is the product, and the attack narrative is the part that changes behaviour.
  9. State the limitations — implied completeness is worse than honest bounds.
  10. Retest, or you never learn whether anything was fixed.
