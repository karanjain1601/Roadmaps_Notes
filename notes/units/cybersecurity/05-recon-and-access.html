Reconnaissance and initial access

Getting in is the stage with the fewest technical requirements and the highest success rate, because it targets the part of the system that cannot be patched. Almost all of it reduces to three routes: someone is persuaded to act, a credential is reused, or something exposed was not supposed to be.

~32 min read OSINT · phishing · credential attacks 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapter 4

5.1What reconnaissance finds

Nearly all of it happens on systems you do not own and cannot monitor, which is why it barely appears in your logs. The productive defensive response is not detection but reducing what is findable — and knowing what is findable, because you should look before they do.

Where the information comes from
SourceYieldsWhat you can do
Certificate transparencyEvery subdomain you have ever issued a certificate for, including dev, staging, vpn-testNothing — CT logs are public and permanent by design. So assume every hostname is known, and never rely on a subdomain being obscure
DNSMail providers, cloud vendors, SaaS in use, sometimes internal namingReview your own records; remove stale entries that point at deprovisioned resources (subdomain takeover)
Job advertsYour exact technology stack, versions, and which teams are short-staffedDescribe skills rather than versions. "Experience with container orchestration" not "Kubernetes 1.24 on RHEL 8"

Attack surface management is the counter

The single most useful exercise: enumerate what you expose, from the outside, on a schedule. Not a penetration test — an inventory. Every domain and subdomain, every certificate, every IP range, every open port, every cloud storage bucket, every SaaS tenant. The finding is almost never "our firewall is misconfigured". It is a forgotten staging server, a decommissioned product's DNS still pointing at a cloud resource someone else can now claim, an S3 bucket from a 2019 campaign, or a developer's test instance on a corporate card. You cannot defend an asset you do not know you have, and the attacker's inventory of you is frequently more complete than your own.

Campaign arithmetic

Why "most people don't click" is not a defence, and where each control actually removes risk.

The control that actually works

Awareness training reduces click rates and has a floor it never gets below, because the people clicking are doing their jobs — recruiters open attachments from strangers, finance receives invoices, support opens whatever a customer sends. Blaming them is both unkind and useless. Phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication is the control that changes the outcome, because FIDO2 and passkeys bind the credential to the origin: a user who is fully deceived, on a perfect replica of your login page, still cannot produce a working credential for the attacker, since the authenticator will not sign for the wrong domain. That defeats the entire credential-harvesting class including real-time proxy kits, which do defeat one-time codes and push approval. Everything else — filtering, training, reporting — reduces volume. This one removes the outcome.

5.3The rest of social engineering

Phishing is one delivery channel for a broader thing: persuading a person to act against their interest. The techniques are the same ones used in legitimate persuasion, which is precisely why they work.

The levers, and what they look like
LeverIn use
Authority"This is the CFO. I need this done before the board call."
UrgencyA deadline that prevents verification. The most reliable single indicator of a social engineering attempt is time pressure that discourages checking
Scarcity"Only for the first fifty" — pushes action before thought
Social proof"Everyone else on the team has already completed this"
Liking and familiarityRapport built over several innocuous messages before any request
ReciprocityA small favour first, then a request
Fear"Your account will be suspended", or a threat of exposure

Beyond messages, the physical and procedural variants: tailgating through a door behind someone; pretexting, an invented scenario that justifies the request — a supplier audit, an IT migration; baiting, leaving media where it will be picked up; shoulder surfing; dumpster diving; and the watering hole, compromising a site the target community visits rather than the target itself.

The help desk is the weak point

A recurring and highly effective route: call the service desk claiming to be an employee who has lost their phone and cannot complete multi-factor authentication. The desk's job is to be helpful, they are measured on resolution time, and the caller has all the public facts — name, manager, office, start date, the last four of an employee number. The result is an MFA reset for the attacker. This has featured in several of the largest recent intrusions. The control is procedural rather than technical: a defined identity-proofing process for credential and MFA resets that does not rely on publicly available facts — an in-person check, a callback to a known number, verification by the person's manager through a separate channel, or a video call against a stored photo. And critically, the desk must be authorised to refuse and supported when they do, because a policy that makes an agent the obstacle to an angry "executive" will not survive contact.

5.4Credential attacks

The other main route in, and increasingly the dominant one: not breaking in but logging in. Four distinct techniques that people frequently conflate.

Four attacks, four different defences
AttackMethodDefeated by
Brute forceMany passwords against one accountAccount lockout, rate limiting — easy to stop and easy to detect
Password sprayingOne or two common passwords against every account, slowlyLockout does not stop it. Needs per-source and per-tenant rate limiting, and detection of the pattern
Credential stuffingUsername and password pairs from other breaches, replayedBreached-password blocking, MFA, impossible-travel detection
Infostealer harvestMalware on a personal or unmanaged device takes saved credentials and session cookiesMFA alone is insufficient — a stolen session cookie is already authenticated. Needs short sessions, device binding, token protection

Why password spraying works

Lockout policies are built for brute force. Spraying is designed to slide underneath them.

5.5The exposed-service route

The third way in, and the one that needs no human at all: something reachable that should not be, or something reachable that is unpatched. Internet-wide scanning means exposure is discovered in minutes, not weeks — a new service on a public IP will receive automated probes almost immediately, and an exploitable one will be found by someone who was not looking for you specifically.

Why does an account lockout policy fail to stop password spraying?

Because lockout counts failures per account, and spraying is designed around exactly that. A brute-force attack throws thousands of passwords at one account, so a threshold of five failures stops it after five attempts. A spray inverts the shape: it tries one password — Autumn2026!, or the company name and a year — against every account in the directory, then waits out the lockout reset window, then tries a second password. From any single account's point of view there has been one failure, which is indistinguishable from a typo, so no account ever locks and the policy never fires. Meanwhile the attacker only needs one success out of thousands of accounts, and in an organisation of any size someone is using a sprayable password. Three things actually help. Detect the right shape: alert on a large number of distinct accounts failing from one source address or in one short window — that is the signal lockout is blind to, and it is a straightforward query over authentication logs you probably already have. Remove the target: block breached and common passwords at the moment a user chooses one, which is far more effective than complexity rules because sprays use exactly the passwords those lists contain. And make a correct password insufficient: phishing-resistant MFA means the one account that does have a weak password still does not yield access. Note also that aggressive lockout has its own cost — an attacker who knows the threshold can lock out your entire directory deliberately, turning a lockout policy into a denial-of-service tool.

Your organisation has MFA on everything and is still compromised through a phishing link. How?

Most likely a real-time reverse proxy, and the important point is that this defeats one-time codes and push approval by design rather than by exploiting a bug. The user visits a link that renders your genuine login page, proxied through infrastructure the attacker controls. Everything the user sees is real, because it is real — the proxy is relaying to your actual identity provider. The user types their password, the provider issues a genuine MFA challenge, the user completes it on their real authenticator, and the provider returns a valid session cookie. The proxy captures that cookie and replays it. Authentication succeeded legitimately; the attacker simply took the result. There is nothing the user could have noticed, because there was nothing wrong with the page. Three other routes reach the same outcome. MFA fatigue: the attacker has the password and sends repeated push prompts until one is approved out of irritation or confusion — number matching largely closes this. Infostealer: malware on an unmanaged personal device exfiltrates saved session cookies, so authentication is not attempted at all. And help desk reset: the attacker enrols their own authenticator by calling support. The defence against the proxy case specifically is phishing-resistant MFA — FIDO2 or passkeys — because the authenticator signs a challenge bound to the origin, and the proxy's domain is not your domain, so no valid assertion can be produced no matter how convinced the user is. Against the cookie-theft cases, the defences are shorter session lifetimes, binding tokens to a device, and continuous evaluation that re-checks conditions rather than trusting a cookie until it expires.

Is there any point running phishing simulations if click rates never reach zero?

Yes, but not for the reason they are usually run, and the way most programmes measure them is actively counterproductive. Click rate is a poor primary metric because it has a floor set by people doing their jobs correctly — a recruiter must open CVs from strangers, accounts payable must open invoices, support must open whatever a customer attaches. Driving that number down means training people to be worse at their work, and reporting it to leadership as a performance measure produces blame rather than security. The metrics worth tracking are different. Report rate — how many people flagged it — is the one that matters, because reporting is the behaviour that shortens dwell time for the real thing, and unlike clicking it has no natural floor. Time to first report is better still: if someone reports within two minutes, your response team can pull the message from every other mailbox before most people have opened it, which turns a campaign into a non-event. And time to containment measures the thing you can actually control. So the simulation's real function is to exercise the reporting and response pipeline, not to grade employees — and a programme that punishes clickers destroys the reporting culture it depends on, because someone who clicked and fears consequences will say nothing, which is precisely the outcome you cannot afford. Run them, measure reporting, make reporting effortless and thank people for it, and treat a click as a signal about your controls rather than about the person.

Recap

  1. Reconnaissance happens on other people's systems, so reduce what is findable rather than trying to detect it.
  2. Certificate transparency makes every hostname public. No subdomain is obscure.
  3. Inventory your external surface on a schedule. The finding is almost always a forgotten asset, not a misconfigured firewall.
  4. Phishing needs no vulnerability, and business email compromise involves no malware at all.
  5. Phishing-resistant MFA is the control that removes the outcome — everything else reduces volume.
  6. Time pressure that discourages verification is the most reliable indicator of social engineering.
  7. The help desk is a primary route. Identity-proof resets, and support staff in refusing.
  8. Lockout stops brute force and not spraying. Alert on many accounts failing from one source.
  9. A stolen session cookie is already authenticated, so MFA does not help against infostealers.
  10. Measure report rate and time to report, not click rate — and never punish clickers.
