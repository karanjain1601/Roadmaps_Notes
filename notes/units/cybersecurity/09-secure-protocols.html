Secure protocols

Every protocol in this chapter is the same primitives from Chapter 8 assembled into something usable, and each one protects a specific thing while leaving something else exposed. The recurring mistake is assuming a protocol covers more than it does — which is how "we use a VPN" becomes a security claim it cannot support.

9.1The plaintext ancestors

Most secure protocols exist because an earlier one sent credentials in the clear. Recognising the pairs is worth a moment, because the insecure half is still running somewhere in every large estate.

STARTTLS is opportunistic, and that is the problem

Protocols that begin in plaintext and upgrade — SMTP, IMAP, LDAP, XMPP — advertise TLS support in the initial exchange. An attacker in the middle simply removes the advertisement, and the client, finding no TLS offered, continues unencrypted. This is STARTTLS stripping, and it succeeds silently because the client has no way to know TLS was ever available. Two defences: prefer implicit TLS on a dedicated port, where there is no plaintext phase to attack; and where you must use STARTTLS, configure the client to require it rather than prefer it, so a missing advertisement is an error instead of a downgrade. The same logic explains HSTS for the web.

9.3What "we use a VPN" does and does not mean

A VPN moves the point at which your traffic joins the internet. That is genuinely useful and it is not the same as security, and the confusion between the two is the most commercially exploited misunderstanding in the field.

Two entirely different products sharing a name
Corporate remote-access VPNConsumer privacy VPN
PurposeReach internal resources from outsideHide your address from destinations, and your destinations from your ISP
What it protectsTraffic to the corporate network; access control at the edgeTraffic between you and the provider
Who can see your trafficYour employerThe VPN provider — you have moved trust, not removed it
Against a malicious café networkEffectiveEffective — but so is HTTPS, which you already have
Against the destination siteNo effectHides your IP; cookies, logins and fingerprinting still identify you
Against malwareNoneNone
Security risk it addsAn internet-facing appliance holding credentials, and a recurring source of critical vulnerabilitiesA single party seeing everything you do
The honest summary

Since almost all web traffic is already HTTPS, a consumer VPN's incremental protection against a hostile local network is small — the content was already encrypted. What it genuinely changes is who sees the metadata: your ISP no longer sees which sites you visit, and the provider does instead. Whether that is an improvement depends entirely on which of the two you trust more, and that is a judgement, not a technical property. For a corporate VPN the calculus is different again: it is an access control mechanism, and the thing to watch is that the appliance itself is internet-facing, holds credentials, and has been the initial access vector in a long series of major intrusions — so it needs patching with more urgency than almost anything else you run.

9.5IPsec, and where tunnelling fits

The layer decides what is covered
Operates atProtectsTypical use
TLSTransport / applicationOne connection between two applicationsWeb, APIs, mail, anything per-service
SSH tunnelApplicationSpecific forwarded portsAd-hoc access to a single service
IPsecNetworkEvery packet between two endpoints, transparently to applicationsSite-to-site links; anything where you cannot modify the applications
WireGuardNetworkSame, with a far smaller codebase and modern cryptography onlyThe current default choice for new tunnels
Service mesh mTLSTransport, injectedEvery service-to-service call, with identity on both endsContainer platforms — encryption and authentication without changing code

IPsec has two modes worth distinguishing. Transport mode encrypts the payload and leaves the original IP header, so the endpoints are visible — used host to host. Tunnel mode encapsulates the entire original packet inside a new one, hiding the internal addressing — used for site-to-site gateways, which is the common case. And two protocols: AH provides authentication only and is essentially unused, while ESP provides encryption and authentication and is what everyone deploys.

9.6DNS

Two separate problems that get conflated because both involve DNS and encryption.

Integrity and confidentiality are different questions
DNSSECDoH / DoT
ProvidesIntegrity — the answer is authentic and unmodifiedConfidentiality — nobody on the path sees the query
Does not provideConfidentiality. Queries and answers are still plaintextIntegrity. The resolver could still lie to you
Protects againstCache poisoning, spoofed answersPassive surveillance, ISP-level logging and blocking
Trust moves toThe DNS root and the zone ownersWhoever operates the resolver — often a large third party
Defensive costComplexity, and a signing failure takes your domain offlineYou lose visibility of DNS queries on your own network, and so does your detection

The defender's tension is worth naming: encrypted DNS is good for users and bad for the network's own detection. DNS logs are among the highest-value telemetry available (Chapter 15), and a device using DoH to a public resolver bypasses your logging, your filtering and your blocklists entirely. The usual resolution is to run your own DoH or DoT resolver internally, block direct external DNS at the egress point, and accept that some applications with hardcoded resolvers will need specific handling.

9.7Email authentication

SPF, DKIM and DMARC

Three mechanisms that only work together. See which spoofing attempts each one stops.

Why did TLS 1.3 reduce the cipher suite list from hundreds to five?

Because the size of the list was itself the vulnerability. In TLS 1.2 a suite specified four independent choices — key exchange, authentication, bulk cipher and MAC — and the combinatorics produced hundreds of named suites, many of which included something obsolete. That created three problems. Misconfiguration became the norm: administrators could not reasonably evaluate each suite, so servers shipped with weak options enabled for compatibility with clients that no longer existed, and a scan of any large estate would find RC4, 3DES, export-grade ciphers and static RSA still offered. Downgrade attacks worked: an attacker in the middle could influence negotiation towards the weakest mutually supported option, which is the mechanism behind FREAK, Logjam and POODLE — the server was not exploited so much as persuaded to use something it should never have offered. And the combinations interacted badly: CBC mode with a MAC-then-encrypt construction produced padding oracles that took years to fully mitigate. TLS 1.3 removed the choice rather than documenting it: forward-secret key exchange is mandatory, AEAD is mandatory, and the remaining suites differ only in which AEAD cipher and hash are used, none of which is a bad answer. Authentication was separated out of the suite entirely. The result is that a TLS 1.3 server cannot easily be misconfigured into weakness, and the transcript signature covers the whole handshake so downgrade attempts are detected. It is a good general lesson about secure defaults: removing the unsafe option beats documenting that it is unsafe.

Your organisation publishes SPF and DKIM but DMARC is set to p=none. What is and is not protected?

Almost nothing is protected, and this is a very common state that looks like completion. SPF says which servers may send for your domain and DKIM signs the message so tampering is detectable — but neither tells a receiving mail server what to do when a check fails, and neither is checked against the address the user actually sees. That second point is the subtle one: SPF validates the envelope sender, which is invisible in a mail client, while the user reads the From header, and a message can pass SPF for the attacker's own domain while displaying your domain in From. DMARC is the piece that closes the gap, because it requires alignment — the domain in the visible From header must match the domain that passed SPF or DKIM — and it publishes a policy telling receivers to quarantine or reject on failure. With p=none you get the reports and no enforcement, so a receiving server that finds an unaligned failure delivers the message anyway. The reports are genuinely valuable as a first step, because they show you every service sending as your domain, including the marketing platform and the ticketing system nobody told you about, and moving to enforcement before you have that inventory will break legitimate mail. So the correct reading is that p=none is a valid stage and an invalid destination. What none of the three ever stops is a lookalike domain, a display-name spoof, or a genuinely compromised mailbox — those need separate controls, which is why business email compromise remains effective against organisations with perfect DMARC.

A colleague says a VPN will protect staff on public Wi-Fi. Is that right?

Partly, and the reasoning matters more than the answer. The threat people picture — someone in the café reading your traffic — was largely solved by HTTPS becoming universal. On a hostile network an attacker can already see which hostnames you connect to, via SNI and DNS, and how much data moves, but the content of nearly everything is already encrypted, so the incremental confidentiality a VPN adds for web browsing is small. Where it does help is with the traffic that is not already protected: legacy internal applications speaking plaintext protocols, anything using STARTTLS that can be stripped, an application with certificate validation disabled, and DNS queries if they are not already encrypted. It also defeats a captive portal that tampers with unencrypted pages. For a corporate VPN there is a stronger and different argument, which is access control rather than confidentiality — it is how staff reach internal systems at all, and that is a legitimate reason to require it regardless of the Wi-Fi question. Two cautions to add. A VPN moves trust to the provider rather than removing it, so for a consumer product the question is whether you trust them more than the network you are on. And the corporate VPN concentrates risk: the appliance is internet-facing, holds credentials for everyone, and has repeatedly been the initial access vector in major intrusions, so it needs patching faster than almost anything else you operate. The better framing for staff is therefore "use the corporate VPN because that is how you reach internal systems, and rely on HTTPS for everything else" — with the real public Wi-Fi risks being shoulder surfing and captive portals rather than packet sniffing.

Recap

  1. Every secure protocol has a plaintext ancestor still running somewhere. Find it.
  2. STARTTLS can be stripped. Prefer implicit TLS, and require rather than prefer where you cannot.
  3. TLS 1.3 removed the choices that caused the failures — non-ephemeral exchange, CBC, compression, renegotiation.
  4. SNI leaks the hostname even under TLS, unless Encrypted Client Hello is in use.
  5. 0-RTT data is replayable. Only for idempotent requests.
  6. A VPN moves trust, it does not remove it — and a corporate VPN appliance is a recurring initial access vector.
  7. SSH: keys not passwords, certificates at scale, and never forward the agent.
  8. DNSSEC gives integrity, DoH gives confidentiality. They are not alternatives.
  9. Encrypted DNS removes your own best telemetry. Run the resolver yourself.
  10. DMARC is the piece that enforces alignment. SPF and DKIM without it protect very little.
