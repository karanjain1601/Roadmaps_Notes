Perimeter and endpoint controls

This is the category with the most products and the least clarity about what each one does. Every control here inspects something, at some point, against some notion of bad — and the useful questions are what it can see, what it does when it sees it, and what happens when it is wrong.

The distinction that matters operationally: a firewall knows which conversations may happen; it does not know what is said in them. Once you permit HTTPS outbound, everything from a software update to command and control uses the same permitted channel. That is why egress filtering by destination and inspection at the proxy add something a port-based rule cannot.

12.2Detection versus prevention

The difference between an IDS and an IPS is one decision: whether the control sits beside the traffic or in it.

The trade, stated plainly 
IDS   out of band, on a mirror port. Sees, alerts, cannot block.
      A false positive costs an analyst's time.
      A false negative costs you the detection.

IPS   inline. Sees, alerts, and drops.
      A false positive costs an OUTAGE.
      A false negative costs you the detection.

WHICH IS WHY MOST IPS DEPLOYMENTS RUN MOSTLY IN
DETECT MODE, with a small set of high-confidence rules
set to block. That is not timidity — it is the correct
response to the asymmetry: the cost of a wrong block
scales with how important the traffic was, and the most
important traffic is the traffic you least want to drop.

THE SAME ASYMMETRY APPEARS EVERYWHERE
  WAF        block mode vs monitor mode
  EDR        quarantine vs alert
  DLP        block the email vs flag it
  Mail       reject vs junk folder

RULE OF THUMB
  Block automatically where confidence is very high and the
  action is reversible. Alert where confidence is moderate.
  Never automate a response whose failure mode is worse than
  the attack it prevents.

The base rate problem

Why a 99%-accurate detector produces mostly false alarms, and what that does to a team.

Precision matters more than sensitivity

Security detection lives at an extreme base rate: millions of events, a handful of which matter. At that ratio, even a very low false positive rate produces far more false alarms than true ones, and the useful metric is not "what proportion of attacks do we catch" but "what proportion of our alerts are real". A detector that catches 70% of attacks with 90% precision is more valuable than one catching 95% with 2% precision, because the second one's alerts will not be investigated — and an alert nobody investigates has a real-world detection rate of zero. This is the arithmetic behind alert fatigue, and it explains why tuning a noisy rule out of existence is often a security improvement.

The honest framing for EDR: it is a recording device with opinions. Its detections are useful and its real value is that when something happens you can reconstruct exactly what ran, in what order, from where, and reach out to contain it — which is the difference between an investigation that takes an hour and one that takes three weeks. That value only exists if someone is watching, which is why managed detection exists as a category and why buying EDR without staffing it is a common and expensive mistake.

Canaries are the best precision-to-effort ratio available

A file share nobody should open, a database row nobody should read, an API key that appears in a config file and is monitored for use, a fake privileged account in the directory. These generate essentially no false positives, because there is no legitimate reason for anyone to interact with them — so an alert means something real, and it means it at a stage where the attacker is still exploring. Against the base-rate arithmetic in §12.2 this is unusually valuable: a low-volume, near-perfect-precision detector is worth more than a high-volume noisy one, and they cost almost nothing to deploy. They are also one of the few controls that works against an insider.

Why does a detector with 99.99% accuracy produce mostly false alarms?

Because accuracy is the wrong measure when one class is vanishingly rare, and security detection has one of the most extreme base rates anywhere. Suppose two million events a day and twelve of them genuinely malicious. A detector catching 95% of attacks finds about eleven true positives. A false positive rate of 0.01% — which sounds excellent, and is far better than most real detections achieve — applied to the two million benign events produces two hundred false alarms. So the alert queue is 211 items of which 11 are real, and precision is about 5%: nineteen out of twenty alerts waste someone's time. Pushing the false positive rate down by a factor of ten only brings it to 35%, which is still the majority being wrong. The consequence is not merely inefficiency. An analyst who has dismissed forty consecutive false alarms is measurably worse at recognising the forty-first as real, and a queue that exceeds the team's capacity means alerts expire unread — so the detector's real-world sensitivity is not 95% but 95% multiplied by the probability that a given alert is actually looked at, which can be far lower. That is why precision beats sensitivity at these base rates, why tuning a noisy rule out of existence is frequently a net security improvement, and why very-low-volume high-precision detectors such as canaries are worth so much more than their alert count suggests. The metrics to track are precision per rule and queue depth against capacity, not an overall accuracy figure.

Your organisation has a WAF, a next-generation firewall and EDR. What is still uncovered?

Several things, and they cluster in a recognisable way. Anything inside TLS you do not terminate: a next-generation firewall classifies applications and inspects payloads, but without interception it sees an encrypted stream to a permitted destination, so command and control over HTTPS to a legitimate cloud service passes as ordinary traffic. Anything that never crosses the network path those controls sit on: with remote work and split tunnelling, a laptop's traffic to the internet does not traverse the corporate perimeter at all, so the firewall and proxy are simply not in the path — the endpoint is your only control there. Identity-layer attacks: none of the three sees a password spray against your identity provider, an anomalous federated sign-in, a consent grant to a malicious application, or a stolen session token being replayed, because none of that involves a payload on your network or a process on your endpoint. Insider actions using legitimate access: a user reading and downloading files they are permitted to read is invisible to all three. Unmanaged and unmanageable devices: personal machines, contractor laptops, IoT, printers and building systems cannot run the endpoint agent, so a large part of the estate is outside the strongest control you have. And the cloud control plane: an API call that creates a new access key or changes a bucket policy never touches your network. The pattern is that the three controls cover network payloads and endpoint processes well and cover identity, data and unmanaged infrastructure barely at all — which is why identity telemetry and cloud audit logs are the usual next investment rather than a better firewall.

Should you run an IPS in blocking mode?

For a small subset of rules, yes; for the whole ruleset, almost never — and the reasoning generalises to every automated response decision. The asymmetry is that a false negative costs you a detection you would not have had anyway, while a false positive costs an outage, and the cost of that outage scales with how important the blocked traffic was. Given the base rates in §12.2, a ruleset producing hundreds of false positives a day will, in blocking mode, produce hundreds of small outages a day, and the organisational response to that is to disable the control entirely — so aggressive blocking frequently ends with less protection than conservative alerting. The workable approach has three parts. Run the full ruleset in detect mode and use it as telemetry. Promote individual rules to block only when you have evidence from your own environment that they do not fire on legitimate traffic — which means measuring precision per rule over weeks, not trusting the vendor's confidence rating. And prefer blocking where the action is reversible and narrow: dropping a single connection is recoverable, quarantining a file can be undone, isolating one host is a contained decision, whereas blocking an address range or disabling an account can cascade. Two additions worth making. Fail-open versus fail-closed is a separate decision from block versus alert, and an inline device that fails closed becomes a single point of failure for the whole network. And whatever mode you choose, the detections still need someone reading them — an IPS in blocking mode with nobody watching the alerts stops the noisy attacks and hides the interesting ones.

Recap

  1. A firewall knows which conversations may happen, not what is said in them.
  2. IDS versus IPS is one decision: whether a false positive costs an analyst's time or an outage.
  3. At security base rates, precision beats sensitivity. An alert nobody reads has a detection rate of zero.
  4. Tuning a noisy rule out of existence is often a security improvement.
  5. EDR is a recording device with opinions, and unstaffed EDR is expensive telemetry.
  6. Antivirus cannot see living off the land because the binaries are legitimate.
  7. Remote work removes the network controls from the path. The endpoint becomes the only one.
  8. Nothing in this chapter sees identity attacks or cloud control-plane actions.
  9. DLP is good against accidents and weak against intent.
  10. Canaries have the best precision-to-effort ratio available, and they work against insiders.
