Replication and consensus

Every copy of your data is a bet: it buys availability, read capacity and durability, and it costs you the problem of keeping copies in agreement. This chapter covers the three replication architectures, the quorum arithmetic that governs the third, how conflicts arise and get resolved, and what consensus protocols like Raft buy you and what they charge.

~40 min read Leader · multi-leader · quorums · Raft 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapters 4–5

6.1Four reasons to keep more than one copy

  1. Availability. A machine dies; another already has the data and can serve.
  2. Read throughput. Reads are served from many replicas, so read capacity scales with replica count. Write capacity does not — every replica must apply every write.
  3. Locality. A copy near the user removes a hundred milliseconds of physics from every read.
  4. Durability. Data on three machines in three failure domains survives events that data on one machine does not.
Replication scales reads, partitioning scales writes

This is the single most useful sentence about scaling data. Ten replicas give you ten times the read capacity and the same write capacity, because all ten must apply every write. If your write volume exceeds one machine, no number of replicas helps — you need to split the data so different machines own different writes, which is Chapter 7. Real systems do both: partition into shards, replicate each shard.

6.2What actually gets shipped

Before the topology, the mechanism. There are four ways to tell a replica what changed, and the choice has real consequences.

Logical replication is the one worth understanding in a system design, because it doubles as a change data capture stream. The same feed that keeps a replica current can keep a search index, a cache, a data warehouse and an event bus current — one source of truth, many derived views, all fed from the database's own log rather than by dual writes from application code. That pattern shows up again in Chapter 17 as the outbox and CDC patterns.

6.3Single-leader replication

One replica is designated leader. All writes go to it; it applies them, appends them to its log, and streams the log to followers. Reads can come from any replica. This is the default in Postgres, MySQL, SQL Server, MongoDB, Redis and Kafka partitions, and it is the right starting point for almost every design.

Synchronous, asynchronous, and the useful middle

When does the leader tell the client "done"?

Fully synchronous replication is almost always a mistake: it makes availability worse as you add replicas, because every additional node is another thing whose failure stops writes. Semi-synchronous — commit locally plus one confirmed follower, ideally in a different availability zone — is the sweet spot for most systems: RPO of zero for single-node failure, one extra millisecond of latency, and no dependence on any specific follower.

The semi-sync degradation trap

Most semi-synchronous implementations fall back to asynchronous when no follower acknowledges within a timeout, so that a follower outage does not stop the service. Sensible — but it silently converts your zero-RPO guarantee into a lossy one, and it usually does so during exactly the kind of incident where you are also likely to lose the leader. Alert on "replication mode degraded to async", and treat it as a page, not a log line.

Adding a follower without stopping the world

A question that comes up in real operations and occasionally in interviews. You cannot copy the data files while writes are happening — you would get a torn, inconsistent snapshot. The standard procedure:

  1. Take a consistent snapshot of the leader, noting the exact log position it corresponds to.
  2. Copy the snapshot to the new follower.
  3. The follower connects and asks for all log records since that position.
  4. It replays until it catches up, then switches to streaming, and is now a normal follower.

The only requirement is that the leader retains log from the snapshot position onwards — which is why a slow catch-up or a disconnected replica can cause the leader's disk to fill with retained WAL, a classic 3 a.m. incident. Bound it: cap the retained log, and let a hopelessly lagging replica be rebuilt from a fresh snapshot rather than holding the leader hostage.

Replication lag and what it does to users

Asynchronous followers are behind. Usually by a millisecond or two; occasionally, under write bursts or during a long-running query on the follower, by minutes. The resulting anomalies and their fixes were covered in Chapter 5 — read-your-writes, monotonic reads, writes-follow-reads. The operational side is simple and often neglected:

  • Measure lag in bytes and in seconds. Seconds alone lies when writes are idle; bytes alone lies when writes are large.
  • Alert on lag, not just on replica health. A replica that is up and 400 seconds behind is serving wrong answers to real users.
  • Give the router a lag threshold. Automatically stop sending reads to any replica lagging beyond, say, 5 seconds — a good default that fails safe.
  • Remember that failover to a lagging replica loses data equal to the lag. That is your RPO, whatever the design document claims.

6.4Multi-leader replication

Several nodes accept writes and replicate to each other. It solves specific problems and creates one large one.

When multi-leader is the right answer
SituationWhy single-leader fails
Multi-region with local writesWriters on the far side of the world pay a cross-continent round trip on every write
Offline-capable clientsEach device is effectively a leader that syncs later — a calendar app, a note-taking app
Collaborative editingEvery participant writes locally for responsiveness, then merges
Migration between systemsBoth old and new accept writes during a cutover window

The large problem is write conflicts. Two leaders accept a change to the same record at the same time; both are already acknowledged to their clients; the changes are incompatible. Nobody can undo an acknowledgement, so something must decide the outcome.

Conflict resolution strategies
StrategyHowVerdict
Avoid conflictsRoute all writes for a given record to one designated leader (by user, tenant, or key range)By far the best option when the data partitions naturally. Multi-leader in topology, single-leader per record
Last write winsHighest timestamp winsSimple and silently destroys data. Also depends on clock sync, which is not reliable across machines
Version vectorsTrack causality per replica; detect true concurrency and surface both versionsCorrect detection, but someone still has to resolve the siblings
Application mergeDomain logic decides: union the shopping carts, keep the higher bid, prompt the userCorrect, because only the domain knows what a merge means
CRDTsData types whose merge is automatic and deterministicExcellent where the data fits the shape; useless for invariants
Why last-write-wins is worse than it looks

It relies on comparing timestamps generated on different machines. Clock skew of even a few milliseconds means the write that happened second can carry the earlier timestamp and lose. NTP-synced servers routinely drift by more than that, and a clock jump — a VM resuming, a leap-second adjustment — can lose hours of writes in one moment. If you must use LWW, use a logical clock (a counter, a hybrid logical clock) rather than wall time, and be explicit that concurrent writes will be discarded silently.

6.5Leaderless replication and quorums

The Dynamo model: no leader at all. The client (or a coordinator node acting for it) sends every write to all N replicas and considers it successful once W of them acknowledge. Reads go to all N and are considered successful once R respond; if the responses disagree, the newest wins and the stale replicas are repaired.

The things that make quorums leaky

R + W > N sounds like a guarantee. In real Dynamo-style systems it is closer to a strong tendency, for reasons worth knowing:

Sloppy quorums and hinted handoff
If some of the N home replicas are unreachable, the system may accept W acknowledgements from other nodes that happen to be reachable, which then hold "hints" to be handed off later. This keeps writes flowing during a partition — and means the write set and read set may not overlap at all until the hints are delivered. It is an availability feature that quietly weakens the arithmetic.
Concurrent writes to the same key
Two clients write different values, both reaching quorum. Which is "latest"? Without causality tracking the system falls back to timestamps, with all the LWW hazards above.
Partial write failures
A write reaches W−1 nodes and fails. It is reported as failed, but those nodes keep the value, and a later read may return it. There is no rollback.
Read repair and anti-entropy
Two convergence mechanisms. Read repair fixes stale replicas discovered during a read — which means rarely-read keys are rarely repaired. Anti-entropy is a background process comparing replicas, usually with Merkle trees so that only differing ranges are transferred. You need both: read repair for hot data, anti-entropy for the cold tail.

Version vectors, briefly

To tell "newer than" from "concurrent with", each replica keeps a counter and every value carries the vector of counters it saw. Comparing two vectors gives three outcomes: A dominates B (A is newer, keep A), B dominates A, or neither dominates (genuinely concurrent — keep both as siblings and let the application merge). This is the same machinery that makes causal consistency possible, and it is the honest alternative to pretending timestamps can order events across machines.

6.6Consensus

Quorums answer "did enough replicas store this value?". Consensus answers a harder question: can a group of unreliable machines agree on a single sequence of decisions, such that no two of them ever believe different things? That is what you need for leader election, distributed locks, cluster membership, configuration and any operation that must be exactly-once at the cluster level.

Raft in plain terms

Raft is deliberately designed to be understandable, and its three ideas cover most of what you need to know.

1 · Terms
Time is divided into numbered terms. Each term has at most one leader. Term numbers are the logical clock of the system: any message carrying an old term is rejected, which is the fencing mechanism from Chapter 4 built into the protocol.
2 · Leader election
Followers expect heartbeats. If a follower hears nothing for its randomised election timeout (typically 150–300 ms), it increments the term, votes for itself and asks the others for votes. A node grants its vote only if the candidate's log is at least as up to date as its own — this is the safety rule that prevents a node with missing entries from becoming leader. A candidate with votes from a majority becomes leader. The randomised timeout is what stops every node campaigning simultaneously forever.
3 · Log replication
Clients send commands to the leader, which appends them to its log and sends them to followers. Once a majority have written an entry to their logs, the leader marks it committed, applies it to the state machine, and tells the client. Followers apply committed entries in the same order. Because every log is identical up to the commit index, every replica's state machine goes through the identical sequence of states.

The standard architecture: consensus for the small thing

You rarely put your bulk data in a consensus system. The usual pattern is to run a small, strongly consistent store (etcd, ZooKeeper, Consul) that holds who the leader is and which node owns which shard, and let the data plane — the databases, caches and services doing the actual work — use cheaper replication while consulting that store for coordination. A few kilobytes under consensus makes terabytes of ordinary replication safe.

Paxos, Raft, ZAB, Viewstamped Replication

Four names for closely related things. Paxos is the original and is notoriously hard to turn into working code — practical systems use Multi-Paxos, which adds a stable leader and looks a lot like Raft. Raft was designed for understandability and is what most new systems use. ZAB is ZooKeeper's protocol; Viewstamped Replication predates Paxos and is essentially equivalent. For system design purposes they are interchangeable: majority quorum, a stable leader, a replicated log, safety across leader changes.

Leases: consensus you only pay for occasionally

Running consensus on every operation is expensive. A lease amortises it: a node acquires, through consensus, the exclusive right to act as leader for the next N seconds. Within that window it may serve reads locally with no coordination at all, because no other node can hold the lease. It renews before expiry.

Leases are how strongly consistent systems get fast reads, and they rest on an assumption worth being nervous about: that clocks tick at roughly the same rate on different machines. A leader whose process is paused — by garbage collection, by a hypervisor, by the machine being suspended — can wake up believing it still holds a lease that expired long ago. The defence is the same fencing token as before: the storage layer records the lease generation and rejects writes carrying a stale one, so the zombie leader can believe whatever it likes and still do no damage.

The default advice is unglamorous and correct: start single-leader. It is the simplest thing that works, it gives you strong consistency for free on the write path, and the ceiling — a few thousand writes per second, more with tuning — is well above what most systems ever reach. Move to partitioned single-leader (each shard has its own leader) when writes outgrow one node. Reach for multi-leader only when geography or offline clients force it, and for leaderless when you specifically want write availability during partitions and can accept the reconciliation work.

You run N=5, W=3, R=3. Two nodes fail. What still works, and are reads still guaranteed fresh?

Three nodes remain, so both W=3 and R=3 are still satisfiable — writes and reads both continue, with no spare capacity for a third failure. R + W = 6 > 5, so the read and write sets must overlap and reads still see the latest acknowledged write. The catch is availability of the operation, not correctness: with exactly three nodes alive, every read and every write must reach all of them, so any slow node now sets the latency for every operation. The tail latency will be noticeably worse even though nothing is technically broken.

Why does adding a fourth node to a three-node Raft cluster not improve fault tolerance?

Because a majority of 4 is 3, and a majority of 3 is 2. Three nodes tolerate one failure (2 of 3 remain); four nodes also tolerate only one (3 of 4 remain). You have added a machine, added a node that must be kept in sync, and increased the chance that some node is unhealthy — for zero extra fault tolerance. Consensus groups should be odd: 3 for most things, 5 when you want to survive two failures or do maintenance while tolerating one.

Your primary fails over to a replica that was 8 seconds behind. What did that cost, and how would you have known in advance?

Eight seconds of acknowledged writes are gone — the clients were told those writes succeeded, and they did not. Downstream systems that already consumed those events (a search index, an email that was sent, a payment reference given to a customer) are now inconsistent with the database, which is usually worse than the data loss itself. You would have known from a replication-lag metric with an alert threshold, and you would have prevented it by using semi-synchronous replication so that at least one replica is always current, and by configuring the failover tool to refuse promotion of a replica lagging beyond a set bound — preferring a longer outage to silent data loss.

Recap

  1. Replication scales reads and buys availability; partitioning scales writes. Real systems do both.
  2. Logical replication doubles as change data capture — one log feeding replicas, indexes, caches and warehouses.
  3. Semi-synchronous is the sweet spot: leader plus one confirmed follower gives zero RPO for a single failure at one round trip of cost. Fully synchronous makes availability worse as you add nodes.
  4. Measure and alert on replication lag, and stop routing reads to replicas past a threshold. Lag is your real RPO.
  5. Multi-leader means conflicts. Best strategy is to avoid them by routing each record to one leader; last-write-wins loses data silently and depends on clocks.
  6. R + W > N makes read and write sets overlap — weakened in practice by sloppy quorums, concurrent writes and partial failures.
  7. Consensus (Raft) gives a replicated log with a majority quorum, costs a round trip per decision, needs an odd-sized group, and should hold metadata rather than bulk data.
  8. Leases make consistent reads cheap and require fencing tokens, because a paused process can wake up believing it still holds one.
  9. Start single-leader. Its ceiling is higher than most systems reach, and every alternative costs conflict semantics or operational complexity.
