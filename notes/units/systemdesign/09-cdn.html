CDN and the edge

A CDN is a cache you rent in a few hundred cities. It removes physics from the latency budget, absorbs the traffic your origin could never serve, and — once the numbers get big — becomes the largest line on the bill. This chapter covers how caching at the edge actually decides what to store, the header semantics that control it, how to invalidate without a stampede, and why more locations can mean a worse hit ratio.

9.1Four reasons a CDN exists

  1. Latency. A user in Sydney fetching from Virginia pays roughly 200 ms per round trip, and a TLS handshake is several. Terminating the connection 5 ms away and serving from local storage removes almost all of it — including for content the edge has to fetch, because the edge-to-origin leg runs over warm, optimised connections instead of the user's lossy mobile link.
  2. Offload. A good static hit ratio means 90–99% of requests never reach you. That is the difference between provisioning for peak internet traffic and provisioning for peak miss traffic.
  3. Availability. A CDN serving stale content during an origin outage keeps a site readable when your infrastructure is entirely down — the cheapest availability win available.
  4. Security. Volumetric attacks land on a network with terabits of capacity spread across hundreds of sites rather than on your one connection. Bot management, WAF and TLS management come along with it.

9.2The shape of a CDN

Point of presence (PoP)
A rack of caching servers in a datacenter near users, reached by anycast. Hundreds of them for a large provider.
Tiered / mid-tier caching
A second layer between PoPs and origin, so misses at many small PoPs collapse into one request upstream.
Origin shield
A single designated cache that all misses funnel through, so the origin sees at most one request per object per TTL, regardless of how many PoPs wanted it.
More PoPs is not automatically a better hit ratio

Spreading the same traffic across 300 locations means each one sees 1/300 of the requests, so each one's cache is colder and evicts sooner — a phenomenon usually called footprint fragmentation. For very popular objects it does not matter; for a long tail of rarely-requested content it matters a lot. This is precisely what tiered caching fixes, and it is why "how many PoPs does it have" is a weak way to compare providers.

Pull is the default and the right answer nearly always. The one real weakness — that the first user in each region pays for the miss — is addressed by pre-warming the specific objects you know will be hot, which gives you push's benefit for the 0.1% of content where it matters.

9.4The cache key

Everything about caching correctness comes down to one question: what makes two requests "the same request"? The cache key is the answer, and the default is usually the scheme, host and full path including the query string. That default is wrong in both directions.

Cache key problems and their fixes
ProblemEffectFix
Tracking parameters in the query string (?utm_source=…)Every campaign creates a separate cache entry for identical content. Hit ratio collapsesStrip or ignore parameters that do not affect the response; allow-list the ones that do
Parameter order and case (?a=1&b=2 vs ?b=2&a=1)Duplicate entriesNormalise: sort parameters, lowercase where safe
Cookies in the keyPer-user keys mean a hit ratio near zeroExclude cookies from the key for static paths; only vary on the specific cookie that matters
Vary: User-AgentThousands of variants of the same objectVary on a normalised device class the CDN computes, not the raw header
Missing Vary: Accept-EncodingA gzip-compressed body served to a client that cannot decompress itAlways vary on encoding, or let the CDN handle compression itself
Language or currency not in the keyA French user's page served to a German oneVary on the normalised language, or encode it in the path (/fr/…) which is easier to reason about
Vary is a blunt instrument

Vary multiplies your cached copies by the number of distinct values of the named headers. Vary: Accept-Encoding, User-Agent, Accept-Language can turn one object into thousands, each individually less likely to be hit and each occupying space that evicts something else. Prefer encoding meaningful variation in the URL — it is visible, debuggable and purgeable — and use Vary only for genuinely transparent negotiation like content encoding.

9.5Cache-Control, precisely

The directives that matter, with the distinctions people get wrong:

Response directives
DirectiveMeans
max-age=NFresh for N seconds, for every cache including the browser
s-maxage=NFresh for N seconds in shared caches only. Lets you keep a long CDN TTL and a short browser TTL — the most useful directive most people do not use
publicMay be stored by shared caches even if the request was authenticated
privateBrowser only. A CDN must not store it
no-cacheMay be stored, but must be revalidated before use. Not "do not cache"
no-storeDo not write it down anywhere. This is the one that means what people think no-cache means
must-revalidateOnce stale, do not serve it — go and check, even if the origin is unreachable
immutableThis will never change; do not even revalidate on reload. For content-hashed asset URLs
stale-while-revalidate=NServe the stale copy instantly and refresh in the background for up to N seconds past expiry. Removes the latency cost of expiry entirely
stale-if-error=NIf the origin errors or times out, keep serving the stale copy for up to N seconds. Turns an origin outage into a freshness problem

Alongside these are the validators. ETag is an opaque version identifier; Last-Modified is a timestamp. When a cached copy goes stale, the cache re-requests with If-None-Match or If-Modified-Since, and an unchanged object comes back as a 304 Not Modified with no body. That saves bandwidth but not the round trip, which is why stale-while-revalidate — which avoids making the user wait for the revalidation at all — is usually the bigger win.

9.6Invalidation

Four approaches, in increasing order of how much you will like them.

Wait for the TTL
Free, requires nothing, and means your content is wrong for up to the TTL. Fine for content whose staleness nobody notices.
Explicit purge
Tell the CDN to drop a URL. Works, but propagating a purge to hundreds of PoPs takes seconds and providers rate-limit it. Purging by wildcard or "everything" is available and dangerous — see the stampede warning below.
Versioned URLs (cache busting)
Put a content hash in the filename: /static/app.4f2c9b.js. Now the URL is the version, you can cache it for a year with immutable, and "invalidation" means publishing a new HTML page that references a new URL. There is nothing to purge because nothing ever changes. This is the correct answer for all static assets and it eliminates an entire class of bug.
Surrogate keys / cache tags
Tag responses with logical identifiers on the way out — Surrogate-Key: product-123 category-shoes — then purge by tag. Changing one product evicts every page that mentioned it, without you having to know which pages those were. This is what makes it feasible to cache HTML for a dynamic site, and it is the single feature most worth checking a CDN for.
Purge-all is an outage waiting to happen

Dropping the entire cache means every subsequent request is a miss, and they all arrive at once. An origin sized for 3% of traffic now receives 100% of it, falls over, and the cache cannot refill because the origin is down. Defences: purge narrowly using tags; prefer soft purge, which marks entries stale rather than deleting them, so stale-while-revalidate keeps serving while the refresh happens; ensure the CDN coalesces concurrent misses for the same object into a single origin request; and make sure the origin shield exists so a miss storm is one request per object rather than one per PoP.

9.7What to put at the edge

Cacheability by content type

Authenticated API responsesSometimesprivate, or cache at the edge keyed on a user identifier with a very short TTL and strict correctness review
Anything with a Set-CookieNoA shared cache storing a session cookie hands one user's session to the next. Configure the CDN to refuse to cache such responses

The personalisation problem is the interesting one, because "the page differs per user" is what stops most sites caching HTML. Three standard escapes: render a cacheable shell and fetch the personal fragment with a separate uncached request; use edge-side composition, where the edge assembles a cached template with a small dynamic hole; or move personalisation to the client entirely and let the edge serve one static document to everybody. Which is best depends on how much of the page is actually personal — usually far less than the architecture assumes.

9.8Even uncacheable traffic benefits

Routing dynamic, uncacheable requests through a CDN still helps, which surprises people:

  • TLS and TCP terminate at the edge, a few milliseconds away, so the expensive handshakes happen over a short link instead of an intercontinental one.
  • The edge-to-origin connection is already open and pooled, so your request skips connection setup entirely.
  • The long leg runs over the provider's optimised backbone with tuned congestion control, rather than over whatever the public internet routes today.
  • Packet loss is isolated. A retransmit on the user's mobile link costs a short round trip rather than a long one.

For a user far from your origin, this frequently takes a second off a dynamic page load without caching a single byte.

9.9Edge compute

Small programs running in every PoP, started per request in a few milliseconds. The constraints are real — short CPU budgets, limited memory, no persistent local disk, and any database call goes back over the long link you were trying to avoid — so the useful work is small and decision-shaped:

  • Auth and token checks, rejecting bad requests before they cross an ocean.
  • A/B assignment and feature flags, so the variant is decided at the edge and the cache key includes it.
  • Request and response rewriting, header manipulation, redirects, normalising cache keys.
  • Personalising a cached page by injecting a small fragment.
  • Geolocation-based routing and compliance decisions.
  • Assembling a response from several cached pieces.

What it is not good for: anything needing a database on every request, long-running computation, or strong consistency. Edge state is either eventually consistent key-value storage or a round trip back to a region, and pretending otherwise produces a system with worse latency than not using the edge at all.

9.10The economics of hit ratio

Two different ratios, and confusing them will make you optimise the wrong thing. Request hit ratio is the fraction of requests served from cache — it drives origin QPS. Byte hit ratio is the fraction of bytes served from cache — it drives origin egress cost. A site with tiny cached assets and large uncached video downloads can have a 98% request hit ratio and a 40% byte hit ratio.

9.11Operating a CDN sensibly

  • Log the cache status. Every provider emits a header (X-Cache: HIT, cf-cache-status, x-cache-status). Put it in your dashboards broken down by path — a hit ratio that drops from 96% to 60% is usually one deploy that added a cookie or a query parameter, and without this breakdown you will find it by guessing.
  • Alert on origin request rate, not just on error rate. A rising miss rate is the leading indicator of a cache configuration mistake, and it appears hours before the origin falls over.
  • Test with cache disabled. Your origin must survive a purge-all or a provider outage. If it cannot serve 100% of traffic even slowly, you have a hidden single point of failure.
  • Keep the origin reachable but not public. Restrict origin access to CDN address ranges plus signed headers, or an attacker simply addresses the origin directly and bypasses every protection you paid for.
  • Watch egress pricing tiers. CDN egress is billed per gigabyte with steep volume discounts, so a 20% reduction in average object size is usually a bigger saving than any infrastructure change.
Your CDN hit ratio drops from 95% to 30% after a release, and the origin is struggling. What do you look at first?

The cache key. In order: has a new query parameter appeared on asset URLs (analytics tags, a cache-buster added unconditionally rather than by content hash)? Has the origin started sending Set-Cookie on responses that used to be cookie-free — many frameworks do this the moment session middleware touches a request — which makes most CDNs refuse to cache? Has a Vary header appeared? Has Cache-Control changed to private or no-store? Compare the response headers for one asset before and after the deploy; the answer is almost always visible there in under a minute. Meanwhile, protect the origin: enable stale-if-error and, if the provider supports it, temporarily override the TTL at the edge to stop the bleeding while you fix the origin's headers.

Why is a content hash in the filename better than a query-string version like app.js?v=12?

Three reasons. Some intermediary caches and older proxies refuse to cache URLs with query strings at all, so the versioned-query approach can silently defeat caching in exactly the places you least control. The hash is derived from the content, so it changes if and only if the content changes — a manual version number gets forgotten, and then users hold a stale file for a year because you told them it was immutable. And distinct filenames mean old and new versions coexist, so a user who loaded the page mid-deploy can still fetch the old asset it references instead of getting a broken page.

Your site is entirely personalised — every page shows the user's name. Can you use a CDN at all?

Yes, and you should. First, all static assets are unaffected by personalisation and are the majority of requests. Second, dynamic acceleration helps every uncacheable request through TLS termination at the edge and pooled origin connections. Third, "entirely personalised" usually means one small region of an otherwise identical page — cache the shell and fetch the personal fragment separately, or inject it at the edge, which turns the bulk of your HTML into cacheable content. The design smell to avoid is Vary on a session cookie, which technically caches everything and hits on nothing while consuming storage.

Recap

  1. A CDN buys latency, offload, availability and DDoS absorption. Even uncacheable traffic gets faster from edge TLS termination and pooled origin connections.
  2. Tiered caching and an origin shield collapse per-PoP misses into one origin request. Without them, more PoPs can mean a worse hit ratio.
  3. Pull, with pre-warming for the few objects that matter. Push is for scheduled large-file launches.
  4. The cache key is the whole game. Strip tracking parameters, keep cookies out of it, and prefer URL-encoded variation over Vary.
  5. no-cache means revalidate; no-store means do not store. Use s-maxage to split CDN and browser lifetimes, and stale-while-revalidate plus stale-if-error to remove expiry latency and survive origin outages.
  6. Content-hashed URLs make invalidation unnecessary for static assets; surrogate keys make it feasible for HTML.
  7. Purge-all causes stampedes. Purge by tag, prefer soft purge, and make sure concurrent misses coalesce.
  8. Track request hit ratio for origin QPS and byte hit ratio for cost, and alert on origin request rate as the early warning.
