CAP, PACELC and consistency

CAP is the most quoted and least understood result in the field. This chapter states it precisely, kills the "CA system" myth, replaces it with PACELC — which describes the trade-off you make every day rather than the one you make twice a year — and then walks the full ladder of consistency models with the anomaly each one still permits.

~35 min read CAP · PACELC · consistency models 2 interactive panels Prerequisite: chapter 4

5.1What CAP actually says

Eric Brewer conjectured it in 2000; Gilbert and Lynch proved a formal version in 2002. The three properties, stated precisely, because the imprecise version is where all the confusion comes from:

C — Consistency
Specifically linearizability: the system behaves as if there were a single copy of the data, and every read returns the value of the most recently completed write. Not the C in ACID, which is about database invariants and is a completely different concept that happens to share a letter.
A — Availability
Every request received by a non-failing node must return a non-error response. Note what this does not say: nothing about latency, and nothing about the request being fast or the whole system being up. A system that answers in an hour is "available" by this definition.
P — Partition tolerance
The system continues to operate despite the network arbitrarily dropping or delaying messages between nodes.

The theorem: when a partition occurs, you must give up either consistency or availability. That is the whole content. It is a statement about behaviour during a partition, not a taxonomy of databases.

The intuition takes ten seconds. Two replicas, a network cut between them, a client writes to node A and another client immediately reads from node B. Node B has two options and no third one: return the old value (fast, available, inconsistent) or refuse to answer until it can reach A (consistent, unavailable). No amount of engineering creates an option C, because information cannot cross a severed link.

"We chose CA" is not a thing

Partitions are not a design choice; they are a property of networks, which drop packets, lose links, misconfigure routes and get overloaded. You do not choose whether partitions happen — you choose what happens when one does. So the real menu has two items: CP (refuse service on the side that cannot be sure) and AP (keep serving, accept divergence). A "CA system" is a single-node system, or a distributed system that will corrupt itself the first time a switch reboots.

The other things CAP does not say

  • It is not a global label. Systems make the choice per operation. The same database can serve a strongly consistent read of a balance and an eventually consistent read of a follower count.
  • It is not a spectrum with a dial you set once. The 2-of-3 framing is a slogan; real systems sit at many points and change position under configuration.
  • It says nothing about latency, which is the thing you actually pay for every millisecond of every normal day. That gap is what PACELC fixes.
  • Giving up "availability" rarely means going down. A CP system under partition typically keeps serving reads on the majority side and refuses writes on the minority side. Most users see nothing.

Examplesetcd, ZooKeeper, Consul, Spanner, CockroachDB, HBase, single-leader Postgres with sync replicationCassandra, DynamoDB (eventual mode), Riak, CouchDB, most DNS, most CDNs

Two things about that table are worth dwelling on. First, "AP" does not mean "no consistency ever" — it means convergence is deferred. A well-built AP system reconciles within milliseconds when the network is healthy, and the eventual in "eventual consistency" is usually measured in single-digit milliseconds, not minutes. Second, most real deployments are tunable per request. DynamoDB lets you ask for a strongly consistent read on the same table you normally read eventually; Cassandra exposes consistency levels from ONE to ALL on every statement. The question in a design discussion is therefore never "is this database CP or AP" but "what consistency does this operation need, and what does that cost."

5.3PACELC: the trade-off you make every day

Daniel Abadi's extension is more useful than CAP itself, because it names the branch that applies 99.99% of the time:

PACELC 
if (Partition)   choose between  Availability  and  Consistency
else             choose between  Latency       and  Consistency

The else branch is the everyday reality. Even with a perfectly healthy network, a strongly consistent read must confirm with a quorum, and a strongly consistent write must reach a majority of replicas before it is acknowledged. That is one or two extra network round trips on every operation — 1 ms within a region, 60–150 ms across the world. You pay that on every request forever, not just during the outage that happens twice a year.

The sentence to use in a design review

"Under a partition this operation must stay available, so I accept a stale read and reconcile with last-write-wins; and in normal operation I take the local-replica read for latency rather than a quorum read, because a two-second-old follower count is fine." That single sentence covers both PACELC branches, names the mechanism, and justifies it against the requirement. It is what "understanding CAP" actually looks like in practice.

5.4The consistency ladder

"Strong" and "eventual" are the ends of a ladder with useful rungs in between. Each rung forbids a specific set of observable anomalies, and the cost rises as you climb.

Consistency models, strongest first
ModelGuaranteeCostTypical use
Strict serializabilityTransactions appear in a single global order that respects real timeHighest — consensus plus global orderingSpanner, CockroachDB; financial ledgers
LinearizabilityEvery single-object read sees the latest completed write, as if one copy existedQuorum round trip per operationLocks, leader election, unique-name claims, config
Sequential consistencyAll nodes see operations in the same order, but that order need not match real timeSlightly cheaper than linearizableRarely offered by name
Causal consistencyIf A happened before B, everyone sees A before B. Concurrent events may be seen in any orderMetadata per write (version vectors), no coordinationComment threads, chat, collaborative apps
Bounded stalenessReads are never more than t seconds or k versions behindModerate — needs a clock or version boundDashboards, reporting, "as of" views
Session guaranteesConsistent from one client's point of view (see below)Cheap — routing or a tokenAlmost every user-facing app
Eventual consistencyIf writes stop, replicas eventually converge. Nothing else is promisedLowest — write locally, replicate laterCounters, caches, DNS, presence, telemetry

The four session guarantees

These are the cheapest large win available, because they fix the anomalies users actually notice while requiring no global coordination:

Read-your-writes
A client always sees its own writes. Without it, a user edits their profile, the page reloads from a lagging replica, and the edit appears to have vanished — the single most common consistency complaint in real products.
Monotonic reads
A client never sees time move backwards. Without it, two successive reads hit different replicas with different lag and the user watches a comment appear and then disappear.
Monotonic writes
A client's writes are applied in the order it issued them. Without it, "set status to away" then "set status to online" can land in the wrong order and stick.
Writes follow reads
If you read a value and then write based on it, your write is ordered after the value you read. This is what keeps a reply from being visible before the message it replies to.
Three cheap implementations of read-your-writes
  1. Sticky reads after a write. Route this user's reads to the primary for the next few seconds — long enough to exceed typical replication lag. One line in a router, no data-model change.
  2. Version tokens. The write returns a log position (an LSN, a timestamp, an opaque token); the client sends it back on subsequent reads; a replica that has not caught up to that position either waits briefly or forwards the read. Precise, and works across regions.
  3. Write-through the client. Keep the just-written value in the client's own state and render it locally, so the round trip does not matter at all. Standard practice in modern front ends, and it is a consistency mechanism whether the front-end team calls it that or not.

5.5Linearizability is not serializability

Two words that sound the same and mean different things. Mixing them up produces confused design discussions, so it is worth a table.

Two different guarantees that people conflate
LinearizabilitySerializability
ScopeOne object, one operation at a timeMultiple objects, grouped into transactions
PromisesRecency — you see the latest valueIsolation — the result equals some serial order of the transactions
Real time?Yes. Order respects wall-clock completionNo. Any serial order is acceptable, including one that reorders transactions in time
Comes fromDistributed systems literatureDatabase transaction theory
Both togetherStrict serializability — transactions, in a single order, consistent with real time. What Spanner provides, and what most people imagine "ACID plus distributed" means.

A concrete consequence: a serializable database that is not linearizable may let transaction T2, which started after T1 committed, be ordered before T1. That is legal serializability and it will still surprise a user who made a payment and then immediately checked their balance. Isolation levels and the anomalies they permit are the subject of Chapter 12.

5.7Choosing per operation

The productive move is to stop asking what the system's consistency is and start classifying operations. Most applications have a small number of operations that need strong guarantees and a large number that do not, and treating them identically means either paying too much or being wrong somewhere important.

A worked classification for a marketplace
OperationNeedsWhyMechanism
Take paymentLinearizable + idempotentCharging twice is unrecoverable trust damageSingle-leader write, idempotency key, transaction
Decrement last item of stockLinearizable CASOverselling has a real cost per incidentConditional update on a version, or a reservation row
Show stock level on a listingBounded staleness"3 left" being 30 seconds old is fine; the checkout check is authoritativeCached with short TTL, verified at checkout
Update a seller's profileRead-your-writesThe seller must see their own edit; nobody else cares for a secondSticky primary reads after write

Auth token revocationLinearizable, or bounded to token TTLA revoked session that still works is a security incidentShort TTLs plus a strongly consistent deny-list

Notice the pattern in the "needs strong" rows: they are all cases where the system takes an irreversible action or where two concurrent actors can conflict over a scarce resource. That is a good test. If the operation is a read of something informational, or a write that only its author cares about, eventual or session consistency is almost always right.

5.8What strong consistency actually costs

  1. Latency, on every operation. A quorum write to three replicas in three availability zones adds roughly a millisecond. Across three continents it adds 100 ms or more, on every write, forever. Spanner's TrueTime adds a deliberate commit wait — the transaction pauses for the clock-uncertainty window, typically a few milliseconds, before it is visible — which is a direct, honest purchase of external consistency with latency.
  2. Availability during partitions, by definition. The minority side stops accepting writes.
  3. Throughput ceilings. All writes for a key range go through one leader, so a hot key or a hot partition cannot be scaled out by adding replicas. Recall the USL from Chapter 3: coordination is exactly the term that makes scaling curves turn over.
  4. Operational complexity. Consensus groups need odd membership, careful failover, and clock or lease management. That is real work for a team.

Convergence without coordination: CRDTs

There is a third path for some data types. A conflict-free replicated data type is structured so that concurrent updates merge deterministically, regardless of the order they arrive in — the merge function is commutative, associative and idempotent. Every replica accepts writes locally, gossips them, and all replicas converge to the same value without any coordination.

The classic CRDTs 
G-Counter      grow-only counter: each node keeps its own count,
               value = sum of all nodes' counts, merge = element-wise max
PN-Counter     two G-Counters (increments and decrements)
G-Set          grow-only set, merge = union
OR-Set         add/remove set with unique tags, so a concurrent
               add and remove resolves in favour of the add
LWW-Register   last-write-wins by timestamp — simple, and it loses data
RGA / Yjs      sequence types used for collaborative text editing

CRDTs are excellent for counters, sets, presence, shopping carts and collaborative documents. They are not a general answer: you cannot express "only if stock remains" as a CRDT, because that constraint is fundamentally a coordination problem. The rule of thumb is that CRDTs handle merging beautifully and invariants not at all.

A candidate says "we'll use a CA system so we don't have to worry about partitions." What is wrong, and what is the useful version of what they meant?

Partitions are not opt-in. Networks partition, and a system that has not decided what to do during one will do something arbitrary — most likely diverge and corrupt data. The useful version is probably: "this system runs in a single region where partitions are rare and short, so I will choose CP and accept that a partition causes a brief write outage on the minority side, rather than accepting divergence I would have to reconcile by hand." That is a real position with a stated consequence.

Your product shows "likes" on posts. Which consistency model, and what breaks if you pick strong?

Eventual, with a session guarantee for the acting user. Nobody can distinguish 12 843 likes from 12 841, so a quorum read on every page render buys nothing and costs a round trip on the hottest read path in the product. The one thing users do notice is tapping like and seeing the count not move, which is solved by rendering the change optimistically on the client plus read-your-writes on the server. Picking strong consistency here means a coordinated write on your highest-volume operation, a hot key on popular posts, and a latency cost on every page view — for a difference no user can perceive.

Why is "eventual consistency" usually a much better deal than it sounds?

Because the window is tiny and the alternatives are expensive. In a healthy single-region cluster, replication lag is typically single-digit milliseconds — shorter than the time it takes the user's browser to paint the page. The word "eventually" makes people imagine minutes; the measurement usually says 2 ms. The right question is not "can we tolerate eventual consistency" but "what is our replication lag at p99, and which specific screens break if a read is that stale" — and then fix those specific screens with session guarantees rather than paying for global coordination everywhere.

Recap

  1. CAP says: during a partition, choose consistency or availability. That is all it says. C means linearizability, and A means non-failing nodes still answer.
  2. "CA" is not available as a choice — partitions happen to you. The menu is CP or AP, per operation.
  3. PACELC is the more useful framing: partition → A or C; else → latency or consistency. The else branch is the cost you pay every day.
  4. There is a ladder, not a switch: strict serializable, linearizable, sequential, causal, bounded staleness, session guarantees, eventual.
  5. Session guarantees are the cheap win. Read-your-writes and monotonic reads fix the anomalies users actually report, using routing or a version token.
  6. Linearizability is recency on one object; serializability is isolation across many. Strict serializability is both.
  7. Classify operations, not systems. Strong consistency belongs where actions are irreversible or actors contend for something scarce.
  8. CRDTs give convergence without coordination for merge-shaped data, and nothing at all for invariant-shaped data.
